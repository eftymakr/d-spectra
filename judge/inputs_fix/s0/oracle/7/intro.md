## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit behaviors such as refusal within their activation space. These behaviors are often the result of safety alignment techniques like reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\], which aim to make models act in a helpful and harmless manner. However, identifying and controlling such behaviors remains a significant challenge. Existing methods often rely on predefined refusal templates detectable in output tokens \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07858)\], or require manual analysis \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20624)\], which limits their scalability and effectiveness in adversarial settings. 

Recent work has explored the use of activation engineering to steer model behavior. For example, ActAdd introduces a technique for modifying intermediate activations during inference to control high-level output properties like sentiment and topic \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10248)\]. Similarly, Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) computes steering vectors by contrasting residual stream activations between positive and negative examples of a behavior \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of activation-based steering but often require extensive training or model-specific tuning. 

The fragility of safety alignment has also been highlighted in several studies. Red teaming efforts have shown that even models trained with RLHF can be subverted to generate harmful content \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07858)\]. This vulnerability is further exacerbated by the fact that subversive fine-tuning techniques, such as LoRA, can significantly reduce refusal rates with minimal computational resources \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20624)\]. These findings underscore the need for more robust and generalizable methods for controlling model behavior. 

To address these challenges, we introduce COSMIC, an automated framework for direction selection that identifies viable steering directions and target layers using cosine similarity. This approach is entirely independent of model outputs, unlike existing methods that rely on predefined refusal templates or manual analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. COSMIC achieves steering performance comparable to prior methods without requiring assumptions about a model's refusal behavior, such as the presence of specific refusal tokens \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. It reliably identifies refusal directions in adversarial settings and weakly aligned models, and is capable of steering such models toward safer behavior with minimal increase in false refusals, demonstrating robustness across a wide range of alignment conditions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. 

In addition to steering, recent work has explored the use of linear subspace projection to remove or modify specific features in language models. For example, Iterative Null-space Projection (INLP) removes information from neural representations by projecting representations onto their null-space \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07667)\]. Similarly, LEACE provides a closed-form method for linear concept erasure, which provably prevents all linear classifiers from detecting a concept while changing the embedding as little as possible \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03819)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of linear subspace techniques for controlling model behavior, but they often require extensive training or model-specific tuning. 

Overall, the field of activation engineering and safety steering is rapidly evolving, with a growing body of research focused on improving the transparency, controllability, and robustness of large language models. The work described in this paper contributes to this effort by introducing a novel, automated framework for steering model behavior based on cosine similarity.
