## Related Works

Conversational recommendation systems (CRSs) have gained significant attention due to their ability to interact with users and capture nuanced preferences through multi-turn dialogues \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\]. However, effectively understanding user preferences remains a fundamental challenge, especially when preferences are multifaceted and complex \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\]. Traditional recommendation systems often rely on static user-item interactions and struggle to adapt to dynamic preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\]. In contrast, CRSs enable direct interaction with users, allowing for more accurate preference modeling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\]. Nevertheless, frequent user involvement can degrade the user experience, necessitating automated solutions for interaction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\].

To address this, several approaches have been proposed. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12979)\] introduces a conversational recommendation framework that unifies attributes and items in the same arm space, enabling seamless exploration-exploitation (EE) trade-offs via Thompson Sampling. This method improves recommendation effectiveness by considering both attributes and items simultaneously, rather than separately \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12979)\]. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11650)\] proposes a framework that addresses the long-tail problem in CRSs by simulating and utilizing balanced datasets, ensuring diverse recommendations even for rarely mentioned items \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11650)\]. This is particularly important as existing datasets often suffer from long-tail issues, leading to over-recommendation of frequent items \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11650)\].

Another key challenge in CRSs is the need for efficient and effective interaction strategies. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\] introduces a method for training verifiers to solve math word problems, demonstrating that verification significantly improves performance on complex reasoning tasks. This approach is relevant to CRSs, where accurate feedback is crucial for refining recommendations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\]. Building on this, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12832)\] proposes a hybrid approach that combines reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF), leading to more accurate synthetic preference labels. This is particularly useful for CRSs, where generating high-quality feedback is essential for improving recommendation accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12832)\].

Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240)\] introduces generative verifiers that leverage next-token prediction to enhance reasoning performance. These verifiers integrate seamlessly with instruction tuning and enable chain-of-thought reasoning, which is beneficial for CRSs requiring complex decision-making \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240)\]. Furthermore, \[[7]()\] explores the use of large language models as zero-shot conversational recommenders, demonstrating their potential to provide personalized recommendations without explicit training on conversational data \[[7]()\].

In summary, existing works have made significant contributions to CRSs, addressing challenges such as preference modeling, feedback generation, and interaction efficiency. However, the proposed approach introduces a novel generative reward model based simulated user (GRSU) that enables automatic interaction with CRSs, offering a more efficient and effective solution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\].
