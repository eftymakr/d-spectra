## Related Works

Fairness in recommendation systems has gained significant attention due to its implications on both users and item producers. Prior work has identified a trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness, with fairness considerations often leading to a decline in user-side accuracy \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]. This trade-off is analogous to the dynamics of economic transactions, where item-side fairness constraints can be likened to a commodity tax on suppliers, ultimately translating into a cost passed on to consumers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13619)\]. Similarly, the accuracy-fairness trade-off in re-ranking is governed by the elasticity of utility between item groups, a concept borrowed from economics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13619)\]. This insight highlights the limitations of current fair re-ranking evaluations, which often rely solely on a single fairness metric, thereby hindering a comprehensive assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13619)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to address fairness in recommendation systems. FairRec \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\] investigates fairness in two-sided platforms, ensuring that both customers and producers receive fair exposure. The algorithm guarantees Maximin Share (MMS) of exposure for most producers and Envy-Free up to One item (EF1) fairness for every customer. While effective, FairRec incurs a marginal loss in overall recommendation quality \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\]. Similarly, CPFair \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\] introduces an optimization-based re-ranking approach that integrates fairness constraints from both consumer and producer sides, demonstrating that fairness can be improved without reducing overall recommendation quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].

The concept of fairness in ranking has also been explored through the lens of fair division and economic principles. FairRank \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07247)\] develops a new axiomatic approach rooted in fair division principles, avoiding the need to choose a link function between item exposure and relevance. This approach ensures envy-freeness and dominance over uniform rankings, with guarantees of Pareto optimality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07247)\]. In contrast, conventional exposure-based fairness can lead to large amounts of envy and disparate impact on items \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07247)\].

Efforts to improve fairness in ranking have also focused on re-ranking strategies. Tax-rank \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\] introduces a new fair re-ranking method that leverages the concept of taxation, reformulating fair re-ranking as an item-level tax policy. This approach ensures continuity and controllability over accuracy loss, outperforming existing baselines in effectiveness and efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. VerFair \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03046)\] further improves upon existing methods by proposing a novel algorithm that balances exposure fairness and ranking performance, demonstrating superior trade-offs in both individual and group levels \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03046)\].

Fairness in recommendation systems has also been addressed through the use of generalized Gini indices \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06521)\]. This approach weights individuals based on their ranks, giving more weight to worse-off individuals to promote equality. By leveraging non-smooth optimization techniques, this method minimizes the Gini index of item exposure, achieving better trade-offs than baselines on various fairness criteria \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06521)\].

In summary, while existing works have made significant strides in addressing fairness in recommendation systems, there remains a need for more comprehensive evaluation frameworks that account for the elasticity of utility between item groups. This motivates the introduction of the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) and ElasticRank as novel contributions to the field \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13619)\].

However, the underlying mechanisms are still not fully understood. An analogy can be drawn between re-ranking and the dynamics of economic transactions. The accuracy-fairness trade-off parallels the coupling of the commodity tax transfer process. Fairness considerations in re-ranking, similar to a commodity tax on suppliers, ultimately translate into a cost passed on to consumers. Analogously, item-side fairness constraints result in a decline in user-side accuracy. In economics, the extent to which commodity tax on the supplier (item fairness) transfers to commodity tax on users (accuracy loss) is formalized using the notion of elasticity. The re-ranking fairness-accuracy trade-off is similarly governed by the elasticity of utility between item groups. This insight underscores the limitations of current fair re-ranking evaluations, which often rely solely on a single fairness metric, hindering comprehensive assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms.
