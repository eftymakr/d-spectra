## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area to address the "right to be forgotten" in federated learning (FL) systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. This concept allows users to remove their data contributions from the global model while preserving the model's utility \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Early approaches to federated unlearning primarily relied on retraining the model from scratch, which is computationally expensive \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. To improve efficiency, several methods have been proposed that leverage historical parameter updates or local unlearning techniques \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\]. For instance, FedEraser eliminates the influence of a client’s data by reconstructing the unlearned model using retained historical updates, achieving a speed-up of up to 4× compared to full retraining \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. 

Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of unlearning in FL, particularly in the context of recommendation systems \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. The FRU method, inspired by transaction rollback mechanisms, enables efficient unlearning by rolling back and recalibrating historical parameter updates \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\]. This approach is especially relevant in scenarios where users request the removal of their contributions from federated training processes \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f2f8f7a2ec1b2ede48cbcd189b376ab9fa0735ef)\]. Furthermore, the development of unlearning methods that can handle both class and sample-level unlearning has been a key focus \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\]. For example, the SISA training framework reduces the computational overhead of unlearning by strategically limiting the influence of specific data points during training \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\].

The evaluation of unlearning methods remains a challenge, as existing approaches often rely on a single metric, which may not fully capture the effectiveness of the unlearning process \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed multiple evaluation metrics, including reverse stochastic gradient ascent (SGA) and elastic weight consolidation (EWC), to assess unlearning efficacy and efficiency \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\]. These metrics help quantify the impact of unlearning on model performance and ensure that the unlearning process does not introduce over-unlearning or under-unlearning issues \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. Additionally, the need for verifiable federated unlearning has gained attention, as unlearning itself may not be sufficient to implement the right to be forgotten unless the unlearning effect can be independently verified \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. Verifiable federated unlearning frameworks, such as VeriFi, have been introduced to enable systematic analysis and quantification of unlearning effects \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08096)\].

In the context of federated online learning to rank (FOLTR), the challenge of unlearning client interactions and data has been highlighted as a critical issue \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\]. Current FOLTR methods face limitations in ranking effectiveness, robustness, and unlearning capabilities, which are essential for maintaining privacy and performance in decentralized settings \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06edb1f6eadb6c141d720fcca4c854578dc5d670)\]. This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a comprehensive evaluation of unlearning strategies, adapting and introducing new metrics to assess both under-unlearning and over-unlearning scenarios \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]. The results of this study provide valuable insights into optimizing federated unlearning within FOLTR, balancing data privacy and system performance \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.03575)\].

### <a id='category-federated-learning-and-privacy-frameworks'></a>Federated Learning and Privacy Frameworks: 8 papers found.
Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a critical framework for collaborative training of machine learning models while preserving user privacy, particularly in scenarios where data is distributed across multiple parties \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. This approach enables the training of models without sharing raw data, thus addressing privacy concerns associated with centralized data storage \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. However, FL faces challenges when client data distributions differ significantly, leading to suboptimal model performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. To mitigate this, clustered FL (CFL) was introduced as a novel federated multitask learning (FMTL) framework that leverages geometric properties of the loss surface to group clients into clusters with similar data distributions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. Unlike existing FMTL methods, CFL does not require changes to the FL communication protocol and can handle nonconvex objectives, such as deep neural networks, without prior knowledge of the number of clusters \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. Furthermore, it ensures strong mathematical guarantees on clustering quality and can adapt to dynamic client populations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. In parallel, federated transfer learning (FTL) has been proposed to improve statistical models by enabling knowledge sharing across organizations without compromising privacy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. This framework allows a target-domain party to leverage rich labels from a source-domain party, enhancing model flexibility and accuracy while maintaining privacy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. Additionally, federated learning systems have been developed to support the implementation of various privacy-preserving techniques, such as homomorphic encryption and secret sharing, to address feature heterogeneity and covariate shift \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in FL for privacy-preserving collaborative learning, especially in domains like healthcare and search result ranking \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\].

This requirement has spurred research into federated unlearning, which aims to remove data from models without retraining from scratch, thereby reducing computational overhead \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Various approaches have been proposed, including methods like linear filtration for efficient data sanitization \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\], and techniques such as reverse stochastic gradient ascent (SGA) and elastic weight consolidation (EWC) to enable more effective unlearning \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\]. Additionally, verifying the success of unlearning remains a challenge, with recent work introducing backdoor-based verification mechanisms to ensure data deletion is effectively achieved \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. These developments highlight the growing need for robust, efficient, and verifiable unlearning strategies that align with evolving data privacy regulations.

Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) has emerged as a promising approach to address privacy concerns in ranking systems by enabling collaborative model training across decentralized data sources without sharing raw user data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Unlike traditional Learning to Rank (LTR) methods that rely on static, manually annotated datasets, FOLTR continuously updates ranking models using implicit user feedback such as clicks, making it more adaptive to evolving user preferences and query intents \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. However, FOLTR faces challenges in terms of ranking effectiveness, robustness across varying data distributions, vulnerability to attacks, and the ability to unlearn specific client interactions or data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. These limitations highlight the need for further research into improving the performance and security of FOLTR systems, particularly in the context of emerging privacy regulations such as the "right to be forgotten," which requires machine learning models to support user data removal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Recent efforts have focused on developing unlearning strategies within FOLTR to address these challenges, with a growing emphasis on rigorous evaluation practices that consider multiple metrics to assess the success of unlearning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\].
