## Related Works

Prosody modeling has been a central challenge in text-to-speech (TTS) systems, as it directly impacts the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech. Early TTS systems often relied on handcrafted rules or statistical models to generate prosody, but these approaches lacked the flexibility and adaptability needed for modern applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Recent advances in neural TTS have enabled more natural speech synthesis, but the control over prosody remains limited, especially in post-hoc scenarios \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. For instance, many TTS systems implicitly encode prosody within acoustic features, making it difficult to alter without retraining \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. This limitation is particularly evident in systems that lack explicit prosody labeling in their training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. 

Several works have attempted to improve prosody control by introducing explicit prosody modeling. For example, Hierarchical Prosody Modeling for Non-Autoregressive Speech Synthesis proposes a hierarchical architecture where phoneme-level prosody features are conditioned on word-level features, leading to improved audio quality and prosody naturalness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Similarly, the work on Deep Voice 3 demonstrates how fully-convolutional attention-based models can scale to large datasets, improving both training efficiency and audio quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07654)\]. However, these models still face challenges in fine-grained prosody control, especially when dealing with multi-sentence inputs \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\].

To address these limitations, researchers have explored the use of pre-trained language models to enhance TTS systems with richer semantic and syntactic information. For example, Pre-Trained Text Embeddings for Enhanced Text-to-Speech Synthesis introduces BERT embeddings as an additional input to Tacotron2, improving prosody and pronunciation \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\]. This approach is further extended in Improving Prosody Modelling with Cross-Utterance BERT Embeddings for End-to-End Speech Synthesis, where cross-utterance context vectors are used to improve prosody generation for multi-sentence inputs \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05161)\]. These methods demonstrate that incorporating linguistic information can lead to more natural and expressive speech, even in the absence of explicit prosody annotations.

Another line of research focuses on non-autoregressive TTS models, which offer faster inference but often struggle with prosody modeling. Non-Attentive Tacotron replaces the attention mechanism with an explicit duration predictor, improving robustness and enabling fine-grained control over duration \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. Similarly, Parallel Tacotron introduces a variational autoencoder-based residual encoder, allowing for parallelizable training and inference while improving naturalness \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. Despite these advancements, post-hoc adjustments to prosody and pronunciation remain challenging, as most methods require retraining or specialized modules \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\].

To overcome these limitations, recent works have explored model-agnostic approaches that enable post-hoc manipulation of internal representations. For example, the work on Prosospeech introduces a word-level prosody encoder and latent prosody vector predictor, allowing for the generation of expressive speech conditioned on predicted latent vectors \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. These methods highlight the potential of latent space manipulation for improving prosody control without requiring retraining.

### <a id='category-prosody-modeling-in-tts-systems'></a>Prosody Modeling in TTS Systems: 18 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Many existing approaches rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. For instance, non-autoregressive TTS models often struggle with predicting natural prosody at inference time, necessitating hierarchical architectures that condition phoneme-level features on word-level prosody \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Similarly, traditional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are impractical in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have explored unsupervised methods for prosody control, such as fully automatic prosody embedding networks that enable sentence-wise pace and expressiveness adjustments \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. These methods often incorporate novel attention mechanisms to improve pace control sensitivity and attention convergence \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Additionally, some studies focus on improving prosody generation by leveraging contextual information from neighboring sentences, as seen in cross-utterance (CU) context vectors that enhance the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08947)\]. Others propose latent prosody spaces conditioned on acoustic features, allowing for intuitive control over dimensions like pitch, duration, and energy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. Furthermore, models like FastSpeech and its variants have been developed to enable faster inference while maintaining high-quality speech synthesis, often by incorporating more variation information such as pitch and energy during training \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance TTS systems with more flexible and controllable prosody modeling, aiming to bridge the gap between pre-trained models and editable speech synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\].

Explicit prosody modeling has been a key focus in recent text-to-speech (TTS) research, with approaches aiming to improve robustness and control over duration and intonation. One notable method is Non-Attentive Tacotron, which replaces the attention mechanism in Tacotron 2 with an explicit duration predictor, enabling more precise control over speech duration at inference time \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. This approach enhances robustness, as measured by metrics such as unaligned duration ratio and word deletion rate, and achieves a 5-scale mean opinion score for naturalness of 4.41, slightly outperforming Tacotron 2 \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. The duration predictor also allows for both utterance-wide and per-phoneme adjustments, offering flexibility in prosody manipulation. Furthermore, the method supports semi-supervised or unsupervised training of the duration predictor when accurate target durations are scarce, achieving results comparable to supervised training \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in explicit prosody modeling, particularly in scenarios where post-hoc adjustments and robustness are critical.

Non-autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) models have gained significant attention due to their ability to improve synthesis efficiency while maintaining naturalness \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. Unlike autoregressive models, which generate speech sequentially and are computationally intensive, non-autoregressive approaches enable parallel processing, making them suitable for real-time applications \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. One notable example is the Parallel Tacotron model, which integrates a variational autoencoder-based residual encoder to enhance the model's capacity to capture complex speech patterns \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. This design helps relax the one-to-many mapping challenge inherent in TTS tasks, contributing to more natural speech synthesis \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. Additionally, the use of lightweight convolutions and an iterative spectrogram loss further refines the output, ensuring high-quality results \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. These advancements highlight the potential of non-autoregressive models to match or even surpass autoregressive baselines in terms of both efficiency and subjective quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\].
