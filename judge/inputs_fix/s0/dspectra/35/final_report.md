## Related Works

Protein dynamics and conformational ensembles play a critical role in biological function, necessitating advanced computational methods to study and generate realistic protein structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Traditional approaches rely on molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which are computationally expensive and limited in throughput \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling, particularly denoising diffusion models, have enabled efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven approaches remains challenging, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\].

Several studies have explored energy-based models (EBMs) for protein conformation generation. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\] proposes an EBM that operates at atomic scale and is trained solely on crystallized protein data, achieving performance comparable to the Rosetta energy function. This model captures physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy and is evaluated on the rotamer recovery task \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\] introduces techniques to improve contrastive divergence training of EBMs, highlighting the importance of a neglected gradient term and demonstrating improved performance on benchmarks such as image generation and OOD detection \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\]. These efforts underscore the potential of EBMs in capturing physical properties of proteins.

Diffusion models have also been extensively applied to protein structure prediction. \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\] presents a diffusion-based generative model that generates protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state towards a stable folded structure. This approach mirrors the natural folding process and leverages the inherent shift and rotational invariance of the representation to avoid complex equivariant networks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\] proposes a latent diffusion model that reduces the complexity of protein modeling while flexibly capturing the distribution of natural protein structures in a condensed latent space. This model uses an equivariant protein autoencoder to embed proteins into a latent space and then applies an equivariant diffusion model to learn the distribution of the latent representations \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\].

Moreover, \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\] introduces EvoDiff, a diffusion framework that combines evolutionary-scale data with the conditioning capabilities of diffusion models for controllable protein generation in sequence space. EvoDiff generates high-fidelity, diverse, and structurally-plausible proteins that cover natural sequence and functional space, demonstrating the potential of sequence-based approaches in protein design \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\]. These models highlight the growing trend of using deep learning to generate protein structures with high accuracy and diversity.

In addition, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17196)\] develops P2DFlow, a generative model based on SE(3) flow matching, to predict the structural ensembles of proteins. This model incorporates a valuable prior for the flow process and enhances the ability to distinguish intermediate states by adding an additional dimension to describe the ensemble data, reflecting physical laws governing conformational distributions \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17196)\]. These models further demonstrate the potential of generative modeling in capturing the conformational landscapes of proteins.

In summary, while traditional MD simulations remain the gold standard for studying protein dynamics, recent advances in generative modeling, particularly denoising diffusion models and energy-based models, offer promising alternatives. These models integrate physical supervision and capture conformational ensembles with high accuracy and efficiency, paving the way for new applications in structural biology and drug discovery.

### <a id='category-generative-modeling-in-protein-structure-prediction'></a>Generative Modeling in Protein Structure Prediction: 3 papers found.
Recent advances in generative modeling have significantly impacted protein structure prediction and conformational sampling, offering efficient and accurate methods to explore the complex conformational landscapes of proteins \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Traditional approaches, such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, are computationally expensive and often limited in their ability to capture the full range of conformational flexibility \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have developed flow-based generative models like AlphaFlow and ESMFlow, which leverage highly accurate single-state predictors such as AlphaFold and ESMFold under a custom flow matching framework \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These models are trained on the PDB and can generate diverse and precise protein structures, outperforming AlphaFold with MSA subsampling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Additionally, methods like BioEmu have been introduced to generate thousands of statistically independent samples from protein structure ensembles per hour, using deep learning and extensive data from MD simulations and experimental protein stabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. This enables the exploration of functionally relevant conformational changes, such as the formation of cryptic pockets and large-scale domain rearrangements \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Furthermore, machine learning models like idpGAN have been trained on simulation data to generate physically realistic conformational ensembles without the need for explicit sampling, demonstrating the potential of these approaches to reduce computational costs while maintaining accuracy \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of integrating physical supervision into data-driven models to enhance the physical plausibility of generated structures, aligning with the goals of the proposed Energy-based Alignment (EBA) method \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\].

### <a id='category-energy-based-models-for-protein-conformation'></a>Energy-Based Models for Protein Conformation: 3 papers found.
Energy-based models (EBMs) have emerged as a powerful framework for modeling protein conformations, offering a flexible approach to capturing the complex energy landscapes of biomolecules \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Unlike traditional energy functions that rely on physical principles and extensive tuning, EBMs learn from data, enabling them to capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy without explicit parameterization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. This approach is particularly advantageous in scenarios where the energy function is difficult to define due to the complexity of molecular interactions. The Rosetta energy function, a state-of-the-art method, has been instrumental in protein structure prediction and design, and recent work has shown that EBMs can achieve comparable performance to Rosetta on tasks such as rotamer recovery \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Training EBMs remains challenging due to the unknown normalizing constant, but modern approaches such as Score Matching and Noise Contrastive Estimation have made significant strides in addressing this issue \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. These methods allow for more efficient and scalable training, enabling EBMs to be applied to a wide range of biomolecular modeling tasks. The Rosetta energy function, which has been continuously refined to include features relevant to diverse biomolecules, serves as a benchmark for evaluating the performance of EBMs \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. As research in this area progresses, the integration of physical supervision into data-driven models continues to be a key focus, with methods like Energy-based Alignment (EBA) showing promise in aligning generative models with physical models to improve the accuracy of conformational sampling \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\].

### <a id='category-applications-in-structural-biology-and-drug-discovery'></a>Applications in Structural Biology and Drug Discovery: 4 papers found.
In the context of structural biology and drug discovery, recent advancements have focused on improving the accuracy and efficiency of protein structure prediction and conformational analysis. The concept of molecular recognition has evolved from the traditional "induced fit" hypothesis to include the "conformational selection" mechanism, which suggests that proteins exist in multiple conformations and ligands select the most favorable one \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. This shift has implications for understanding processes such as signaling and gene regulation, and it informs strategies in drug design by emphasizing the importance of pre-existing conformational states \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Additionally, new algorithms like TM-align have been developed to enhance the accuracy of structural alignments between proteins, offering faster and more reliable comparisons than previous methods \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. These tools are essential for analyzing large-scale structural data, such as those from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), and for identifying structural analogs in predicted models \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. Furthermore, energy-based models (EBMs) have been refined to address challenges in generative modeling, with approaches like GraphEBM ensuring permutation invariance in molecular graph generation, thus improving the reliability of generated structures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. These developments collectively contribute to more robust and physiologically relevant models, supporting both fundamental research and applied fields such as drug discovery.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-for-protein-folding-and-structure-generation'></a>Diffusion Models for Protein Folding and Structure Generation: 7 papers found.
Recent advances in diffusion models have significantly enhanced the ability to predict and generate protein structures, addressing the limitations of traditional molecular dynamics simulations \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. These models, such as EigenFold \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\], ConfDiff \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\], and Genie \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\], leverage diffusion processes to sample conformational ensembles and capture the flexibility essential for biological function. EigenFold introduces a diffusion framework that models proteins as harmonic oscillators, enabling cascading-resolution generative processes and achieving high accuracy in structure prediction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. ConfDiff, on the other hand, integrates physical prior knowledge through a force-guided network, improving the fidelity of generated conformations by aligning with equilibrium distributions \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. Genie utilizes denoising diffusion probabilistic models combined with equivariant neural networks to generate diverse and designable protein backbones, demonstrating superior performance in structure prediction \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Similarly, Chroma employs a diffusion process that respects polymer conformational statistics, allowing for the direct sampling of novel protein structures and sequences while conditioning on desired properties \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. The work in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\] presents a diffusion-based model inspired by natural folding, generating realistic protein structures by denoising from an unfolded state, which mirrors the native folding process. Additionally, the latent diffusion model proposed in \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\] reduces the complexity of protein modeling by embedding structures into a latent space, enabling efficient generation of novel protein backbones. These approaches collectively highlight the growing trend of using diffusion models to enhance the accuracy, diversity, and physical plausibility of protein structure prediction and design.

Recent advances in sequence-based protein generation techniques have significantly enhanced the capabilities of deep learning models in protein design and structure prediction. ProteinMPNN, for instance, demonstrates superior sequence recovery on native protein backbones compared to traditional physically based approaches like Rosetta, achieving a 52.4% recovery rate \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Similarly, ProGen2, with its 6.4B parameters, excels in capturing evolutionary sequence distributions and generating viable sequences without additional fine-tuning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. EvoDiff further expands the scope by integrating evolutionary data with diffusion models, enabling controllable protein generation in sequence space and producing high-fidelity, structurally plausible proteins \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. These models also address limitations of structure-based approaches by generating proteins with disordered regions and functional motifs, as demonstrated through experimental validation \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. Additionally, ProGen, trained on over 280 million sequences, provides fine-grained control over generated proteins through taxonomic and functional tags, enhancing secondary structure accuracy and conformational energy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. These developments underscore the growing importance of data distribution and model scalability in advancing protein engineering \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Overall, these techniques represent a shift toward sequence-first design, offering broader applicability and improved performance in both in silico and experimental settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]\[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\].

INTRODUCTION Statistical mechanics aims to compute the average behavior of physical systems on the basis of their microscopic constituents. For example, what is the probability that a protein will be folded at a given temperature? If we could answer such questions efficiently, then we could not only comprehend the workings of molecules and materials, but we could also design drug molecules and materials with new properties in a principled way. To this end, we need to compute statistics of the equilibrium states of many-body systems. In the protein-folding example, this means to consider each of the astronomically many ways to place all protein atoms in space, to compute the probability of each such “configuration” in the equilibrium ensemble, and then to compare the total probability of unfolded and folded configurations. As enumeration of all configurations is infeasible, one instead must attempt to sample them from their equilibrium distribution. However, we currently have no way to generate equilibrium samples of many-body systems in “one shot.” The main approach is thus to start with one configuration, e.g., the folded protein state, and make tiny changes to it over time, e.g., by using Markov-chain Monte Carlo or molecular dynamics (MD). However, these simulations get trapped in metastable (long-lived) states: For example, sampling a single folding or unfolding event with atomistic MD may take a year on a supercomputer. RATIONALE Here, we combine deep machine learning and statistical mechanics to develop Boltzmann generators. Boltzmann generators are trained on the energy function of a many-body system and learn to provide unbiased, one-shot samples from its equilibrium state. This is achieved by training an invertible neural network to learn a coordinate transformation from a system’s configurations to a so-called latent space representation, in which the low-energy configurations of different states are close to each other and can be easily sampled. Because of the invertibility, every latent space sample can be back-transformed to a system configuration with high Boltzmann probability (Fig. 1). We then employ statistical mechanics, which offers a rich set of tools for reweighting the distribution generated by the neural network to the Boltzmann distribution. RESULTS Boltzmann generators can be trained to directly generate independent samples of low-energy structures of condensed-matter systems and protein molecules. When initialized with a few structures from different metastable states, Boltzmann generators can generate statistically independent samples from these states and efficiently compute the free-energy differences between them. This capability could be used to compute relative stabilities between different experimental structures of protein or other organic molecules, which is currently a very challenging problem. Boltzmann generators can also learn a notion of “reaction coordinates”: Simple linear interpolations between points in latent space have a high probability of corresponding to physically realistic, low-energy transition pathways. Finally, by using established sampling methods such as Metropolis Monte Carlo in the latent space variables, Boltzmann generators can discover new states and gradually explore state space. CONCLUSION Boltzmann generators can overcome rare event–sampling problems in many-body systems by learning to generate unbiased equilibrium samples from different metastable states in one shot. They differ conceptually from established enhanced sampling methods, as no reaction coordinates are needed to drive them between metastable states. However, by applying existing sampling methods in the latent spaces learned by Boltzmann generators, a plethora of new opportunities opens up to design efficient sampling methods for many-body systems. Boltzmann generators overcome sampling problems between long-lived states. The Boltzmann generator works as follows: 1. We sample from a simple (e.g., Gaussian) distribution. 2. An invertible deep neural network is trained to transform this simple distribution to a distribution pX(x) that is similar to the desired Boltzmann distribution of the system of interest. 3. The integration of physical supervision in generative models has been a focal point in recent advancements, particularly in the context of molecular simulations and protein structure prediction. Traditional methods, such as molecular dynamics (MD), are computationally intensive and often struggle with sampling rare events or metastable states \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have explored the use of deep learning techniques, such as Boltzmann generators, which combine neural networks with statistical mechanics to sample equilibrium states efficiently \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. These models learn invertible transformations from complex system configurations to simpler latent spaces, enabling the generation of unbiased equilibrium samples in one shot \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. This approach leverages the energy function of the system to ensure that the generated samples adhere to the Boltzmann distribution, thereby maintaining physical plausibility \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. Furthermore, the integration of physical supervision allows for the calibration of generative models using feedback from physical models, ensuring that conformational states are balanced based on their energy differences \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. This is particularly relevant in the context of protein dynamics, where accurate sampling of conformational ensembles is crucial for understanding biological functions and properties \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. The development of methods like Energy-based Alignment (EBA) further demonstrates the potential of combining data-driven generative models with physical principles to achieve state-of-the-art results in generating high-quality protein ensembles \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\].
