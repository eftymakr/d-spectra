## Related Works

The detection and segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions, particularly paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), have been extensively studied in recent years. MS is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by the formation of lesions in white matter brain tissue, and quantitative susceptibility maps (QSMs) from magnetic resonance images (MRIs) have emerged as a valuable tool for distinguishing between "rim" and "non-rim" lesion types \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. PRLs, surrounded by paramagnetic iron deposits, are an emerging biomarker in MS and are associated with more aggressive disease progression and increased disability \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/422983aa9f20d9e5eb7d17f618f8c4cf785971f8)\]. The challenge of detecting PRLs lies in their rarity and the class imbalance between rim and non-rim lesions, which complicates the training of effective classifiers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\].

Several studies have explored automated methods for MS lesion segmentation. Early approaches relied on manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-observer variability \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Automated methods have since been proposed, including statistical imaging analysis techniques and deep learning models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. For example, a fully convolutional neural network (CNN) was used to segment white matter lesions from multi-contrast MR images, showing significant improvements over existing methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\]. Similarly, a multi-branch CNN was developed for MS lesion segmentation, achieving high performance on both public and private datasets \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\].

The detection of PRLs has also been addressed through machine learning techniques. A study proposed a supervised framework using intensity subtraction and deformation field features to detect new T2-weighted lesions in MS, achieving a mean Dice similarity coefficient of 0.77 \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a6c3b459dcaed58347ae0d270c0cfd93ee8aed6f)\]. Another study introduced a random forest classification model to identify PRLs based on radiomic features extracted from T2*-phase images, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80 \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. These methods, however, face challenges due to the heterogeneity of PRLs and the difficulty in obtaining sufficient labeled data \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\].

To address the class imbalance issue, synthetic data generation has been proposed. A recent study introduced a generative adversarial network (GAN) to create synthetic QSMs of PRLs, which were then used to improve classifier performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. This approach allows for the augmentation of minority class data, enabling more robust training on rare PRL cases. The synthetic data generation was extended to include multi-channel contrasts and probabilistic segmentation maps, enhancing the clinical interpretability of the results \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. Additionally, a denoising approach was proposed to improve the detection of ambiguous rim cases by leveraging the projection capability of the trained generative network \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\].

In summary, while numerous methods have been developed for MS lesion segmentation, the detection of PRLs remains challenging due to their rarity and variability. Recent advances in deep learning and synthetic data generation offer promising solutions to these challenges \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]\[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]\[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a6c3b459dcaed58347ae0d270c0cfd93ee8aed6f)\].

This feature distinguishes them from non-rim lesions and has been linked to greater clinical disability and neurodegeneration \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. The ability of QSM to quantify susceptibility changes related to iron deposition allows for the non-invasive detection of these lesions, which are challenging to identify using conventional MRI techniques \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. Furthermore, QSM has been shown to provide more consistent and orientation-independent contrast compared to phase images, enhancing the accuracy of lesion detection and characterization \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Studies have demonstrated that rim+ lesions are associated with increased cognitive impairment, cortical thinning, and reduced thalamic volume, highlighting their significance in disease progression \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. The integration of QSM with other modalities, such as PET imaging, has further validated the presence of active inflammation in rim+ lesions, offering a more comprehensive understanding of their role in MS \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. These findings underscore the potential of QSM as a biomarker for monitoring disease activity and guiding therapeutic interventions in MS.
