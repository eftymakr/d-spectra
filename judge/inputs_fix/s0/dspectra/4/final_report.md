## Related Works

In the field of in-context learning (ICL), the reliance on high-quality demonstrations from large annotated corpora has been well established \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. However, the presence of noisy annotations poses a significant challenge, as existing methods often assume that noisy samples yield higher perplexity than their clean counterparts \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07461)\]. This assumption, while useful in low-noise scenarios, fails when the noise ratio is high and many demonstrations are flawed \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.06974)\]. 

Several approaches have been proposed to address label noise in training deep neural networks. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\] introduces a framework that models the relationship between noisy and clean labels using an undirected graphical model, enabling semi-supervised learning from noisy datasets. This method has shown effectiveness in reducing label noise in image labeling tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\] explores the use of instance-dependent label noise, where the noise rate is not uniform across instances, and proposes a second-order approach to estimate covariance terms between noise rates and Bayes optimal labels. This method allows for the transformation of instance-dependent noise into class-dependent noise, enabling the use of existing solutions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

Another line of work focuses on learning with noisy labels through semi-supervised techniques. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12275)\] introduces DivideMix, a framework that dynamically divides training data into labeled and unlabeled sets, and trains the model in a semi-supervised manner. This approach helps avoid confirmation bias by training two diverged networks simultaneously \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02391)\]. Additionally, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\] presents a unified distillation framework that leverages side information, such as a small clean dataset and label relations in a knowledge graph, to hedge the risk of learning from noisy labels \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04019)\].

In the context of scene graph generation, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06125)\] addresses the issue of noisy labels by proposing NICEST, a strategy that detects and corrects noisy samples through a combination of negative and positive sample detection and correction techniques. This method generates high-quality samples and employs a multi-teacher knowledge distillation strategy to improve model performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03683)\].

Moreover, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07131)\] highlights the challenges of text classification with noisy, historical data, emphasizing the discrepancy between training and prediction data. This work demonstrates the utility of dirty training datasets for building prediction models that generalize to cleaner inputs \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\].

In summary, while many approaches have been proposed to handle noisy annotations, the assumption that noisy samples yield higher perplexity is not universally valid, especially in high-noise scenarios. The introduction of a dual debiasing framework that uses synthesized neighbors to correct perplexity estimates represents a significant advancement in this area \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01326)\].

In the context of text classification with noisy data, several studies highlight the challenges and approaches to handling imperfect datasets. Industry datasets are often by-products of historical data, which can contain significant noise in both text and labels, making it difficult to assess model performance on future, cleaner inputs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.06974)\]. Transfer learning has been widely adopted in NLP, where models are pre-trained on large corpora and fine-tuned for specific tasks, yet this approach can inherit biases from the training data, such as gender stereotypes in word embeddings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\]. These biases can be amplified in downstream applications, necessitating methods to mitigate them while preserving useful properties of the embeddings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\]. Additionally, the effectiveness of transfer learning in NLP is influenced by factors such as pre-training objectives, architectures, and unlabeled datasets, with recent work focusing on creating unified frameworks that convert diverse language problems into text-to-text formats \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. The General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE) benchmark further underscores the need for models that can generalize across domains and tasks, especially when training data is limited or mismatched with test sets \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07461)\]. In parallel, research on natural language generation (NLG) has revealed biases in generated text based on demographic groups, emphasizing the importance of metrics like regard and sentiment to evaluate and mitigate such biases \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. These findings collectively highlight the complexities of working with noisy, real-world data in text classification and the ongoing efforts to improve robustness and fairness in machine learning models.

### <a id='category-label-noise-in-deep-learning'></a>Label Noise in Deep Learning: 6 papers found.
The category "Label Noise in Deep Learning" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the robustness of deep learning models in the presence of noisy or unreliable labels. Existing work highlights that human annotations, such as image tags or captions, often exhibit "human reporting bias," where subjective judgments lead to inconsistent or incomplete labeling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. This issue is further compounded by the fact that noisy annotations can be structured in ways that can be modeled and mitigated \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed frameworks that either explicitly model the noise distribution or leverage semi-supervised learning to separate clean from noisy samples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.06974)\]. For example, some methods use graphical models to represent the relationship between noisy and clean labels, enabling tractable inference during training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07461)\]. Others, like DivideMix, dynamically divide training data into clean and noisy subsets and employ strategies such as label co-refinement and co-guessing to improve performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.06974)\]. Additionally, methods have been developed to estimate the probability of class corruption, allowing for loss correction without requiring extensive manual annotation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\]. These approaches demonstrate that label noise can be effectively managed through both theoretical insights and practical algorithms, leading to improved performance on datasets such as CIFAR-10, MS COCO, and others \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\]. The challenge of handling noisy labels remains a critical area of research, especially as deep learning models increasingly rely on large-scale, crowd-sourced datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\].
