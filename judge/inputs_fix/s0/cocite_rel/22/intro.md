## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has attracted significant research interest due to its practical importance in real-world applications where labeled data in the target domain is scarce or unavailable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Traditional UDA methods typically assume that the category settings between the source and target domains are known, which is often not the case in real-world scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes (classes present in the target domain but not in the source) exist \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. To address this limitation, several works have explored methods that can handle unknown category settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].

One line of research focuses on improving the alignment between source and target domains by leveraging adversarial learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. For example, ADVENT \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] introduces an adversarial entropy minimization approach to reduce domain shift, achieving state-of-the-art results on synthetic-to-real benchmarks. Similarly, CyCADA \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\] proposes a cycle-consistent adversarial domain adaptation model that enforces cycle-consistency while leveraging a task loss, enabling domain adaptation without requiring aligned image pairs. These methods, however, still assume that the category sets are known \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].

Another approach is to utilize self-training with pseudo-labels to improve the performance of UDA models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. For instance, Prototypical Pseudo Label Denoising \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\] introduces a method that uses representative prototypes to address noisy pseudo-labels and improve the alignment of target features. Similarly, BUDA \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\] defines a new problem called "Boundless Unsupervised Domain Adaptation," where the target domain may contain classes not present in the source, and proposes a framework that combines domain adaptation and zero-shot learning to handle such scenarios. These methods demonstrate the potential of self-training and zero-shot learning in UDA settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].

Recent works have also explored the use of depth estimation as a supervisory signal to bridge the domain gap \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]. For example, Domain Adaptive Semantic Segmentation with Self-Supervised Depth Estimation \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\] leverages self-supervised depth estimation to refine pseudo-labels and improve the adaptation process. This approach has shown promising results on benchmark datasets like SYNTHIA-to-Cityscapes and GTA-to-Cityscapes \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\].

Additionally, methods that incorporate curriculum learning and dual-level domain mixing have been proposed to improve the adaptation process \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13613)\]. For example, Semi-supervised Domain Adaptation based on Dual-level Domain Mixing \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10349)\] introduces a framework that reduces the domain gap at both region-level and sample-level, leading to improved performance on synthetic-to-real benchmarks. These methods highlight the importance of carefully designed adaptation strategies in UDA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].

In summary, while many existing methods have made significant progress in UDA for semantic segmentation, they often assume known category settings or rely on specific domain-specific assumptions. The proposed UniDA-SS framework addresses these limitations by introducing a novel approach that does not require prior knowledge of category settings, making it more robust and applicable to real-world scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].
