## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have been widely studied in continual learning (CL) to address catastrophic forgetting, where models lose previously learned knowledge when trained on new tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These methods typically store a subset of past data and train it alongside new data in the current task \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is a common approach, recent work has questioned whether this strategy is always optimal \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Inspired by human learning, where revisiting tasks sequentially helps mitigate forgetting, there is growing interest in exploring sequential rehearsal as a potentially more effective strategy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\].

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of rehearsal-based approaches in mitigating catastrophic forgetting. For instance, experience replay buffers have been proposed as a general solution to reduce forgetting by storing and reusing past experiences \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12631)\]. This approach has been shown to be effective in both reinforcement learning and classification tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Similarly, generative replay has been explored as a method to sample and reuse past data, particularly in scenarios where task identity is not known \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. The idea of using generative models to replay past experiences has been further refined by integrating feedback connections, enabling more efficient and scalable solutions \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\].

The importance of task similarity in determining the effectiveness of rehearsal strategies has also been highlighted. For example, in class-incremental learning scenarios, where tasks are not disjoint, methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) and Memory Aware Synapses (MAS) have been proposed to selectively protect important weights \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\]. These methods aim to preserve knowledge from previous tasks while allowing the model to learn new tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10635)\]. Additionally, the role of memory in continual learning has been extensively studied, with approaches such as the Deep Generative Replay framework proposing a dual model architecture to sample and interleave past and new data \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7bfb4ef17eabec1acd266958bdb08622eebfbb05)\].

In the context of sequential rehearsal, recent work has suggested that training similar tasks concurrently and revisiting dissimilar tasks sequentially may offer better performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. This idea aligns with the theoretical analysis presented in this paper, which compares concurrent and sequential rehearsal strategies in overparameterized linear models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. The analysis shows that sequential rehearsal performs better when tasks are less similar, motivating the development of a hybrid approach that combines both strategies \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\].

In addition to theoretical insights, empirical studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of hybrid rehearsal strategies. For example, the Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) method has been shown to outperform existing approaches by allowing beneficial transfer of knowledge to previous tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12369)\]. Similarly, the Learning without Forgetting method has been proposed to preserve original capabilities while training on new tasks, without requiring access to past data \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09553)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to develop more robust and efficient continual learning strategies.

In summary, while concurrent rehearsal remains a popular approach, recent research has explored sequential rehearsal as a promising alternative, particularly in scenarios where tasks are less similar \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12588)\]. The hybrid rehearsal method proposed in this work builds on these insights, offering a novel approach that combines the strengths of both strategies \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05922)\].

### <a id='category-catastrophic-forgetting-mitigation-techniques'></a>Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation Techniques: 8 papers found.
Catastrophic forgetting remains a significant challenge in continual learning, where models must learn sequentially without losing previously acquired knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Various approaches have been proposed to mitigate this issue, such as task-based hard attention mechanisms that preserve previous task information while learning new tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\], and methods like Learning without Forgetting that enable training on new tasks without access to original task data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Additionally, methods like OML aim to directly minimize catastrophic interference by learning representations that are robust to forgetting and accelerate future learning \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12588)\]. Research also highlights that deeper layers in neural networks are disproportionately affected by forgetting, and strategies that stabilize these layers can be effective \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07400)\]. Furthermore, intelligent synapses have been introduced to accumulate task-relevant information over time, enabling rapid memory storage without forgetting old information \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a99d857ecc78316a0d9a774972b775058d5644ca)\]. While rehearsal-based methods, such as concurrent rehearsal, have shown promise, recent studies suggest that sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first and past data is revisited sequentially, can offer greater benefits, especially when tasks are less similar \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. This insight has led to the development of hybrid rehearsal strategies that combine concurrent and sequential approaches, improving performance in deep neural networks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These techniques reflect ongoing efforts to bridge the gap between human-like lifelong learning capabilities and the limitations of current machine learning models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12631)\].

### <a id='category-experience-replay-and-generative-replay'></a>Experience Replay and Generative Replay: 9 papers found.
Experience Replay and Generative Replay are critical techniques in addressing catastrophic forgetting in continual learning, where models must learn sequentially without losing previously acquired knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Traditional methods often rely on storing past data in replay buffers, which can be memory-intensive and impractical in real-world applications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. To mitigate this, generative replay has emerged as a promising alternative, using deep generative models to sample and replay previous experiences without explicit task boundaries \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. This approach is inspired by the hippocampus's role in memory consolidation, enabling models to maintain performance across tasks without storing all historical data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. However, generative replay faces challenges in scalability and efficiency, particularly when dealing with complex tasks or large datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12631)\]. Recent work has focused on improving generative replay through controlled sampling strategies, such as selecting memories most affected by parameter updates, which enhances performance and reduces forgetting \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Additionally, hybrid approaches combining concurrent and sequential rehearsal have been proposed to optimize learning efficiency, especially when tasks are dissimilar \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. These methods often integrate feedback mechanisms or distillation techniques to further enhance model retention and generalization \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. Overall, experience and generative replay remain central to advancing continual learning, with ongoing efforts to improve their efficiency, scalability, and alignment with biological memory mechanisms \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\].

### <a id='category-continual-learning-frameworks-and-architectures'></a>Continual Learning Frameworks and Architectures: 11 papers found.
The category "Continual Learning Frameworks and Architectures" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at enabling models to learn sequentially without catastrophic forgetting, emphasizing both memory retention and knowledge transfer. Existing works highlight that catastrophic forgetting remains a central challenge, where models struggle to retain past knowledge when learning new tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. To address this, several frameworks have been proposed, such as modular architectures that allow for the composition of atomic skills, enabling efficient learning on long task sequences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Additionally, task-conditioned hypernetworks have been introduced, which generate target model weights based on task identity, allowing for memory retention without explicit task identity at test time \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\]. These models demonstrate strong performance on standard benchmarks and show capacity for long memory retention in compressive regimes \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\]. Another approach, Reinforced Continual Learning, uses reinforcement learning strategies to search for optimal neural architectures for each task, effectively preventing forgetting while fitting new tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12369)\]. Furthermore, hybrid frameworks have been developed that combine architecture growth and experience replay to preserve both task-specific and shared features, offering superior performance over purely memory-based or architecture-based methods \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09553)\]. The concept of dynamic prompting, as seen in the L2P method, allows pre-trained models to learn sequentially by maintaining small learnable prompts in memory, enabling task-invariant and task-specific knowledge management without access to task identity \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08654)\]. These diverse strategies reflect the ongoing efforts to improve continual learning by balancing plasticity and stability, with a growing emphasis on transfer learning and selective memory retention \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10635)\].

### <a id='category-empirical-evaluation-of-rehearsal-techniques'></a>Empirical Evaluation of Rehearsal Techniques: 2 papers found.
In the context of continual learning (CL), prior work has explored methods to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by leveraging rehearsal techniques, where past data is stored and retrained alongside new data. Several studies have evaluated different rehearsal strategies, including concurrent and sequential rehearsal, and their impact on model performance \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12488)\]. Concurrent rehearsal, where past and current data are trained together, has been shown to be effective in some scenarios, though its optimality remains debated \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Sequential rehearsal, which involves training on new data first and then revisiting past data, has been argued to offer greater benefits in certain settings, particularly when tasks are less similar \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. This approach is inspired by human learning, where revisiting past tasks helps reinforce memory \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Additionally, research has investigated the role of episodic memory in CL, with findings suggesting that simple baselines, such as joint training on current and stored examples, can outperform more complex methods \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. The effectiveness of rehearsal strategies often depends on factors like memory size and the diversity of tasks \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12488)\]. Some studies have also emphasized the importance of writing strategies, such as reservoir sampling, which ensures a balanced representation of classes in memory \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. These findings highlight the ongoing exploration of rehearsal-based methods in CL, with a focus on improving generalization and reducing forgetting \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12488)\].
