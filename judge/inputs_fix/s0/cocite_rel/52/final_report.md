## Related Works

Dense retrievers have become a cornerstone in information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) to encode text into dense vector representations that can be compared via simple similarity operations such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. These models typically employ contrastive learning during fine-tuning to improve retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. However, recent research has questioned the relative contributions of pre-training versus fine-tuning in acquiring retrieval knowledge, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily gained during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may only adjust neuron activations rather than reorganize knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This claim was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative dense retriever \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different pre-training and fine-tuning strategies for dense retrieval. For instance, BERT has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art results on various NLP tasks, including question answering and language inference, through its bidirectional training approach \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, BERT's performance can be further optimized through techniques such as RoBERTa, which introduces improvements in training data size and hyperparameter choices \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Additionally, models like Condenser and coCondenser have been proposed to address the limitations of standard language models by pre-training toward dense encoders \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. These models aim to better aggregate text information into dense representations, improving retrieval performance on tasks such as MS-MARCO and Natural Questions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\].

The role of fine-tuning in dense retrieval has also been scrutinized. For example, the use of contrastive learning during fine-tuning has been shown to significantly improve retrieval performance, particularly when combined with large-scale pre-training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. However, the extent to which fine-tuning can acquire new knowledge remains debated. Some studies suggest that fine-tuning may not be sufficient to acquire knowledge not already present in the pre-trained model \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. This is supported by findings that in models such as Contriever, which uses mean pooling, and LLaMA-based decoder-only models, the pattern of pre-training dominance over fine-tuning does not hold universally \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)\].

In addition to architectural choices, the effectiveness of dense retrievers is influenced by training strategies. For example, the use of hard negatives during training has been shown to improve retrieval performance by focusing on the most challenging examples \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15466)\]. Similarly, techniques such as Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE) have been introduced to construct more realistic negative training instances, leading to improved performance on benchmarks such as MS-MARCO \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\].

Finally, the importance of pre-training tasks in learning effective dense representations has been emphasized. For instance, the use of contrastive learning, as demonstrated in SimCSE and SimCLR, has been shown to produce high-quality sentence embeddings that are robust to noise and domain shifts \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00300)\]. These findings highlight the importance of carefully designed pre-training objectives in achieving strong retrieval performance.
