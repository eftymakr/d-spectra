## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advancements focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as ARIMA and exponential smoothing have been widely used, but they often struggle with capturing complex temporal dependencies and non-stationarity \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7b74990bff941aece41437ab9aa9c1f48a3fafca)\]. More recently, deep learning models have emerged as a powerful alternative, offering greater flexibility and performance in forecasting tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13278)\].

Several studies have explored the use of Transformers for time series forecasting, leveraging their ability to model long-range dependencies. However, Transformers face challenges in handling non-stationary data and large lookback windows, leading to performance degradation and computational inefficiency \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various modifications, such as the PatchTST model, which introduces a patching design to retain local semantic information and reduce computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, the Autoformer model incorporates an Auto-Correlation mechanism to improve efficiency and accuracy in long-term forecasting \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of Transformers when properly adapted for time series tasks.

In addition to Transformers, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and their variants, such as LSTMs and GRUs, have been extensively studied for time series analysis \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. The LSTNet framework, for example, combines CNNs and RNNs to capture both short-term and long-term patterns, achieving significant improvements over traditional methods \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. However, these models often require careful tuning and may not generalize well to diverse forecasting tasks without additional adaptations.

Probabilistic forecasting has also gained traction, with models like DeepAR providing accurate forecasts by estimating the probability distribution of future values \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. This approach is particularly useful in financial applications where uncertainty quantification is essential. Similarly, the Multi-Horizon Quantile Recurrent Forecaster (MQRF) extends probabilistic forecasting to multi-step predictions, accommodating both static and dynamic covariates \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11053)\]. These models highlight the importance of incorporating uncertainty into forecasting frameworks, especially in high-stakes domains like finance.

Recent work has also explored the use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting, aiming to leverage their strong pattern recognition and reasoning capabilities \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. However, studies have shown that LLMs may not always provide significant advantages over traditional models, particularly when pre-trained without specific time series knowledge \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. The Chronos framework, which pretrains language models on time series data, has shown promising results in improving zero-shot forecasting performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\]. These findings underscore the potential of LLMs when appropriately adapted for time series tasks.

In summary, while traditional models like ARIMA and exponential smoothing remain relevant, modern deep learning approaches, including Transformers, RNNs, and LLMs, offer significant improvements in forecasting accuracy and robustness. The choice of model depends on the specific requirements of the task, such as the need for uncertainty quantification or the ability to handle non-stationary data. The proposed RTS-PnO framework builds on these advancements, integrating risk-aware forecasting and allocation strategies to address the limitations of existing approaches.

### <a id='category-transformer-based-time-series-models'></a>Transformer-Based Time Series Models: 7 papers found.
Recent advancements in Transformer-based time series models have focused on improving forecasting accuracy, interpretability, and adaptability to complex temporal patterns. Models such as PatchTST \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\] introduce efficient patching strategies to retain local semantic information while reducing computational overhead, enabling better long-term forecasting. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\] combines recurrent layers with self-attention mechanisms to handle multi-horizon forecasting and provide interpretable insights into temporal dynamics. Non-stationary Transformers \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\] address the issue of over-stationarization by incorporating modules that preserve non-stationary characteristics, enhancing predictive performance on real-world data. The iTransformer \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\] reimagines the Transformer architecture by inverting dimensions to better capture multivariate correlations and handle arbitrary lookback windows. Meanwhile, the LTSF-Linear model \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\] challenges the dominance of Transformer-based approaches by demonstrating that simple linear models can outperform complex architectures in long-term forecasting tasks. Chronos \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\] extends the utility of Transformers by pretraining probabilistic models on diverse time series data, achieving strong zero-shot performance across unseen forecasting tasks. These developments highlight the evolving landscape of Transformer-based time series forecasting, emphasizing the need for models that balance accuracy, efficiency, and adaptability to real-world non-stationary data.

### <a id='category-deep-learning-approaches-for-time-series-forecasting'></a>Deep Learning Approaches for Time Series Forecasting: 6 papers found.
Time series forecasting has seen significant advancements through deep learning, with various architectures addressing the challenges of temporal variation and uncertainty. Traditional methods often struggle with capturing complex patterns and providing reliable probabilistic forecasts, whereas deep learning models offer more flexible and accurate solutions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. For instance, DeepAR leverages an auto-regressive recurrent network to produce probabilistic forecasts, achieving notable accuracy improvements over classical approaches \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Similarly, recent works such as TimesNet and TimeMixer introduce innovative methods to model temporal variations by transforming 1D time series into 2D tensors or analyzing multiscale-mixing patterns, respectively \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14616)\]. These models demonstrate enhanced performance in both short- and long-term forecasting tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. Additionally, hybrid models that combine statistical methods with neural networks have been proposed to improve forecast reliability and decision support \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. In financial applications, models like LSTNet integrate CNNs and RNNs to handle multivariate time series, effectively capturing both short-term and long-term dependencies \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. Furthermore, the Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (STGCN) address the spatial and temporal dependencies in traffic forecasting, achieving superior results compared to traditional models \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.04875)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of deep learning in time series forecasting, particularly in domains requiring high accuracy and robustness to uncertainty.

### <a id='category-large-language-models-(llms)-in-time-series-forecasting'></a>Large Language Models (LLMs) in Time Series Forecasting: 5 papers found.
The application of Large Language Models (LLMs) in time series forecasting has garnered significant attention, particularly in financial domains where the integration of multi-modal data and explainability is crucial \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Studies have explored the potential of LLMs to address challenges such as cross-sequence reasoning, incorporation of historical news, and interpretability, with some demonstrating that LLMs can effectively reason over textual and price data to generate explainable forecasts \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. However, ablation studies have also shown that removing the LLM component or replacing it with simpler attention layers does not degrade forecasting performance, sometimes even improving it \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. Additionally, while pre-trained LLMs are computationally expensive, they often do not outperform models trained from scratch and may not capture sequential dependencies in time series data \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. To overcome data scarcity in time series analysis, researchers have leveraged pre-trained language or vision models, fine-tuning them for various tasks and achieving comparable or state-of-the-art results \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11939)\]. Furthermore, frameworks like Time-LLM have been developed to align time series data with natural language by reprogramming inputs and using techniques such as Prompt-as-Prefix to enhance reasoning capabilities \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. These approaches highlight the evolving landscape of using LLMs for time series forecasting, balancing their potential with practical limitations.
