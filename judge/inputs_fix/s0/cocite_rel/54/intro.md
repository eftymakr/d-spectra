## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommender systems, evolving from basic matrix factorization techniques to more sophisticated deep learning approaches \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\]. Early methods such as matrix factorization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] and later neural network-based models like Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] have laid the foundation for modern recommendation systems. NCF introduces a multi-layer perceptron to model the user-item interaction function, enabling non-linear modeling of collaborative signals \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. However, these methods often rely on pre-defined features and do not explicitly encode the collaborative signal from user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Recent advances in graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly impacted CF. LightGCN \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] simplifies the design of GCNs by focusing on neighborhood aggregation, leading to a linear and efficient model that outperforms NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] by about 16.0% on average. Similarly, UltraGCN \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\] further simplifies GCNs by removing infinite layers of message passing, achieving a 10x speedup over LightGCN while maintaining performance. These models emphasize the importance of efficient and scalable graph-based approaches for recommendation.

Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have also been applied to large-scale recommendation systems, as demonstrated by the work at Pinterest \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. This approach leverages efficient random walks and graph convolutions to generate embeddings that incorporate both graph structure and node features, enabling high-quality recommendations on massive graphs. The success of GCNs in recommendation tasks has led to the development of models like SpectralCF \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\], which introduces a spectral convolution operation to capture both proximity and connectivity information in the spectral domain, effectively addressing the cold-start problem.

Another notable contribution is the Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\], which focuses on disentangling user intents by modeling distribution over intents for each user-item interaction. This approach iteratively refines intent-aware interaction graphs and representations, leading to more interpretable and effective recommendations. Additionally, the work on Graph Attention Networks (GATs) \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\] introduces attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of different neighbors in the graph, improving the adaptability of models to different graph structures.

The field has also seen the integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) into recommendation systems, as seen in KGAT \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\], which explicitly models high-order relations in KGs to enhance recommendation accuracy. Furthermore, the use of self-supervised learning for recommendation, as explored in SGL \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\], aims to improve the robustness and accuracy of GCNs by introducing auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning.

In summary, while early CF methods relied on simple matrix operations, recent approaches leverage graph-based models to capture complex collaborative signals. These models, ranging from LightGCN \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] to KGAT \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\], demonstrate the potential of graph-based methods in recommendation systems, addressing challenges such as scalability, interpretability, and robustness.
