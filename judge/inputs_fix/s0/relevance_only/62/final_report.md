## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional retrieval methods, which rely on static index structures and separate encoding and decoding stages \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. Unlike conventional approaches, generative retrieval integrates the entire retrieval process into a single neural network, enabling direct generation of document identifiers from queries \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. This paradigm has attracted significant attention, particularly due to its potential for improved efficiency and seamless integration with large language models \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. However, several challenges remain, including the generalization of generative retrieval to out-of-distribution corpora and the limitations of constrained auto-regressive generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].

Early work on generative retrieval focused on the use of sequence-to-sequence models to directly map queries to document identifiers \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. These models were often trained on large-scale corpora and fine-tuned to capture the relevance distribution of documents \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. However, the effectiveness of these models was largely limited to small-scale datasets, and their scalability to larger corpora remained an open question \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Subsequent studies explored the use of synthetic queries during indexing, which proved crucial for improving retrieval performance on large-scale corpora \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This approach was further refined in the development of the Generative Retrieval for Book Search (GBS) framework, which introduced data augmentation and outline-oriented book encoding to better handle the complexity of book information \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11034)\].

The use of beam search in generative retrieval has also been extensively studied. While beam search is widely used in autoregressive generation, it has been shown to be suboptimal for retrieval tasks, particularly when marginal distributions are used to guide the decoding process \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03678)\]. This limitation was further explored in the context of constrained decoding, where the effectiveness of beam search was questioned due to its inability to fully capture the global probability distribution over generated sequences \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03678)\]. In contrast, recent work on controlled decoding has proposed alternative strategies, such as Discrete Auto-regressive Biasing, which operates entirely in the discrete text domain and improves constraint satisfaction while maintaining fluency \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03685)\].

The generalization capabilities of generative retrieval have also been a focus of recent research. Studies have shown that generative retrieval models can be adapted to dynamic corpora, where documents are frequently updated or added \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\]. This adaptability is particularly important for real-world applications, where the ability to handle evolving information is crucial. The performance of generative retrieval on dynamic corpora has been compared to traditional dual encoder approaches, with generative retrieval demonstrating superior adaptability and efficiency \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\].

In summary, while generative retrieval has shown promise, several challenges remain, including the limitations of constrained auto-regressive generation and the need for more robust generalization across different corpus types. These challenges have motivated recent research into alternative decoding strategies and improved training methodologies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03685)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03678)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\].

### <a id='category-beam-search-limitations'></a>Beam Search Limitations: 1 papers found.
Beam search, while widely used for decoding auto-regressive machine translation models, has limitations in aligning with end metrics or scores that practitioners prioritize \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\]. Although beam search consistently improves BLEU scores, it focuses solely on maximizing model likelihood, ignoring other evaluation criteria \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\]. This gap has led researchers to explore alternative decoding algorithms that incorporate value functions parameterized by neural networks, aiming to better align with target metrics \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\]. One such approach is Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), which has shown competitiveness in certain scenarios \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\]. These methods highlight the need for more flexible decoding strategies that can adapt to diverse evaluation goals, suggesting that the choice of algorithm significantly depends on the specific characteristics of the target metric \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\].
