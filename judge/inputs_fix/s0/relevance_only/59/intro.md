## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has gained significant attention as recommender systems and ranking algorithms increasingly influence decision-making in socially impactful domains. Prior work has identified a trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. This trade-off is analogous to economic principles, where fairness considerations in re-ranking can be likened to a commodity tax on suppliers, ultimately translating into a cost passed on to consumers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. Similarly, item-side fairness constraints result in a decline in user-side accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. In economics, the extent to which a tax on suppliers transfers to users is formalized using the concept of elasticity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. This insight suggests that the re-ranking fairness-accuracy trade-off is similarly governed by the elasticity of utility between item groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].

Several studies have explored fairness-aware re-ranking methods. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\] presents a re-ranking approach that learns individual preferences across multiple fairness dimensions and uses them to enhance provider fairness in recommendation results. Their method achieves a better trade-off between accuracy and fairness than prior approaches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] proposes a Fairness-Aware Re-ranking algorithm (FAR) to balance ranking quality and provider-side fairness. The algorithm iteratively generates the ranking list by trading off between accuracy and the coverage of the providers, while also incorporating personalized user tolerance towards provider diversification \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\].

The concept of fairness in rankings has also been studied in the context of exposure distribution. \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e)\] introduces a framework for fairness constraints on rankings in terms of exposure allocation, allowing for the formulation of fairness goals that can be application-specific. They develop efficient algorithms for finding rankings that maximize user utility while satisfying fairness constraints \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e)\]. This work is closely related to the idea of elasticity in the trade-off between accuracy and fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].

Other studies have focused on fairness-aware recommendation systems that consider both user and item fairness. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00424)\] proposes FairSort, a re-ranking model that finds a trade-off among user-side fairness, provider-side fairness, and personalized recommendations utility. The model treats each recommendation list as a runway, allowing items to gain velocity and run within a specific time, achieving re-ranking for both-side fairness \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00424)\]. Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\] presents a learning-to-rank approach that explicitly enforces merit-based fairness guarantees to groups of items, ensuring amortized group fairness while learning the ranking function from implicit feedback data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\].

In summary, existing work has explored various fairness-aware re-ranking methods, often focusing on specific fairness dimensions or trade-offs between accuracy and fairness. However, few have systematically analyzed the elasticity of utility between item groups or introduced a comprehensive evaluation framework such as the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].
