## Related Works

Nonverbal communication plays a crucial role in human interaction, encompassing gestures, facial expressions, and body language that convey intent and emotion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. However, existing large language models (LLMs) often lack the ability to effectively incorporate these nonverbal elements, limiting their capacity to create fully immersive conversational experiences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. This limitation has been widely recognized in the field of multimodal language analysis, where researchers have explored the integration of nonverbal cues with textual information to enhance the understanding of human conversational utterances \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16427)\]. 

Several studies have attempted to model nonverbal behaviors using multimodal approaches. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\] proposed the Recurrent Attended Variation Embedding Network (RAVEN), which dynamically shifts word representations based on nonverbal cues such as vocal patterns and facial expressions. This work highlights the importance of modeling nonverbal subword sequences to capture the dynamic nature of human communication. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\] introduced the concept of nonverbal alphabets, which are symbols and alphabets that can be communicated through nonverbal means, such as body gestures and eye tracking. These symbols are generated and estimated using multimodal data sources and machine learning methods, demonstrating their potential in applications like synthetic languages and Human-Computer Interfaces \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\].

In addition to modeling nonverbal cues, researchers have also focused on improving the understanding of human communication through multimodal models. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00923)\] proposed the Multi-attention Recurrent Network (MARN), which uses a Multi-attention Block (MAB) to discover interactions between modalities over time and stores them in a hybrid memory structure called the Long-short Term Hybrid Memory (LSTHM). This model has shown state-of-the-art results in multimodal sentiment analysis and emotion recognition \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00923)\]. Furthermore, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16410)\] introduced LENS, a modular approach that leverages large language models (LLMs) to reason over outputs from independent vision modules, enabling the model to perform well in zero- and few-shot object recognition tasks without any multimodal training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16410)\].

The integration of nonverbal cues with text has also been explored in the context of video-based conversation models. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\] introduced Video-ChatGPT, a multimodal model that merges a video-adapted visual encoder with an LLM to understand and generate detailed conversations about videos. This model was trained on a large dataset of video-instruction pairs and evaluated using a quantitative framework to assess its performance in video-based dialogue tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\]. Similarly, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11833)\] introduced MMDU, a benchmark and instruction-tuning dataset designed to evaluate Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) in multi-turn and multi-image conversation scenarios. This dataset includes a large number of image+text tokens and is designed to challenge current LVLMs in real-world human-AI interaction applications \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11833)\].

In summary, the field of nonverbal communication and multimodal language analysis has seen significant advancements, with various models and datasets being proposed to address the challenges of integrating nonverbal cues with text. These works provide a strong foundation for the development of models like MARS, which aims to bridge the gap in conversational AI by incorporating nonverbal elements alongside text.
