## Related Works

The field of large language model (LLM) training and performance modeling has seen significant advancements, particularly in addressing the challenges of distributed execution and efficient deployment. Early work on distributed training focused on optimizing cluster configurations and training speed, often using empirical methods to estimate performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03072)\]. More recently, researchers have introduced frameworks that enable more accurate performance prediction by leveraging empirical data and hardware-specific characteristics \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16363)\]. For example, LLM-Pilot \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\] presents a system for characterizing and predicting LLM inference performance across different hardware platforms, using benchmarking and optimization techniques to improve service efficiency. This aligns with broader efforts to understand and improve the performance of LLMs in real-world deployment scenarios.

Several studies have explored the impact of hardware on LLM performance, particularly in resource-constrained environments. For instance, the paper on distributed inference optimization for LLMs on CPUs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00029)\] demonstrates that efficient data loading and memory management can significantly improve inference speed, achieving a 140 ms/token performance for a 72B parameter model, which is faster than the average human reading speed. Similarly, the work on LLMs with limited memory \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11514)\] introduces techniques such as "windowing" and "row-column bundling" to reduce data transfer and improve inference speed, enabling models up to twice the size of available DRAM to be executed efficiently.

Another critical area of research is the optimization of LLM training and inference through model compression and system-level enhancements. For example, the paper on efficient LLM serving \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15234)\] reviews a wide range of techniques, including model compression (e.g., knowledge distillation and quantization), algorithm improvements (e.g., early exit and mixture-of-experts), and hardware-level optimizations. These approaches aim to reduce computational intensity and memory consumption, making LLM deployment more feasible in low-latency and high-throughput scenarios.

The development of performance models for LLMs has also been a focus of recent research. The work on CM-DARE \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03072)\] introduces a cloud-based framework for analyzing distributed training performance, demonstrating the feasibility of predicting training speed and overhead using regression-based models. This aligns with the broader goal of creating accurate performance models that guide optimization efforts and system-level studies. Additionally, the paper on LLM inference unification \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16363)\] introduces a roofline model-based framework for systematically analyzing LLM inference techniques, identifying bottlenecks and providing insights into hardware and algorithmic trade-offs.

The proposed Lumos toolkit [abstract] builds on these advancements by introducing a trace-driven performance modeling and estimation framework, enabling accurate prediction of execution behaviors across different models and configurations. This aligns with the broader trend of using empirical data to improve the efficiency and reliability of LLM training and inference systems.

The category "LLM Inference Efficiency Strategies" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the performance and cost-effectiveness of deploying Large Language Models (LLMs) on resource-constrained hardware. These strategies include hardware-aware optimization, distributed computing, and predictive modeling to guide deployment decisions. For instance, LLM-Pilot introduces a system for characterizing and predicting the performance of LLM inference services across various GPUs, enabling the optimization of service configurations to maximize performance while reducing costs by up to 60% \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\]. Similarly, distributed computing is leveraged to mitigate single-node memory constraints, with experiments demonstrating that efficient distributed inference on CPUs can achieve inference speeds comparable to human reading speeds \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00029)\]. Additionally, innovative techniques such as "windowing" and "row-column bundling" are proposed to reduce data transfer and improve inference speed by up to 25x on GPUs by optimizing how model parameters are loaded from flash memory \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11514)\]. Furthermore, a comprehensive survey introduces a framework based on the roofline model to systematically analyze LLM inference techniques, identifying bottlenecks and guiding hardware and system-level improvements \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.16363)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the importance of combining hardware-aware design, algorithmic improvements, and predictive modeling to enhance the efficiency of LLM inference.

### <a id='category-efficient-deployment-and-latency-reduction'></a>Efficient Deployment and Latency Reduction: 3 papers found.
Efficient deployment and latency reduction in large language models (LLMs) have been a focal point of recent research, with significant advancements aimed at improving both performance and practicality \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800)\]. Traditional LLMs face challenges in deployment due to their high parameter counts, prompting the development of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures that offer higher performance with fewer parameters \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800)\]. However, deploying MoE LLMs remains challenging due to their computational demands, leading to the exploration of plug-and-play expert-level sparsification techniques to enhance deployment efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800)\]. These methods include task-agnostic and task-specific expert pruning and skipping, which help reduce model size and increase inference speed while maintaining performance across various tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800)\]. In parallel, broader efforts in efficient LLM serving methodologies have emphasized the need for system-level optimizations, covering algorithmic modifications and system design changes to address the computational intensity and memory consumption of LLMs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15234)\]. Additionally, similar challenges are observed in diffusion models (DMs), where training and inference are computationally expensive, prompting innovations such as latent space training and cross-attention layers to reduce complexity while preserving detail and flexibility \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the growing importance of efficient deployment strategies in making large-scale models more practical for real-world applications.
