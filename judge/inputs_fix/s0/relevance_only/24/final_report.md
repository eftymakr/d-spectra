## Related Works

Diffusion Transformers (DiT) have emerged as a powerful architecture for image and video generation, offering superior performance compared to traditional diffusion models based on U-Net architectures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. However, their large parameter counts and high computational demands pose significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. To address these limitations, model quantization has been explored as a means to reduce storage requirements and accelerate inference by lowering the bit-width of model parameters \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. While quantization has been extensively studied for image generation models, its application to video generation tasks remains challenging due to the unique temporal dynamics and multi-frame processing involved \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\].

Several recent works have investigated quantization techniques tailored for diffusion models. ViDiT-Q was introduced as an efficient quantization method for DiT models, achieving W8A8 quantization with minimal degradation in visual quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Q-DiT further advanced this field by addressing the spatial and temporal variance in DiT weights and activations, achieving a notable reduction in FID when quantizing DiT-XL/2 to W6A8 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Similarly, DVD-Quant proposed a data-free quantization framework for video DiTs, enabling W4A4 PTQ without compromising video quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18663)\]. These methods highlight the growing interest in quantizing DiT models for efficient deployment.

The challenges of quantization in diffusion models are further compounded by the temporal variation in activations and the asymmetric distribution of network values \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04056)\]. TQ-DiT introduced time-aware quantization techniques to mitigate these issues, achieving performance comparable to full-precision models at W8A8 \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14172)\]. PTQ4DiT was specifically designed for DiTs, addressing salient channel magnitudes and temporal variability in activation distributions, enabling W4A8 quantization for the first time \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. These approaches demonstrate the importance of tailoring quantization strategies to the unique characteristics of DiT models.

In addition to post-training quantization (PTQ), quantization-aware training (QAT) has also been explored for diffusion models. EfficientDM introduced a data-free and parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework, achieving QAT-level performance with PTQ-like efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03270)\]. QVGen further advanced this area by proposing a quantization-aware training framework tailored for video diffusion models under extremely low-bit quantization \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11497)\]. These methods underscore the potential of QAT in achieving high-quality quantization for diffusion models.

The effectiveness of quantization in diffusion models is also influenced by the choice of bit-width and the alignment of quantization with the model's architecture. HQ-DiT introduced a hybrid floating-point quantization method, achieving W4A4 quantization with minimal performance degradation \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\]. FP4DiT leveraged floating-point quantization to achieve W4A6 quantization, demonstrating its potential for DiT models \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15465)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of selecting appropriate quantization strategies based on the specific requirements of the model.

In summary, the field of diffusion model quantization has seen significant advancements, with various methods tailored to address the unique challenges of DiT models. These works collectively demonstrate the potential of quantization in enabling efficient deployment of diffusion models on resource-constrained devices.

Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a critical technique for reducing the computational and memory footprint of diffusion models, particularly Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), which are increasingly used for image and video generation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. To address these challenges, several PTQ methods have been proposed, each targeting specific issues in DiT quantization. For instance, Q-DiT introduces automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization to handle spatial and temporal variances in DiTs, achieving significant performance improvements under both W6A8 and W4A8 settings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Similarly, PTQ4DiT tackles salient channel magnitudes and temporal activation variability through Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen’s ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC), enabling DiTs to be quantized to 8-bit (W8A8) and even 4-bit (W4A8) precision without substantial performance loss \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. Additionally, HQ-DiT leverages 4-bit floating-point (FP) precision for both weights and activations, offering a more accurate alignment with DiT’s data distribution and minimizing quantization errors \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\]. For video generation, static quantization methods like those applied to OpenSora demonstrate that static quantization can match the performance of dynamic quantization, achieving video quality comparable to FP16 while enabling efficient deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15077)\]. Furthermore, FP4DiT extends PTQ to DiTs by employing floating-point quantization (FPQ) to achieve W4A6 precision, outperforming integer-based PTQ methods in terms of CLIP, ImageReward, and HPSv2 scores \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15465)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the growing interest in PTQ for DiTs, with a focus on preserving quality while enabling efficient deployment.

It utilizes a shared DiT backbone pre-trained on image-only data, with lightweight adapters for modality-specific adaptations, and incorporates temporal attention layers to ensure temporal consistency \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07686)\]. Both documents emphasize the adaptability of diffusion transformers to different modalities and tasks, showcasing their potential in expanding the scope of generative models beyond traditional 2D image generation.
