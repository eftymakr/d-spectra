## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have become a cornerstone of modern information retrieval systems, particularly in Web search, due to their ability to learn complex representations from raw data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11903)\]. However, these models typically require substantial amounts of labeled query-document pairs for training, which are both costly and labor-intensive to obtain \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. To address this challenge, researchers have explored synthetic query generation as a scalable alternative, leveraging large language models (LLMs) to generate queries from Web document collections like ClueWeb22 \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. While this approach offers promise, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal downstream retrieval performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\].

Existing methods typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. However, this approach introduces additional complexity and may not fully address the root cause of suboptimal performance. In contrast, our work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach aims to optimize the model towards generating high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

DPO has been widely adopted for preference alignment of LLMs due to its simplicity and effectiveness \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\]. It directly optimizes LLMs using human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This method has been shown to be stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or significant hyperparameter tuning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been extended to address various challenges, such as token-level importance sampling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\], the inclusion of rationales in preference data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14477)\], and the optimization of dense retrieval models with hard negatives \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\].

In addition to DPO, other approaches have explored the use of synthetic queries for retrieval tasks. For example, some studies have investigated the use of synthetically generated queries for evaluating retrievability bias in PLM-based rankers \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]. These studies have shown that synthetically generated queries can lead to lower bias compared to user-generated queries, suggesting that they may be more suitable for training retrieval models \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]. Similarly, other works have explored the use of weak supervision for training neural ranking models, using pseudo query-document pairs that already exhibit relevance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\]. These approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in improving retrieval performance without requiring extensive manual labeling \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\].

In summary, while synthetic query generation offers a scalable alternative to labeled data in neural retrieval, it remains a challenging task due to the variability in query quality. DPO provides a promising solution by integrating ranking signals directly into the query generation process, thereby improving the quality of generated queries and downstream retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Other related approaches, such as weak supervision and synthetic query evaluation, have also contributed to the field, highlighting the importance of diverse methods in improving retrieval effectiveness \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\].

### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 8 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a promising approach for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences, offering a streamlined alternative to reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Unlike RLHF, which requires complex reward modeling and reinforcement learning steps, DPO directly optimizes LLMs using human preference data, eliminating the need for explicit reward modeling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\]. This method has been shown to be stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, allowing for simpler implementation and training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO has been applied in various contexts, including improving the performance of LLMs on downstream tasks like reasoning and summarization \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\], enhancing counter-speech generation by aligning models with human preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\], and improving the alignment of models with human values in E-commerce search scenarios \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19829)\]. Additionally, researchers have proposed variants of DPO, such as DPO-Positive (DPOP), which addresses potential failure modes by modifying the loss function to avoid reducing the likelihood of preferred examples \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\]. Furthermore, token-level importance sampling in DPO, known as TIS-DPO, has been introduced to improve optimization efficiency by assigning importance weights to tokens based on their reward \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility and effectiveness of DPO in preference alignment, making it a key area of research in the field of LLM development \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]\[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19829)\].
