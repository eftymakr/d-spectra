## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been a central topic in recent years, with a growing body of research addressing the issue of miscalibration in neural networks. Traditional approaches to calibration often rely on post-hoc methods that transform the output probabilities of a model to better align with the true likelihood of correct predictions. For example, temperature scaling \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\] has been widely used as a simple and effective method for calibrating neural networks by applying a single parameter to adjust the output logits. However, this approach is limited in its flexibility, as it assumes a fixed functional form \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.06820)\]. Similarly, Platt scaling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\] and histogram binning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\] have been proposed, but they often fail to capture complex calibration patterns in modern deep networks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\].

Recent work has explored more flexible calibration methods, such as Bayesian binning \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8d115c3b2ee80e0754360a154a9369bc1658b607)\], which is a non-parametric approach that improves calibration by binning predicted probabilities. This method is particularly useful when combined with existing classification algorithms. Another approach, Dirichlet calibration \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\], extends the idea of beta calibration from binary classification to multiclass settings, offering a native multiclass calibration method that is easily implemented with neural networks. These methods, while effective, are still constrained by their fixed functional forms, limiting their ability to model complex calibration behaviors.

The use of ensembles has also been explored as a means to improve calibration. Deep ensembles \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\] provide a simple and scalable way to estimate predictive uncertainty by averaging predictions across multiple models. This approach is computationally efficient and yields high-quality uncertainty estimates that are as good or better than approximate Bayesian methods. However, it requires training multiple models, which can be resource-intensive.

In addition to post-hoc calibration, there has been research on learning calibration directly during training. For instance, focal loss \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\] has been shown to produce well-calibrated models without compromising accuracy. This method addresses miscalibration by adjusting the loss function to focus on hard examples, leading to more reliable confidence scores. Similarly, mixup training \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\] has been found to improve calibration by encouraging the model to learn more robust and generalized representations, which in turn leads to better-calibrated predictions.

Furthermore, the use of dropout as a Bayesian approximation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)\] has been proposed as a way to model uncertainty in deep learning. This approach allows for the estimation of model uncertainty without the computational overhead of full Bayesian inference. However, it still requires careful tuning and may not be suitable for all applications.

In summary, while various methods have been proposed to address calibration in deep learning, many are limited in their flexibility and effectiveness in complex scenarios. The proposed method of using an Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Network (UMNN) offers a more flexible and expressive approach to calibration, allowing for arbitrary monotonic functions to be learned, thereby improving the overall calibration performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\].
