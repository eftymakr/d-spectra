## Related Works

Generative retrieval (GR) has recently emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval (IR), shifting the focus from traditional index-retrieve pipelines to end-to-end document retrieval via sequence-to-sequence models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Unlike conventional methods that rely on precomputed dense vectors or inverted indexes, GR models directly generate document identifiers (docids) for a given query \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\]. However, most existing GR models are primarily evaluated on static document collections, with limited exploration of their performance in dynamic corpora where documents are continuously added \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This section reviews related works in generative retrieval, particularly focusing on docid design, scalability, and continual learning.

Docid design is a critical factor in GR performance. Current approaches typically use either numeric-based or text-based docids. Numeric-based docids are efficient but often lead to poor generalization in dynamic settings due to their strong dependence on the initial training set \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This overfitting issue is exacerbated in dynamic corpora, where new documents are frequently added \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. In contrast, text-based docids, which are more semantically aligned with language models' pretrained knowledge, demonstrate superior performance in dynamic settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This is attributed to their fine-grained design and high lexical diversity, which enable better adaptation to new documents \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Several works have explored the benefits of text-based docids, such as in GenRet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\], which learns semantic docids via a discrete auto-encoding approach, and in NOVO \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6682a3679a17d090830635f1c420126d20eae54a)\], which proposes learnable and interpretable docids optimized for retrieval tasks.

Scalability remains a significant challenge for generative retrieval models, especially when dealing with large-scale corpora. Early works like the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\] demonstrated the potential of using a single Transformer to encode the entire corpus, but scalability to millions of passages was not thoroughly investigated until more recent studies \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. These studies revealed that synthetic queries are essential during indexing, and model parameter scaling does not always translate to improved retrieval performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. To address these challenges, works such as DSI++ \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\] and Ultron \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\] have proposed continual learning strategies to incrementally update models with new documents while maintaining retrieval performance over previously indexed content.

In addition to scalability and continual learning, several works have focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of generative retrieval. For instance, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\] introduces a prefix-aware decoder to enhance recall performance, while the Auto Search Indexer (ASI) \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12455)\] proposes a fully end-to-end retrieval paradigm that automatically learns optimal docids for both existing and new documents. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to bridge the gap between traditional retrieval methods and the flexibility of generative models.

In summary, while GR has shown promising results, several challenges remain, including scalability, continual learning, and docid design. The works reviewed here provide a foundation for addressing these challenges and advancing the field of generative retrieval.

Continual learning in dynamic settings has gained significant attention due to the increasing need for models that can adapt to evolving document collections. Existing generative retrieval (GR) models typically assume static corpora, which limits their applicability in real-world scenarios where new documents are continuously added \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Recent work has focused on addressing this challenge by proposing mechanisms that allow incremental indexing of new documents while preserving the ability to retrieve both previously and newly indexed content \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. For instance, Incremental Product Quantization has been introduced to efficiently encode new documents into docids with minimal computational overhead, enabling scalable updates to the model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Additionally, memory-augmented learning mechanisms have been proposed to mitigate forgetting, ensuring that the model maintains knowledge of older documents while learning from new ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Similarly, Differentiable Search Indices (DSIs) have been extended to support continual learning, where models can be updated incrementally without requiring full retraining, thus reducing computational costs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in developing robust continual learning strategies for GR, particularly in dynamic environments where document collections are not static. The challenge remains to balance efficiency with performance, as models with numeric-based docids often suffer from performance degradation over time due to overfitting on initial data \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. Overall, these studies underscore the importance of continual learning in dynamic settings and provide foundational insights for future research in this area.

### <a id='category-docid-design-approaches'></a>Docid Design Approaches: 6 papers found.
Document retrieval methods have evolved significantly, particularly with the rise of generative retrieval (GR) paradigms that aim to directly generate document identifiers (docids) for retrieval tasks. Traditional index-retrieve frameworks, while effective, are limited in their ability to optimize retrieval quality in an end-to-end manner \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. In contrast, GR approaches represent documents as discrete identifiers and use sequence-to-sequence models to generate these identifiers based on queries, enabling more flexible and semantically rich retrieval \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. However, the design of docids remains a critical challenge. Early approaches relied on fixed rule-based docids, such as document titles or BERT clustering results, which often fail to capture full semantic information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. More recent methods, such as GenRet, propose learning docids through discrete auto-encoding, allowing for semantic-rich representations that are optimized for retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Similarly, NOVO introduces learnable docids based on n-grams, which can be optimized during training to better capture document semantics and relationships \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. Other works, like Ultron, focus on model-based indexers that encode document knowledge directly into the model, using semantically meaningful docids to improve retrieval performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) further advances this by parameterizing the entire retrieval pipeline, assigning meaningful identifiers based on query generation and using semantic features for document memorization \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of docid design in GR, with text-based docids showing superior generalization to unseen documents and dynamic corpora \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. While numeric-based docids offer efficiency, they often suffer from overfitting and poor performance in evolving collections \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This has led to the development of hybrid docid designs that combine the strengths of both text-based and numeric-based approaches, aiming for improved performance without additional retraining \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-(gr)-overview'></a>Generative Retrieval (GR) Overview: 4 papers found.
Generative retrieval (GR) has been increasingly recognized as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to sequence-to-sequence modeling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This approach encodes entire document corpora within a single Transformer, enabling the generation of document identifiers as the retrieval target \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. However, existing GR models are primarily evaluated on static corpora, with limited exploration of their performance on dynamic corpora where document collections evolve continuously \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of docid design in GR, showing that text-based docids, which align with the semantic knowledge of language models, offer superior generalization to unseen documents compared to numeric-based docids \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. The latter, while efficient, suffer from overfitting on the training set, leading to significant performance drops in dynamic settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Furthermore, fine-grained docid designs have been found to enhance retrieval performance, surpassing BM25 and even matching dense retrieval methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. These findings underscore the critical role of docid design in GR, particularly in dynamic environments, and have inspired the development of novel multi-docid designs that combine the strengths of both text-based and numeric-based approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. The challenges and opportunities in scaling generative retrieval to larger corpora and improving its robustness in real-world scenarios continue to drive research in this area \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].
