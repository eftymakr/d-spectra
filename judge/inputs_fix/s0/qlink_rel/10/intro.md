## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike traditional next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts the next $k$ tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly for large models, by enabling more efficient training and faster inference \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in adapting to the MTP objective \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several studies have explored alternative training strategies to improve the effectiveness of MTP. For instance, curriculum learning has been proposed as a method to gradually introduce complexity during pre-training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to recurrent neural networks, where it was used to improve sequence prediction tasks by gradually reducing the reliance on ground-truth tokens during training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. Similarly, in the context of MTP, a forward curriculum has been introduced, which incrementally increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This strategy enables SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective, leading to improved downstream performance and generative output quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Conversely, a reverse curriculum has also been explored, which starts with MTP and gradually reduces the number of predicted tokens \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has shown stronger NTP performance and output quality, although it does not provide the same benefits in terms of self-speculative decoding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The effectiveness of these curricula highlights the importance of carefully designing training strategies to match the capabilities of different model sizes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The concept of curriculum learning is not limited to MTP. Earlier work on sequence prediction with recurrent neural networks demonstrated that a curriculum learning strategy can significantly improve performance by gradually shifting the training process from fully guided to less guided \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. This principle has been applied in various domains, including image captioning, where it led to significant improvements in sequence generation tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\].

Additionally, the use of process supervision has been explored as a method to improve reasoning capabilities in large language models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. By providing feedback at each intermediate step of the reasoning process, process supervision has been shown to outperform outcome supervision, particularly for complex tasks like mathematical reasoning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. This approach has been applied to the MATH dataset, where a process-supervised model achieved 78% accuracy on a representative subset of the test set \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\].

Finally, the importance of training data size and model scale has been extensively studied, with findings indicating that compute-optimal training requires equal scaling of both model size and training tokens \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)\]. This insight has led to the development of models like Chinchilla, which outperformed previous models on a range of downstream tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)\].
