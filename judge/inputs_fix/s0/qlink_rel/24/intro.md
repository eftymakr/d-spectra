## Related Works

Diffusion models have become a cornerstone in generative modeling, particularly for image and video synthesis, due to their ability to produce high-quality outputs through iterative noise estimation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05233)\]. However, their computational demands and memory usage hinder deployment on edge devices, motivating research into quantization techniques to reduce model size and accelerate inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising solution, allowing for model compression without retraining \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08295)\]. Despite its advantages, PTQ faces challenges when applied to diffusion transformers (DiTs), which are increasingly used in video generation due to their scalability and performance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. 

Existing quantization methods for image generation models often fail to generalize to video generation tasks due to two primary issues: the loss of information during quantization and the misalignment between optimization objectives and the unique requirements of video generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. For instance, quantization noise can accumulate over time steps, leading to a low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and degraded video quality \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]. Additionally, the temporal and spatial correlations in video data are not well captured by existing quantization strategies \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\].

To address these challenges, several works have explored PTQ for DiTs. Q-DiT \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\] introduces a novel approach that integrates automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization, achieving significant improvements in image and video generation. Similarly, PTQ4DiT \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\] proposes Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's $\rho$-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) to handle the unique characteristics of DiTs, enabling 8-bit and even 4-bit quantization with minimal performance degradation. These methods demonstrate that careful consideration of temporal and spatial variations is essential for effective quantization of DiTs.

Another line of work, such as ViDiT-Q \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\], focuses on improving quantization for diffusion transformers used in image and video generation by addressing the specific challenges of text-to-image and video tasks. They introduce an improved quantization scheme that achieves W8A8 and W4A8 with negligible degradation in visual quality. Similarly, HQ-DiT \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\] utilizes 4-bit floating-point (FP) precision on both weights and activations, achieving minimal quantization error by aligning with the data distribution within DiT.

The need for efficient quantization of video DiT models has also been addressed by works such as PTQD \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\], which proposes a unified formulation for quantization noise and diffusion perturbed noise, leading to significant improvements in FID scores and computational efficiency. Additionally, Temporal Dynamic Quantization \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\] dynamically adjusts the quantization interval based on time step information, significantly improving output quality without computational overhead during inference.

These efforts highlight the importance of tailoring quantization strategies to the unique characteristics of DiTs and video generation tasks. Our work, Q-VDiT, builds upon these advancements by introducing Token-aware Quantization Estimator (TQE) and Temporal Maintenance Distillation (TMD) to address the specific challenges of video DiT quantization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\].
