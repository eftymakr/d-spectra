## Related Works

Event cameras, which capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis, offer significant advantages over traditional frame-based cameras, including high temporal resolution, high dynamic range, and low power consumption \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. These properties make them particularly suitable for applications such as autonomous driving, robotics, and high-speed vision tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces challenges in processing and analysis, particularly in terms of bandwidth, memory, and computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Prior work has explored event downsampling as a means to reduce these demands, but most approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, limiting their adaptability to different scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\].

Several studies have investigated the use of event cameras for tasks such as optical flow estimation, object classification, and visual odometry. For instance, EV-FlowNet introduces a self-supervised deep learning pipeline for optical flow estimation from event data, using grayscale images captured at the same time as the events as a supervisory signal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Similarly, the work in \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07913)\] proposes a novel feature representation and machine learning architecture for event-based object classification, addressing the lack of effective low-level representations and large real-world datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07913)\]. The Multivehicle Stereo Event Camera Dataset \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10202)\] provides a rich source of labeled data for 3D perception tasks, including feature tracking, visual odometry, and stereo depth estimation, which is essential for training and evaluating algorithms \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10202)\].

Event-based vision has also been integrated with deep learning techniques to improve performance in challenging conditions. For example, the paper \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\] presents a deep neural network approach for steering prediction in self-driving cars, demonstrating that event cameras can provide robust performance even in cases where traditional cameras fail, such as under challenging illumination conditions or during fast motion \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. Additionally, the work in \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\] proposes a recurrent neural network to reconstruct videos from event data, enabling the application of off-the-shelf computer vision algorithms to event-based data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\]. This approach has shown significant improvements in image quality and performance compared to existing methods \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\].

The integration of event cameras with other sensors, such as IMUs and lidar, has also been explored to enhance robustness in visual SLAM and state estimation. For instance, the work in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06310)\] presents a hybrid pipeline that combines events, images, and IMU data, leading to improved accuracy in high-speed and high-dynamic-range scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06310)\]. This approach has enabled autonomous flight in previously inaccessible environments, such as low-light conditions and high dynamic range scenes \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06310)\].

In summary, while event cameras offer unique advantages, the development of specialized algorithms and datasets is crucial for unlocking their full potential. The proposed framework, POLED, addresses these challenges by introducing a probabilistic approach for event importance modeling and zero-shot event downsampling, enabling adaptive and efficient processing of event streams \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\].
