## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has attracted significant attention due to the high cost of pixel-level annotations in real-world scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Traditional UDA methods assume that the category settings between source and target domains are known, which is often not the case in practical applications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes exist in the target domain \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. To address this limitation, several approaches have been proposed to improve domain adaptation without prior knowledge of category settings.

One line of research focuses on improving the alignment between source and target domains by leveraging adversarial learning. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] introduces a method that aligns the marginal distribution in the feature space through adversarial learning. However, this global alignment strategy does not consider the local category-level feature distribution, which can lead to misalignment of well-aligned categories \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. To address this, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\] introduces a category-level adversarial network that enforces local semantic consistency during global alignment. This approach improves the alignment of categories by adjusting the adversarial loss based on category-level alignment \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\].

Another approach, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\], proposes a curriculum-style learning method to minimize the domain gap by first solving easy tasks and then inferring properties about the target domain. This method is particularly effective for urban scenes, where strong idiosyncrasies exist in the spatial layout of objects \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\]. Similarly, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10349)\] introduces a two-step self-supervised domain adaptation approach that minimizes both inter-domain and intra-domain gaps. This method separates the target domain into easy and hard splits and then uses self-supervised adaptation from the easy to the hard subdomain \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\].

In addition to adversarial learning, some works focus on improving pseudo-label quality. \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\] proposes a method that estimates prediction uncertainty during training to rectify pseudo label learning, which helps improve the robustness of the adaptation process. This approach dynamically sets confidence thresholds based on prediction variance and rectifies learning from noisy pseudo labels \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02649)\]. Similarly, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03773)\] introduces a framework that uses self-ensembling with GAN-based data augmentation to enhance the performance of the segmentation network on the target domain \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\].

Recent methods also explore the use of depth information to improve domain adaptation. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08040)\] proposes a depth-aware domain adaptation framework that leverages dense depth in the source domain to improve the performance of the target domain. This method achieves state-of-the-art results on synthetic-to-real benchmarks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.03213)\]. Additionally, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12833)\] introduces a method that uses self-supervised depth estimation to bridge the domain gap, showing that depth information can help refine pseudo-labels and improve semantic segmentation accuracy \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b15362b9a025071aa170f7ed81a761bc057c859)\].

In summary, while many approaches have been proposed to address domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, most assume known category settings or rely on adversarial learning. The proposed UniMAP framework addresses these limitations by introducing a novel approach that does not require prior knowledge of category settings and improves the identification of common classes through domain-specific prototypes and image matching \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05427)\].

### <a id='category-pseudo-label-quality-improvement-methods'></a>Pseudo-label Quality Improvement Methods: 6 papers found.
In the context of unsupervised domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, several approaches have been proposed to improve pseudo-label quality and address domain shift challenges. These methods often focus on reducing the reliance on labeled data and improving the adaptability of models to new domains. For instance, entropy minimization has been widely used in semi-supervised learning to encourage confident predictions, although it can lead to bias towards easy-to-transfer samples \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. To counteract this, the maximum squares loss was introduced to balance the gradient contributions from different samples, ensuring a more uniform training process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Similarly, the DACS method addresses pseudo-label quality by mixing images from both source and target domains along with their corresponding labels and pseudo-labels, leading to state-of-the-art results on benchmarks like GTA5 to Cityscapes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\]. Another approach, ProDA, leverages representative prototypes and feature distances to refine pseudo-labels and align feature spaces across domains, further improving performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\]. Additionally, methods such as those proposed in \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03773)\] focus on estimating prediction uncertainty during training to correct noisy pseudo-labels, dynamically adjusting confidence thresholds based on prediction variance. These techniques collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance pseudo-label reliability and domain adaptation performance, with a growing emphasis on balancing scalability and accuracy in real-world applications \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12197)\].

### <a id='category-self-supervised-domain-adaptation-techniques'></a>Self-supervised Domain Adaptation Techniques: 5 papers found.
In the realm of self-supervised domain adaptation techniques for semantic segmentation, several approaches have been proposed to address the challenges of domain shift and limited annotated data. Traditional methods often rely on annotated data, which is labor-intensive and impractical for large-scale applications \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have explored the use of synthetic data and domain adaptation strategies that bridge the gap between source and target domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. However, these methods often overlook the intra-domain variability within the target data, leading to suboptimal performance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\]. To tackle this, a two-step self-supervised domain adaptation approach has been introduced, which first reduces the inter-domain gap and then addresses the intra-domain gap by adapting from an easy to a hard subdomain \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. Another approach proposes a bidirectional learning framework that alternately improves image translation and segmentation adaptation models, enhancing performance through mutual promotion \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. Additionally, a source-free domain adaptation framework has been developed, enabling model adaptation without access to the source dataset, thus preserving privacy and improving practicality \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\]. These methods collectively highlight the importance of addressing both inter-domain and intra-domain gaps to achieve robust semantic segmentation across different domains.

### <a id='category-category-level-alignment-strategies'></a>Category-level Alignment Strategies: 3 papers found.
Category-level alignment strategies have been extensively explored in unsupervised domain adaptation for semantic segmentation to address domain shift and improve cross-domain performance. These strategies focus on aligning features at the category level rather than globally, which helps avoid misalignment of well-aligned categories during global alignment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. For instance, some approaches introduce category-level adversarial networks to enforce local semantic consistency during global alignment by adjusting adversarial losses based on the alignment status of each class \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]. This allows for more precise alignment of features while preserving the distinctiveness of categories. Additionally, methods have been proposed to differentiate between stuff and thing categories, as stuff categories tend to share similar appearances across domains, whereas things (object instances) exhibit larger differences \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08040)\]. To address this, some approaches generate feature representations for individual instances of thing categories and encourage alignment with the most similar instance in the source domain, thus reducing over-alignment and improving accuracy. These category-level alignment strategies aim to enhance the discriminative power of features while maintaining semantic consistency, which is crucial for robust domain adaptation. The effectiveness of these strategies has been validated on benchmark tasks such as GTA5 → Cityscapes and SYNTHIA → Cityscapes, demonstrating significant improvements over previous methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08040)\].

Curriculum-style learning for domain adaptation has emerged as a promising approach to address the domain mismatch between synthetic and real data in semantic segmentation tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\]. This method leverages the idea of solving easier tasks first to infer domain-specific properties, which are then used to guide the training of the segmentation network \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\]. For instance, initial steps involve learning global and local label distributions, which are easier to estimate due to the idiosyncrasies of urban traffic scenes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\]. Building on this concept, recent work proposes a self-motivated pyramid curriculum domain adaptation (PyCDA) that constructs a pyramid of properties about the target domain, including label distributions over images, regions, and pixels \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09547)\]. By enforcing the network to observe these properties, the generalization capability to the target domain is improved without requiring additional models or solving complex minmax problems \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09547)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of curriculum-style learning in bridging the domain gap, offering a structured and efficient way to adapt models from synthetic to real-world data.
