## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have shown significant promise in web search tasks, particularly due to their ability to capture complex semantic relationships between queries and documents \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. However, training these models typically requires large amounts of labeled query-document pairs, which are expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Recent advances have leveraged large language models to generate synthetic queries, offering a scalable alternative to manual labeling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal performance in downstream retrieval tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Existing approaches typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This method, while effective, introduces additional complexity and computational overhead. In contrast, our work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO is a novel parameterization of the reward model in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach eliminates the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning and simplifies the training process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Several studies have explored the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align language models with human preferences \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. For example, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\] applies preference modeling and RLHF to fine-tune language models to act as helpful and harmless assistants, improving performance across a range of NLP tasks. Similarly, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741)\] explores learning from human preferences to solve complex RL tasks without access to the reward function, demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach in environments such as Atari games and simulated robot locomotion.

In the context of dense retrieval, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\] presents Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Learning (ANCE), which constructs negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index of the corpus. This method improves the training process by ensuring that the negative instances used are representative of the irrelevant documents in testing, thus resolving the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Additionally, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)\] introduces a novel design for $k$-selection, enabling efficient similarity search on large-scale datasets, which is crucial for training retrieval models on massive document collections \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)\].

Furthermore, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\] presents ColBERT, a ranking model that adapts deep language models for efficient retrieval by introducing a late interaction architecture. This architecture independently encodes the query and the document and then employs a cheap yet powerful interaction step to model their fine-grained similarity, significantly improving retrieval speed and efficiency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

In summary, the related works highlight the importance of integrating ranking signals into the query generation process, leveraging reinforcement learning from human feedback, and improving the efficiency and effectiveness of retrieval models through advanced training strategies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\].
