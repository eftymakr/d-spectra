## Related Works

The rapid growth of open-source machine learning (ML) resources has significantly impacted information retrieval (IR) research, as noted in recent studies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11164)\]. However, existing platforms such as Hugging Face have limitations in their ability to support advanced queries and analyses, such as tracing model evolution and recommending relevant datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11164)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges and contribute to the broader field of ML resource management and IR.

Several studies have examined the characteristics and usage patterns of ML models on Hugging Face. For instance, a repository mining study found that while Hugging Face hosts a vast number of ML models, the carbon footprint of these models is often poorly reported, with only a small proportion of models providing detailed emissions data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13380)\]. This highlights a gap in the transparency and documentation of ML models, which is critical for effective resource management \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13380)\]. Similarly, another study analyzed the evolution and maintenance of ML models on Hugging Face, revealing that model maintenance is often inconsistent, with a lack of structured documentation and versioning practices \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13822)\].

The importance of structured representations for advanced querying and analysis has been emphasized in multiple studies. For example, a paper on dataset documentation practices on Hugging Face found that while dataset cards are widely used, they often lack completeness, particularly in sections such as "Considerations for Using the Data" \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02552)\]. This suggests that the absence of standardized and comprehensive documentation hinders the ability to perform in-depth analyses of ML resources \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02552)\]. Additionally, a study on the reuse of pre-trained models (PTMs) in the Hugging Face ecosystem identified challenges such as missing attributes, discrepancies between claimed and actual performance, and model risks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14841)\]. These challenges underscore the need for more structured and transparent representations of ML resources to support advanced querying and analysis \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14841)\].

The development of knowledge graphs for ML resources has been explored in prior work. A study on the Hugging Face repository highlighted the potential of knowledge graphs to capture domain-specific relationships and textual attributes, enabling more sophisticated analyses of ML resources \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08205)\]. This aligns with the goals of HuggingKG, which aims to provide a structured representation of ML resources to support advanced IR tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11164)\]. Furthermore, a survey on transfer learning emphasized the importance of structured data for improving the performance of learning algorithms across different domains \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13058)\]. This reinforces the need for structured representations in ML resource management \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13058)\].

In summary, existing studies have identified key challenges in the management and analysis of ML resources on platforms like Hugging Face, including poor documentation, inconsistent versioning, and limited structured representations. These challenges motivate the development of systems like HuggingKG to support advanced querying and analysis of ML resources \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11164)\].

Transfer learning has emerged as a critical framework for addressing the challenges of learning in scenarios where training and test data may differ in distribution or feature space \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a25fbcbbae1e8f79c4360d26aa11a3abf1a11972)\]. This is particularly relevant in real-world applications where data from different domains may not align, making traditional machine learning approaches less effective \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a25fbcbbae1e8f79c4360d26aa11a3abf1a11972)\]. The core idea of transfer learning is to leverage knowledge gained from one domain to improve learning performance in another, thereby reducing the need for extensive data-labeling efforts \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a25fbcbbae1e8f79c4360d26aa11a3abf1a11972)\]. This concept is closely related to other techniques such as domain adaptation, multitask learning, and covariate shift, which also aim to handle distributional differences between data sources \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a25fbcbbae1e8f79c4360d26aa11a3abf1a11972)\]. By transferring knowledge across domains, transfer learning enables more efficient and effective learning, especially when labeled data is scarce or expensive to obtain. The integration of structured data representations further enhances the potential of transfer learning, allowing for more nuanced modeling of relationships and attributes within complex data environments.
