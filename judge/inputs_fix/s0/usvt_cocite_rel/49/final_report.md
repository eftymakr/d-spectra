## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the domain of Web search. These models often rely on large-scale labeled query-document pairs for training, which are typically expensive to obtain \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09268)\]. However, the availability of Web document collections like ClueWeb22 has enabled the use of synthetic queries generated by large language models as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, synthetic training queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal downstream retrieval performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Existing methods typically address this issue by filtering out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach, while effective, introduces additional complexity and computational overhead. In contrast, our framework leverages Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to integrate ranking signals directly into the query generation process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. By doing so, we aim to optimize the model towards generating high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

DPO has been shown to be a stable, performant, and computationally lightweight method for aligning large language models with human preferences \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. It eliminates the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or significant hyperparameter tuning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. This is particularly advantageous in the context of query generation, where the goal is to produce high-quality synthetic queries that can be used to train retrieval models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align models with human preferences \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, requiring the fitting of a reward model and subsequent fine-tuning using reinforcement learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, DPO simplifies this process by introducing a new parameterization of the reward model that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. This results in a simpler implementation and training process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\].

Additionally, the use of pairwise preferences in training has been explored in the context of learning from human preferences \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. This approach bypasses the need for pointwise rewards and allows for direct optimization of the policy based on pairwise comparisons \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. This is particularly relevant in the context of query generation, where the goal is to produce queries that are preferred over others \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

In summary, the use of DPO in the query generation process offers a promising approach to improving the quality of synthetic queries used in training retrieval models. This approach not only simplifies the training process but also leads to better downstream performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 4 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a framework designed to improve the alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human preferences by simplifying the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) process. Unlike traditional RLHF, which requires training a reward model and then fine-tuning the LLM using reinforcement learning, DPO introduces a new parameterization of the reward model that allows for the extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, eliminating the need for complex sampling or hyperparameter tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach is more stable and computationally efficient, enabling the fine-tuning of LMs to align with human preferences as effectively as or better than existing methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been shown to outperform PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, DPO still relies on the assumption that pairwise preferences can be substituted with pointwise rewards, a key approximation that recent research has sought to address \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)\]. To mitigate potential pitfalls, DPO-Positive (DPOP) was introduced, which avoids the failure mode where the standard DPO loss reduces the likelihood of preferred examples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. DPOP has demonstrated superior performance across various datasets and tasks, including those with high edit distances between completions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. Additionally, methods like Direct Large Model Alignment (DLMA) have been proposed, which use contrastive prompt pairs to generate preference data and align LLMs without relying on human-annotated data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of DPO and its variants in improving the alignment and performance of LLMs.
