## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has emerged as a critical area within sentiment analysis, particularly in the context of user-generated content such as app reviews. While traditional sentiment analysis often focuses on broad polarity categories (positive, negative, neutral), recent research has emphasized the need for more nuanced emotion detection to better capture the complexity of human expression \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6352)\]. This is especially relevant in app reviews, where users frequently express a wide range of emotional responses to features, usability, and overall experience \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/030ea3f854d688659500a949eac095cdba087682)\].

Several studies have explored the creation of large-scale emotion datasets to facilitate this task. For instance, GoEmotions introduces a manually annotated dataset of 58k Reddit comments, labeled for 27 emotion categories, demonstrating the potential of fine-grained emotion classification \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Similarly, DENS presents a dataset of long-form narratives, annotated with multi-class emotion labels, and shows that fine-tuning pre-trained BERT models achieves competitive results \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11769)\]. These datasets highlight the importance of high-quality annotations and the challenges of generalizing across domains.

The development of emotion lexicons has also been a focus of research, with efforts to capture both basic and more complex emotions. CROWDSOURCING A WORD–EMOTION ASSOCIATION LEXICON demonstrates how crowdsourcing can be used to generate large, high-quality word-emotion association lexicons, addressing the limitations of small, manually curated lexicons \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. This approach has been further refined in PO-EMO, which focuses on aesthetic emotions in poetry and considers the reader’s emotional response rather than the author’s intent \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]. These works underscore the importance of context and perspective in emotion annotation.

In the domain of app reviews, the need for structured annotation frameworks has been emphasized. The paper under review adapts Plutchik’s emotion taxonomy to app reviews, developing a structured annotation framework and dataset \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. This aligns with the broader trend of using psychological theories to guide emotion classification, as seen in EmoBank, which distinguishes between writer’s and reader’s emotions and explores the mapping between dimensional and categorical emotion representations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01996)\]. Such approaches are essential for capturing the nuances of emotional expression in user feedback.

Automating emotion annotation has also been explored, with studies evaluating the performance of large language models in this task. The paper under review assesses the feasibility of automating emotion annotation using large language models, finding that while they significantly reduce manual effort, full automation remains challenging due to the complexity of emotional interpretation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. This aligns with findings from other studies, such as Harnessing Twitter "Big Data" for Automatic Emotion Identification, which highlights the limitations of automated systems in capturing the subtleties of emotional expression \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/773dd632f52355eb4f7cb61ae160a10e7acce001)\].

In summary, the related works highlight the growing interest in fine-grained emotion classification, the challenges of annotation, and the potential of automated approaches. These contributions provide a foundation for the current work, which aims to address the gaps in emotion analysis for app reviews.
