## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning general-purpose representations across various domains, including computer vision and natural language processing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. In the context of DNA pre-training, prior work has explored the use of pure sequence-based models, but these approaches often fail to capture the complex regulatory signals that govern DNA function, such as chromatin accessibility and histone modifications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more informative training signals that can better reflect the biological context of DNA sequences.

Recent advances in unsupervised learning have demonstrated that contrastive predictive coding can effectively learn useful representations from high-dimensional data by predicting future samples in latent space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Similarly, self-supervised learning methods such as SimCLR and MoCo have shown that learning from unlabeled data can yield representations that are competitive with or even surpass those obtained through supervised pre-training \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. These methods often rely on contrastive losses to encourage the model to distinguish between positive and negative samples, which can be applied to genomic data to learn meaningful representations \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\].

However, the use of pure sequence pre-training may be insufficient for capturing the full regulatory landscape of DNA, as genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation play a critical role in gene regulation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. To address this, supervised training for genomic profile prediction has been proposed as a more effective alternative, as it leverages labeled data to learn representations that are directly informed by these regulatory signals \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. This approach aligns with the idea that supervised learning can improve the quality of learned representations by incorporating explicit signals about the data \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06670)\].

To further enhance the learning of DNA representations, the SPACE model introduces a multi-species and multi-profile adaptive collaborative framework that leverages the Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)\]. This design allows the model to dynamically select the most relevant experts for different species and genomic profiles, thereby capturing the complex relationships between DNA sequences and their regulatory contexts \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)\]. The effectiveness of this approach is supported by prior work on collaborative learning, which has shown that combining multiple models can lead to improved performance in various tasks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)\].

In addition to these methods, other approaches such as context encoders and dense contrastive learning have also been explored for learning representations from unlabeled data \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07379)\]\[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09157)\]. These methods emphasize the importance of capturing spatial and contextual relationships within the data, which is crucial for tasks such as object detection and semantic segmentation \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07379)\]. The success of these methods underscores the value of incorporating both local and global information when learning representations from complex data.

In summary, while unsupervised pre-training has shown promise in learning general-purpose representations, supervised training for genomic profile prediction offers a more effective alternative for capturing the regulatory signals that govern DNA function. The SPACE model builds on these insights by introducing a novel framework that leverages the Mixture of Experts to better capture the relationships between DNA sequences across different species and genomic profiles \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)\].
