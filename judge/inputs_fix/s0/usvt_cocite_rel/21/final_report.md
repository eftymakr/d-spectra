## Related Works

Video frame interpolation has been a focal point in video processing research, with numerous methods proposed to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones. Traditional approaches typically involve two stages: motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, these methods are often limited by the accuracy of optical flow estimation and the computational complexity of synthesizing intermediate frames \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent advances have shifted toward end-to-end deep learning approaches that combine motion estimation and synthesis into a single process \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\].

Several works have explored the use of adaptive convolutional networks for video frame interpolation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\] introduced a method that considers pixel synthesis as a local convolution over two input frames, allowing the network to capture both motion and synthesis in a single step. This approach eliminates the need for explicit optical flow estimation and enables high-quality frame interpolation even in the presence of occlusion and blur \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\] proposed a method that uses separable convolutions to reduce the number of parameters required for kernel estimation, making the model more efficient while maintaining performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\].

Another line of research focuses on multi-frame interpolation, where multiple intermediate frames are generated at once. \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\] introduced a method that estimates bi-directional optical flow between input frames and uses this information to warp and blend the input frames, producing high-quality intermediate frames. However, this approach is limited by the need for accurate optical flow estimation and can produce artifacts in regions with complex motion \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12622)\]. To address this, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\] proposed a method that uses a U-Net architecture to refine the estimated optical flow and predict soft visibility maps, which helps to exclude occluded pixels from the interpolation process \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\].

Unsupervised learning has also been applied to video frame interpolation, particularly in scenarios where ground-truth data is scarce. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\] proposed a method that uses cycle consistency to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs, achieving results comparable to supervised methods without requiring explicit annotations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00627)\]. Additionally, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\] introduced a pseudo supervised loss term that enforces interpolated frames to be consistent with predictions of a pre-trained model, further improving performance on new target domains \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3a6e72172b7e1fb3b45f53e9aaf7d42e427c26de)\].

Recent methods have also focused on improving the efficiency and quality of video frame interpolation. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\] proposed a lightweight model that uses a cascaded network to first estimate coarse optical flow and then refine the interpolation using an image synthesis module. This approach allows for fine-scale corrections and produces high-quality intermediate frames with reduced computational cost \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\]. Similarly, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05534)\] introduced an adaptive warping module that estimates both kernel weights and offset vectors for each target pixel, enabling the model to handle complex motions more effectively \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06919)\].

In summary, while many methods have been proposed for video frame interpolation, most rely on optical flow estimation or require large computational resources. Recent advances have shifted toward end-to-end deep learning approaches that combine motion estimation and synthesis into a single process, improving both efficiency and quality \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08768)\].

The category "Unsupervised Learning in Video Interpolation" focuses on techniques that enable the synthesis of high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring explicit supervision or ground truth data. These methods leverage cycle consistency and temporal coherence inherent in real-world video sequences to improve interpolation quality. For example, one approach optimizes models to minimize the discrepancy between a center frame and its cycle reconstruction, achieved by interpolating back from intermediate frames, achieving results comparable to supervised methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Another method employs an analysis-by-synthesis strategy, where a convolutional neural network (CNN) is trained for frame interpolation in an unsupervised manner by exploiting temporal coherence, and then used to derive inter-frame correspondences by inverting the learned CNN \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. This approach not only enables unsupervised training but also achieves performance comparable to traditional methods. Additionally, pseudo supervised loss terms are introduced to further enhance model adaptation to new domains by enforcing consistency with pre-trained interpolation models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. These techniques demonstrate the potential of unsupervised learning to significantly improve video interpolation quality, particularly in scenarios where high frame rate training data is scarce \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\].
