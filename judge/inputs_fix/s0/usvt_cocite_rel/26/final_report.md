## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy protection in data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for data privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, especially in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Specifically, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This issue has been explored in several works that examine the security and robustness of DP protocols. 

Several studies have highlighted the vulnerabilities of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols. For instance, it has been shown that LDP is generally vulnerable to malicious data providers who can manipulate their output before sending it to the data collector, thereby achieving plausible deniability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. This manipulation can skew estimations and even allow attackers to control the results \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Similarly, data poisoning attacks have been demonstrated, where attackers inject fake users into an LDP protocol and send carefully crafted data to manipulate the output \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These attacks highlight the need for more robust mechanisms to ensure the integrity of DP protocols \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\].

To address these issues, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms to ensure that data providers follow the agreed randomization protocols \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These mechanisms allow the data collector to verify the completeness of the randomization process, thereby preventing output-manipulation attacks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Furthermore, interactive proofs for differential privacy have been introduced to ensure that the output is both differentially private and reliable \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. These proofs require the publishing entity to output a zero-knowledge proof that convinces an efficient verifier that the output is both DP and reliable \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. This approach helps close the loophole where malicious entities can use the randomness of DP as a smokescreen to mask their actions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\].

In addition to verifiability, the shuffled model has been proposed as a promising avenue for computing accurate statistics while keeping raw data in users' hands \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. This model lies between the local and central models of differential privacy, offering a balance between accuracy and trust assumptions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. The shuffled model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes a set of user-supplied messages, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the results while maintaining user privacy \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model has been shown to provide the power of the central model while avoiding the need to trust a central server \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency and accuracy of LDP protocols, particularly for multidimensional data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00782)\]. These protocols aim to reduce the noise variance and improve the accuracy of the results, which is crucial for applications such as machine learning \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00782)\]. Additionally, mechanisms for repeated collection of counter data have been developed, providing formal privacy guarantees even after long-term execution \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\].

The category "Verifiable Randomization Mechanisms for DP" focuses on addressing vulnerabilities in differential privacy (DP) protocols where malicious data providers can manipulate outputs to mislead data collectors. Traditional LDP mechanisms, such as randomized response, are susceptible to output-manipulation attacks because attackers can exploit the normalization process with predefined randomization probabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This allows them to skew estimations and maintain plausible deniability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. To counteract these threats, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms that enable data collectors to verify the correctness of randomization processes executed by data providers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These mechanisms ensure that the output adheres to the agreed-upon privacy guarantees, thereby preventing malicious alterations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, interactive proofs for DP have been introduced to address the issue of unfaithful execution by requiring the publishing entity to provide zero-knowledge proofs that confirm the output is both differentially private and reliable \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This approach combines private and public randomness to achieve verifiable DP counting queries with theoretical guarantees, making it practical for real-world deployment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. The integration of verifiable mechanisms into DP protocols enhances their robustness against adversarial behaviors, ensuring that the randomness introduced for privacy is not exploited as a smokescreen for malicious activities \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\].

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-vulnerabilities-in-distributed-settings'></a>Differential Privacy Vulnerabilities in Distributed Settings: 4 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) protocols, which enable privacy-preserving data analytics by perturbing user data before aggregation, have been extensively studied for their utility improvements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. However, their security remains a critical concern, particularly in distributed settings where adversaries can manipulate the system. Research has shown that attackers can inject fake users to distort the results of LDP protocols, leading to inaccurate frequency estimates or false heavy hitter identifications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. This vulnerability, termed data poisoning, highlights the need for robust countermeasures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, noninteractive LDP protocols are especially susceptible to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can obscure the true distribution of data, especially under high privacy levels or large domain sizes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These findings underscore the limitations of existing LDP mechanisms and the importance of developing more resilient protocols. While some studies have proposed optimized protocols that are robust to manipulation, their effectiveness varies across scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Furthermore, the use of continuous noise distributions in LDP introduces practical challenges, such as numerical inaccuracies and reduced interpretability, prompting the development of discrete Gaussian noise mechanisms that maintain privacy guarantees while improving usability \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. These insights collectively emphasize the ongoing challenges and evolving solutions in securing LDP protocols against adversarial attacks.

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-for-privacy-preserving-computation'></a>Shuffled Model for Privacy-Preserving Computation: 6 papers found.
The shuffled model for privacy-preserving computation has emerged as a promising framework that bridges the gap between the local and central differential privacy models, offering both improved accuracy and reduced trust assumptions \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Unlike the local model, where each user individually randomizes their data before sending it to a server, the shuffled model introduces an anonymous channel that permutes user messages, thereby preserving privacy while allowing for more efficient aggregation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model has been shown to achieve significantly better sample complexity than the local model, enabling accurate statistical estimation without requiring users to trust the server \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Moreover, recent work has demonstrated that shuffled protocols can achieve the same level of accuracy as central models, while still maintaining the privacy guarantees of local models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. The shuffled model is particularly relevant in distributed settings where users hold sensitive data and the goal is to compute aggregate statistics without revealing individual contributions \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. It has been applied to tasks such as histogram estimation, where error is independent of domain size, thereby offering a substantial advantage over local models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, the shuffled model has been analyzed in terms of its relationship to other privacy models, including the central and local models, and has been shown to offer a middle ground in terms of both privacy guarantees and computational efficiency \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model is especially useful in scenarios where a trusted server is not available, but users still wish to contribute to aggregate computations while maintaining privacy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\].

A key challenge in LDP is achieving high accuracy while maintaining privacy guarantees, especially for multidimensional data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00782)\]. Additionally, efforts have been made to extend LDP mechanisms to handle multidimensional data, including both numeric and categorical attributes, with improved noise variance compared to existing solutions \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00782)\]. Furthermore, techniques such as threshold estimation and truncation have been proposed to reduce noise in streaming data, enhancing the utility of LDP in real-time applications \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11753)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing research into improving the practicality and effectiveness of LDP in various privacy-sensitive scenarios.
