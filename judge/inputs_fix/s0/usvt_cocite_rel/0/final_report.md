## Related Works

Traditional approaches to tax policy design often rely on static models that fail to capture the complexity of taxpayer behavior and economic heterogeneity. For instance, the U.S. federal income tax system, while effective in reducing inequality, lacks adaptability in response to changing economic conditions \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a065b1629ff85e2a0db928bd8ce115b2f5e2a80f)\]. Similarly, models like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework, though dynamic, do not adequately address taxpayer heterogeneity or irrational decision-making \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. This limitation has prompted researchers to explore alternative methodologies, such as agent-based modeling (ABM), which can simulate heterogeneous agent behaviors and social interactions \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a065b1629ff85e2a0db928bd8ce115b2f5e2a80f)\].

Agent-based models have been widely used to study tax compliance and evasion. For example, the Ising model has been adapted to simulate tax evasion dynamics, incorporating social interactions and enforcement mechanisms \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/0806.0344)\]. These models allow for the simulation of how tax evasion may vary over time and how policy interventions, such as audits, can influence behavior \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. Furthermore, the majority-vote model has been applied to study tax evasion on various network structures, demonstrating the robustness of such models across different topologies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/0910.1964)\]. These studies highlight the importance of social and network structures in shaping tax compliance decisions.

Recent advances in computational methods have also enabled the integration of machine learning techniques into economic modeling. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has been proposed as a tool for solving complex macroeconomic models, allowing agents to learn optimal strategies through interaction with the environment \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. This approach has been applied to monetary policy design, where DRL agents outperform traditional adaptive learning methods in solving dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. Similarly, the use of large language models (LLMs) in conjunction with ABM has been proposed as a novel approach to designing adaptive tax policies \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. This integration allows for the simulation of heterogeneous taxpayer behaviors while enabling the government to iteratively optimize tax rates based on real-time data.

The use of multi-agent systems to model economic behavior has also gained traction, particularly in the context of tax credit distribution and household decision-making \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]. These models incorporate reinforcement learning agents of different types, allowing for the simulation of complex interactions within economic systems. The inclusion of government policy as a key actor in these models enables the analysis of how tax credits can be distributed to reduce disparities and improve social welfare \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\].

In summary, while traditional tax policy frameworks have limitations in capturing heterogeneity and irrational behavior, emerging methodologies such as agent-based modeling and deep reinforcement learning offer promising alternatives. These approaches enable the simulation of complex economic systems and the design of adaptive policies that balance equity and productivity \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\].

### <a id='category-agent-based-modeling-in-taxation'></a>Agent-Based Modeling in Taxation: 10 papers found.
Agent-based modeling in taxation has been extensively explored to understand and control tax evasion through mechanisms such as punishment and enforcement. Researchers have developed models inspired by the Ising model of statistical mechanics, where agents (taxpayers) exhibit behaviors influenced by social and institutional factors \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4900a633d3f9bc2e5a9adda2afb2f04f837d5f39)\]. These models incorporate external magnetic fields to represent trust in governmental institutions, which affects tax morale and the likelihood of evasion \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4900a633d3f9bc2e5a9adda2afb2f04f837d5f39)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that introducing audit mechanisms with probabilistic enforcement can effectively reduce evasion by altering agent behavior over time \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fba9c1bee4a5bda0d5dbf01f97ef5a8e212833e)\]. The use of Monte-Carlo simulations and network structures such as Barabasi-Albert and Voronoi-Delaunay lattices has allowed for the analysis of tax evasion dynamics across different topologies, revealing that the Zaklan model is robust regardless of dynamic or network structure \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e3d048b7b4d82a7d0908dd72710dadc7c71b0953)\]. Furthermore, multi-agent models have been proposed to simulate heterogeneous taxpayer behaviors and incorporate reinforcement learning to optimize tax policies, addressing limitations in traditional models that fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16977)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the potential of agent-based modeling to inform policy design and improve fiscal outcomes through adaptive and data-driven strategies.

### <a id='category-deep-reinforcement-learning-applications'></a>Deep Reinforcement Learning Applications: 3 papers found.
The use of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) in macroeconomic modeling has gained traction as a flexible and principled approach to incorporate heterogeneous agents and bounded rationality into dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]. Unlike traditional methods that struggle with agent heterogeneity and stochasticity, DRL enables agents to learn optimal behaviors through interaction with the model environment, without prior knowledge of its structure \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to classical macroeconomic models, such as those involving monetary and fiscal policy interactions, where it outperforms adaptive learning methods by solving the model across various policy regimes \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]. Additionally, DRL has been used to compute optimal interest rate reaction functions, incorporating constraints like the zero lower bound and nonlinear economic structures, with results showing significant improvements in policy performance compared to traditional rules \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fba9c1bee4a5bda0d5dbf01f97ef5a8e212833e)\]. The method's versatility is further demonstrated in combined macroeconomic and epidemiological models, where it captures differential health risks by age and simulates realistic economic behaviors during pandemics \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. These applications highlight the potential of DRL to enhance the accuracy, flexibility, and computational efficiency of macroeconomic models, particularly in addressing complex real-world challenges such as economic inequality and heterogeneous agent behavior \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]\[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]\[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fba9c1bee4a5bda0d5dbf01f97ef5a8e212833e)\].
