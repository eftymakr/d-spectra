## Related Works

Dense retrievers have gained significant traction in information retrieval due to their ability to encode text into dense vector representations that can be efficiently compared using shallow similarity operations such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These models typically rely on pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) that are fine-tuned using contrastive learning to align queries with relevant documents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. However, recent research has questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may not significantly alter the underlying knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\].

This debate has primarily focused on BERT-based encoders using the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative dense retriever \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. The current study extends this analysis by testing different representation approaches, such as CLS token versus mean pooling, and different backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Additionally, we evaluate performance across multiple datasets, including MSMARCO and Natural Questions, to generalize findings beyond the original BERT-based setup \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

Our results confirm that in DPR tuning, pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance, with fine-tuning primarily adjusting neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. However, this pattern does not hold universally. For example, in mean-pooled models like Contriever, and in decoder-based models like LLaMA, fine-tuning plays a more significant role in shaping retrieval performance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\]. This suggests that the effectiveness of fine-tuning may depend on the architecture and representation strategy employed \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\].

Several prior works have explored the training of dense retrievers. For instance, the Condenser architecture was introduced to improve dense encoders by pre-training with a novel Transformer design that conditions on dense representations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. Similarly, the coCondenser model adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of pre-training strategies in achieving effective dense retrieval \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13602)\].

Another line of research focuses on contrastive learning for dense retrieval, such as the ANCE model, which constructs negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index of the corpus to improve training efficiency \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. Similarly, the RocketQA model introduces techniques like cross-batch negatives and denoised hard negatives to enhance retrieval performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00300)\]. These methods demonstrate that training strategies significantly impact the effectiveness of dense retrievers \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

In summary, while pre-training plays a foundational role in dense retrievers, the extent to which fine-tuning contributes to retrieval performance varies depending on the model architecture and representation method \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-and-training-methods'></a>Contrastive Learning and Training Methods: 10 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a critical approach in training dense retrievers, enabling models to learn effective text representations without the need for extensive labeled data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This technique leverages self-supervised objectives to align queries and passages in a shared embedding space, improving retrieval performance by emphasizing discriminative features \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of contrastive learning in overcoming the limitations of traditional sparse retrieval methods, which rely on term frequency statistics and are less effective in capturing semantic relationships \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. For instance, coCondenser introduces an unsupervised contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the reliance on heavy data engineering and large batch training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, ANCE constructs more realistic negative training instances by using an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, thereby resolving the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These approaches demonstrate that contrastive learning can significantly enhance dense retrieval performance, as evidenced by improvements in benchmarks such as MS-MARCO and BEIR \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. Furthermore, the effectiveness of contrastive learning is further supported by studies showing that it can lead to strong performance in various retrieval settings, even when trained on limited or unlabelled data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. The integration of contrastive learning with pre-trained language models, such as BERT and LLaMA, has also been explored, with results indicating that pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance, while fine-tuning primarily adjusts neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Overall, these findings underscore the potential of contrastive learning in advancing the field of dense retrieval.

### <a id='category-pre-training-strategies-and-their-impact'></a>Pre-training Strategies and Their Impact: 12 papers found.
The category "Pre-training Strategies and Their Impact" encompasses a range of studies that explore how pre-training methods influence the performance and capabilities of language models, particularly in tasks such as question answering, information retrieval, and dense retrieval. These studies highlight the significance of pre-training in capturing world knowledge and improving model performance, while also addressing challenges such as computational costs, hyperparameter sensitivity, and the effectiveness of different pre-training approaches. For example, BERT, introduced as a bidirectional encoder, demonstrated state-of-the-art results on multiple NLP tasks by jointly conditioning on left and right context \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Similarly, models like DistilBERT show that smaller, more efficient models can be pre-trained to retain most of the performance of larger models, making them suitable for on-device deployment \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\]. Meanwhile, research on dense retrievers, such as DPR and Spider, emphasizes the role of pre-training in encoding text into meaningful representations for retrieval tasks, with some studies arguing that retrieval knowledge is primarily gained during pre-training rather than fine-tuning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. These findings underscore the importance of thoughtful pre-training strategies in shaping the final capabilities of language models across various applications.

Recent studies have explored the effectiveness of fine-tuning in enhancing pre-trained language models for downstream tasks, with a particular focus on knowledge-intensive tasks where models struggle to access and manipulate information effectively \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. This issue has been addressed through the development of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) models, which combine parametric and non-parametric memory to improve factual accuracy and diversity in generated outputs \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. These models leverage pre-trained sequence-to-sequence models as parametric memory and dense vector indexes of external knowledge sources, such as Wikipedia, accessed via neural retrievers \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. In contrast, other approaches like Universal Language Model Fine-tuning (ULMFiT) have focused on improving transfer learning in NLP by applying task-agnostic fine-tuning strategies that significantly outperform traditional methods, especially when training data is limited \[[29](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1e077413b25c4d34945cc2707e17e46ed4fe784a)\]. ULMFiT demonstrates that fine-tuning can achieve competitive performance with minimal labeled examples, reducing the need for extensive training data \[[29](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1e077413b25c4d34945cc2707e17e46ed4fe784a)\]. These findings highlight the importance of fine-tuning in adapting pre-trained models to specific tasks while also emphasizing the limitations of relying solely on pre-training for knowledge acquisition \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\].
