## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely recognized as a de facto standard for ensuring data privacy in statistical databases \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3946)\]. However, its implementation in distributed settings is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, such as sampling noise from incorrect distributions or generating correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15091)\]. This issue has been addressed in several works. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\] highlights the vulnerability of Local Differential Privacy (LDP) to malicious clients who can manipulate inputs or outputs to skew results. To mitigate this, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] proposes verifiable randomization mechanisms that allow data collectors to verify the correctness of randomization applied by data providers, preventing output manipulation attacks. Similarly, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20926)\] introduces a verifiable LDP mechanism that prevents both input and output manipulation attacks, requiring only one-time interaction between client and server. These works align with our focus on ensuring verifiability in distributed DP systems.

Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) has also been explored in the context of secure multi-party computation (MPC) and distributed settings. \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\] introduces interactive proofs for differential privacy, requiring a publisher to provide a zero-knowledge proof that validates the correctness of DP outputs. This approach ensures that the randomness used in DP is faithfully generated without revealing it. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10209)\] extends this idea by proposing verifiable randomized response mechanisms that allow for the verification of the correctness of randomized responses, enhancing the reliability of DP outputs in distributed environments. These approaches are closely related to our proposed Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP) framework, which incorporates verification mechanisms to ensure the correct execution of DP protocols.

In the context of verifiable LDP, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1212.1186)\] presents a mechanism that allows for the verification of the correctness of DP outputs while maintaining high utility. This is achieved through the use of zero-knowledge proofs that validate the randomness used in the DP process. Similarly, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17523)\] introduces a mechanism that applies randomization to indexes rather than values, ensuring rigorous LDP guarantees while preserving utility. These methods are relevant to our work as they demonstrate the feasibility of verifiable DP mechanisms that balance privacy and utility.

The use of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) in DP has also been explored in the context of secure machine learning. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14848)\] presents a comprehensive survey on verifiable decentralized machine learning using ZKPs, highlighting the role of ZKPs in ensuring the integrity of computation results shared by participants. This aligns with our exploration of the relationship between ZKPs and DP, demonstrating that while ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary. This finding is crucial for our work, as it allows us to design efficient verification mechanisms that do not rely on ZKPs.

In summary, the related works provide a foundation for our proposed VDDP framework, highlighting the importance of verification mechanisms in ensuring the correct execution of DP protocols in distributed settings. These works also demonstrate the feasibility of verifiable DP mechanisms that balance privacy and utility, which is a key focus of our research.

These studies highlight the trade-offs between accuracy and privacy guarantees, showing that different algorithms perform better under varying privacy regimes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12621)\]. For instance, the Basic RAPPOR algorithm is noted for its superior performance in high privacy settings, whereas the k-RR algorithm excels in low privacy scenarios \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12621)\]. In the medium privacy regime, the k-RR, k-subset, and HR algorithms demonstrate competitive performance, often outperforming other methods \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12621)\]. These findings underscore the importance of selecting appropriate algorithms based on the specific privacy requirements and data characteristics, contributing to the ongoing development of LDP mechanisms.
