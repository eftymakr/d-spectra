## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency compared to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike NTP, which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly for large models, by enabling more efficient and effective training strategies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) have struggled to fully leverage the benefits of MTP, highlighting the need for specialized training strategies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\].

Curriculum learning has been explored as a means to address these challenges by gradually increasing the complexity of the training objective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. In this work, we propose two variants of curriculum learning for MTP: a forward curriculum, which transitions from NTP to MTP, and a reverse curriculum, which does the opposite \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. The forward curriculum enables SLMs to better utilize the MTP objective during pre-training, leading to improved downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. In contrast, the reverse curriculum achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\].

Prior research has also investigated the effectiveness of curriculum learning in improving language model performance. While some studies have found no significant improvement, others have demonstrated that curriculum learning can enhance model performance, particularly in specific domains such as code generation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.02170)\]. For instance, curriculum learning has been shown to improve the accuracy of small decoder-only code language models on code execution tasks, although its impact on code completion is less pronounced \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\]. These findings suggest that curriculum learning can be a valuable tool for improving the performance of SLMs in various tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\].

Additionally, multi-token prediction has been explored in the context of vision-language pre-training, where it has been used to improve the representation of multi-modal data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09061)\]. However, the application of MTP to code language models remains an active area of research, with recent work focusing on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of multi-token prediction for code generation tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. These studies highlight the potential of MTP to enhance the capabilities of language models across a wide range of applications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

In summary, while MTP offers promising benefits for large models, its application to smaller models requires careful consideration of training strategies such as curriculum learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Future work should explore how these strategies can be further refined to maximize the benefits of MTP across different model sizes and tasks.
