## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has garnered significant attention in recent years, driven by the increasing use of recommendation systems in socially impactful domains such as employment and housing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. Early work primarily focused on balancing recommendation accuracy with fairness metrics, often considering a single sensitive attribute like race or gender \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. However, these approaches largely ignored individual preferences and the complexity of multi-aspect fairness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. More recent efforts have aimed to incorporate personalized fairness considerations, such as user tolerance for diversity in recommendations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. For instance, the Fairness-Aware Re-ranking (FAR) algorithm balances accuracy and provider-side fairness while considering user preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Similarly, the CPFair method integrates both consumer and producer fairness in a joint optimization framework, demonstrating that it is possible to improve fairness without sacrificing recommendation quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].

The concept of fairness in re-ranking has also been explored through the lens of economic principles, such as taxation and resource allocation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. The Tax-rank method, for example, introduces a new fair re-ranking approach that leverages the difference in utility between items to levy taxes, ensuring both continuity and controllability over accuracy loss \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. This aligns with the idea that fairness considerations in re-ranking can be likened to a commodity tax on suppliers, ultimately translating into a cost passed on to consumers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. The accuracy-fairness trade-off is further formalized using the notion of elasticity, similar to how the extent of tax transfer is determined in economics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. This insight highlights the limitations of current fair re-ranking evaluations, which often rely solely on a single fairness metric, hindering a comprehensive assessment of algorithm performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].

Several works have proposed fairness-aware re-ranking methods that incorporate diversity and individual preferences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03577)\]. The Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) re-ranking method has been extended to include fairness-aware representations of demographic groups, allowing for the incorporation of fairness while maintaining high precision \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03577)\]. Additionally, the BankFair method addresses the challenge of balancing user accuracy and provider fairness under fluctuating user traffic, drawing inspiration from bankruptcy theory \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120)\]. This method uses the Talmud rule to determine fairness degrees and ensures consistent user service across different traffic conditions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120)\]. Furthermore, the BankFair+ method extends this approach by introducing a non-linear function from regret theory to enhance individual fairness while maintaining user accuracy \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14550)\].

In summary, the field of fair re-ranking has evolved from a focus on single fairness metrics to more sophisticated approaches that consider multi-aspect fairness, individual preferences, and economic principles. These developments underscore the importance of a comprehensive evaluation framework that accounts for both accuracy and fairness across different elasticity levels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].
