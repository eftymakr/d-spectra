## Related Works

Protein dynamics are essential for understanding biological functions and properties, yet traditional methods for studying them rely on computationally expensive molecular dynamics (MD) simulations \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/0811.3094)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling have introduced more efficient approaches for protein structure prediction and conformation sampling, particularly through denoising diffusion models \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. These models learn distributions over crystallographic structures, enabling the generation of high-quality protein conformations without the need for extensive simulation data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. However, integrating physical principles into these data-driven models remains a challenge, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization problems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\].

Energy-based models (EBMs) have been explored for modeling protein conformations at the atomic scale, trained solely on crystallized protein data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. These models capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy and achieve performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods like Rosetta \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Despite their success, they lack the ability to model dynamic ensembles of protein conformations, which are crucial for understanding biological functions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17196)\].

To address this, several studies have proposed generative models that can predict structural ensembles of proteins. P2DFlow, for example, is a generative model based on SE(3) flow matching that successfully captures dynamic fluctuations observed in crystal structures and MD simulations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17196)\]. Similarly, ConfDiff introduces a force-guided SE(3) diffusion model that generates protein conformations with high fidelity while preserving diversity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. These models incorporate physical prior knowledge to guide the generation process, improving the accuracy of sampled conformations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\].

Another approach, EigenFold, uses a diffusion process inspired by the natural folding of proteins, modeling the structure as a system of harmonic oscillators \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. This method provides a more comprehensive picture of model uncertainty by generating an ensemble of structures, offering insights into conformational heterogeneity \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Similarly, the Structure Language Model (SLM) framework introduces a novel approach for efficient protein conformation generation by encoding structures into a latent space and applying conditional language modeling \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403)\].

The integration of physical supervision into generative models has also been explored through methods like the one proposed in this paper, which introduces Energy-based Alignment (EBA). EBA aligns generative models with feedback from physical models, efficiently calibrating them to balance conformational states based on energy differences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. This approach improves the physical plausibility of generated structures, enhancing model predictions and enabling applications in structural biology and drug discovery \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\].

In addition to these methods, other studies have focused on enhancing sampling efficiency for protein conformational dynamics. For instance, the use of anharmonic low-frequency vibrations has been shown to encode the necessary information for predicting conformational transitions without requiring prior knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08154)\]. This information can be extracted from short dynamics simulations and combined with enhanced sampling to generate accurate conformational ensembles \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08154)\].

Finally, the use of protein language models has also been explored for generating protein sequences and structures. DiMA, for example, operates on protein language model representations and achieves high performance across multiple encoders and representations \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03726)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of combining language modeling with diffusion techniques to generate diverse and high-quality protein sequences \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03726)\].
