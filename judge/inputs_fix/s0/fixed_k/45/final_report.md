## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification in app reviews has received increasing attention in recent years due to its potential to extract nuanced user sentiment and improve app development strategies. While sentiment analysis typically focuses on broad categories such as positive, negative, and neutral \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\], more recent work has explored the classification of emotions into finer-grained categories to better capture user sentiment \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09739)\]. For instance, the GoEmotions dataset, which contains 58,000 manually annotated Reddit comments, provides a rich resource for fine-grained emotion classification with 27 emotion categories \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. This dataset has been used to evaluate the performance of various models, including BERT-based models, which achieved an average F1-score of 0.46 across the proposed taxonomy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\].

Several studies have also focused on the challenges of manual annotation and the potential of using large language models (LLMs) to automate the process. Human annotations, while valuable, are expensive to obtain and subject to variability due to the subjective nature of emotions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. GPT-4 has shown promise in emotion annotation tasks, achieving high ratings in human evaluation studies and demonstrating a positive correlation with human labels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. However, differences between human and LLM annotations highlight the need for human input in annotation studies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. This is particularly relevant in the context of app reviews, where emotional sentiment can be complex and context-dependent \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\].

Automated feature extraction from app reviews has also been explored using encoder-only models based on the Transformer architecture. These models have shown promising results in improving the precision and recall of extracted features, especially when fine-tuned with crowdsourced annotations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01063)\]. Additionally, the use of transfer learning has been investigated to improve performance across different emotion label sets and domains \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12135)\]. This approach has been applied to the GoEmotions dataset, where models outperformed existing state-of-the-art methods \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12135)\].

The integration of emotion definitions into emotion prediction frameworks has also been explored to improve the accuracy of fine-grained emotion classification. By modeling emotion definitions as an auxiliary task during training, models can better capture the nuances of emotional expressions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12135)\]. This approach has been shown to improve performance on transfer learning tasks, demonstrating the generalization capability of the models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12135)\].

In the context of app reviews, several studies have focused on the challenges of manual annotation and the potential of using LLMs to automate the process. For example, the work by \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\] highlights the limitations of human annotations and the potential of using GPT-4 to reduce manual effort while maintaining agreement with human labels. Similarly, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01063)\] explores the use of encoder-only models for feature extraction from app reviews, showing improvements in precision and recall when fine-tuned with crowdsourced data.

In summary, while sentiment analysis has traditionally focused on broad categories, recent work has explored the classification of emotions into finer-grained categories to better capture user sentiment. The use of LLMs and encoder-only models has shown promise in automating the annotation process, although challenges remain in capturing the complexity of emotional interpretation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\].
