## Related Works

Representation learning is a cornerstone of modern recommender systems, enabling efficient user-item matching through vectorized embeddings \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04163)\]. However, the computational and memory costs of these embeddings can be prohibitive for real-time applications. To address this, binarization techniques have emerged as a promising approach, converting dense embeddings into compact binary representations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. This strategy not only reduces memory usage but also accelerates inference, making it suitable for resource-constrained environments \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01600)\]. 

Existing binarization methods primarily focus on numerical quantization, often at the expense of information retention \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01600)\]. This leads to performance degradation, particularly in collaborative filtering tasks where high-order interactions between users and items are crucial \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. To mitigate this, recent works have explored graph-based approaches that integrate user-item interactions into the embedding process \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. For example, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) explicitly models high-order connectivity by propagating embeddings through a bipartite graph, enhancing the collaborative signal \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) integrates user-user and item-item graphs alongside the user-item graph to capture more comprehensive relational patterns \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These methods demonstrate that leveraging graph structures can significantly improve the quality of embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\].

Another line of research focuses on the efficiency of recommendation models. Binarized Collaborative Filtering with Distilling Graph Convolutional Networks (Binarized GCN) introduces a framework that distills ranking information from a GCN into a binarized model, improving efficiency while preserving performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. This approach converts binary constrained optimization into a continuous problem, allowing for easier optimization using standard solvers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Similarly, Graph-less Collaborative Filtering (SimRec) addresses the limitations of graph neural networks (GNNs), such as over-smoothing and poor scalability, by combining knowledge distillation and contrastive learning \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\]. These techniques highlight the potential of hybrid approaches that balance model efficiency with recommendation accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\].

In addition to efficiency, fairness in recommendation systems has gained attention. Fair Augmentation for Graph Collaborative Filtering (FA4GCF) introduces a method to enhance fairness by learning a fair graph augmentation, ensuring equitable treatment of users \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12208)\]. This work underscores the growing need to address fairness concerns in graph-based collaborative filtering \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12208)\].

Finally, the integration of large language models (LLMs) with graph structure learning models has shown promise in robust representation learning \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12096)\]. LangGSL combines LLMs for noise filtering with graph structure learning models to jointly enhance node features and graph structures \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12096)\]. This synergy highlights the potential of combining different learning paradigms to achieve more robust and interpretable representations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12096)\].

Graph-based collaborative filtering approaches have been increasingly explored to enhance recommendation systems by leveraging the structural information embedded in user-item interaction data. These methods aim to capture high-order connectivity and collaborative signals that are often overlooked in traditional matrix factorization or deep learning-based models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item bipartite graphs into the embedding process, enabling explicit modeling of collaborative filtering effects through embedding propagation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) extends this idea by incorporating user-user and item-item graphs alongside the user-item graph, allowing for more comprehensive modeling of proximal and high-order interactions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. The importance of graph convolution in capturing nuanced relationships is further emphasized by models like SimRec, which addresses over-smoothing issues in GNN-based approaches through knowledge distillation and contrastive learning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Additionally, Weighted Graph-based Collaborative Filtering (W-GCF) and Attentive Graph-based Collaborative Filtering (A-GCF) refine implicit feedback handling by assigning dynamic weights to items based on user interactions, improving the accuracy of preference prediction \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13195)\]. Spectral graph wavelet methods also contribute by representing users and items in a bipartite graph and learning low-dimensional embeddings that capture both local and global structures, enhancing recommendation quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the potential of graph-based methods to better model collaborative signals and improve recommendation performance by explicitly incorporating structural information into the learning process.
