## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommendation systems, with recent advancements leveraging graph-based models to better capture user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Early CF methods, such as matrix factorization, relied on explicit user-item ratings, while later approaches incorporated deep learning and graph neural networks (GNNs) to model complex relationships \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for CF, enabling the aggregation of neighborhood information to generate more informative embeddings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. However, these models often suffer from issues such as over-smoothing and limited expressiveness due to their linear or fixed-frequency filtering mechanisms \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Several works have attempted to address these limitations. For instance, NGCF integrates user-item interactions directly into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph, thereby capturing high-order connectivity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03316)\]. Similarly, Multi-GCCF explicitly incorporates multiple graphs (user-user, item-item, and user-item) to model richer interaction patterns \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04384)\]. RGCF improves upon traditional GCN-based models by introducing a refined graph convolution structure and a fine-tuned normalization to better encode popularity features \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13996)\]. These methods, however, still rely on linear or fixed-frequency filters, which may not fully exploit the spectrum of graph signals \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\].

To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored more flexible graph filters. PolyCF proposes a polynomial-based graph signal filter that captures spectral features across multiple eigenspaces, enabling the recovery of missing interactions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10699)\]. The work in \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14748)\] further extends this idea by designing universal IIR graph filters using Butterworth, Chebyshev, and elliptic functions, achieving more accurate frequency responses than traditional polynomial-based filters \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04755)\]. AutoGCN introduces an automatic graph convolutional filter that dynamically adjusts its bandwidth, capturing both low and high-frequency components of graph signals \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14734)\]. These methods highlight the importance of spectral filtering in improving the expressiveness and robustness of CF models.

Despite these advancements, many existing models still face challenges in handling large-scale and sparse data. For example, the work in \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16888)\] introduces a residual network structure to alleviate over-smoothing in GCN-based CF models, while \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05962)\] proposes a linear residual GCN to improve scalability and efficiency. Additionally, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01376)\] investigates the use of graph filters with adaptive propagation, allowing each node to independently decide the number of communication steps, thereby improving model performance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01378)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of adaptive and flexible graph filtering in enhancing CF performance.

In summary, while existing methods have made significant strides in modeling user-item interactions using graph-based approaches, there remains a need for more expressive and adaptive graph filters that can better capture the full spectrum of graph signals. Our proposed ChebyCF framework builds on these insights by leveraging Chebyshev interpolation and ideal pass filters to achieve more accurate and flexible graph spectral filtering \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10435)\].

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-in-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Neural Networks in Collaborative Filtering: 15 papers found.
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommender systems, offering novel ways to model complex user-item interactions through graph structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Traditional approaches often rely on matrix factorization or deep learning models that fail to fully exploit the collaborative signal embedded in user-item interactions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. In contrast, GNN-based methods, such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\], explicitly incorporate the user-item bipartite graph structure to propagate embeddings and capture high-order connectivity, leading to more expressive and accurate recommendations. However, existing GCN-based models suffer from issues like over-smoothing, information redundancy, and limited ability to model non-semantic ID inputs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed refined graph convolution structures, such as in Refined Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (RGCF) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\], which incorporate symmetric normalization to better encode node popularity and improve embedding quality. Additionally, methods like Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\] and Multi-Component graph convolutional Collaborative Filtering (MCCF) \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\] have been introduced to integrate multiple graphs and decompose latent purchasing motivations, respectively, enhancing the model's ability to capture fine-grained user preferences. Furthermore, recent works like Simple Contrastive Collaborative Filtering (SCCF) \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04384)\] and Wasserstein dependent Graph ATtention network (W-GAT) \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05962)\] have explored contrastive learning and uncertainty modeling to improve robustness and recommendation accuracy. Despite these advancements, challenges remain, such as training difficulties with non-linear activations and the need for more efficient and scalable models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13996)\]. Overall, the integration of GNNs into CF has significantly advanced the field, offering promising directions for future research.

### <a id='category-adaptive-graph-filtering-approaches'></a>Adaptive Graph Filtering Approaches: 5 papers found.
Adaptive graph filtering approaches have been increasingly explored to enhance the performance and efficiency of graph-based recommendation systems and neural networks. These methods aim to overcome limitations such as fixed bandwidth filters and the inability to capture full spectral information, which are common in traditional graph convolutional networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. For instance, existing models often act as low-pass filters, neglecting middle and high-frequency components of graph signals \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04755)\], while others enforce a uniform neighborhood size, which may not align with the true data structure \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03316)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed techniques like Automatic Graph Convolutional Networks (AutoGCN), which dynamically adjust the bandwidth of graph filters to capture the full spectrum of signals while maintaining spatial localization \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04755)\]. Similarly, methods such as ChebyCF leverage spectral graph theory to utilize the full spectrum of user interaction history and apply flexible non-linear filters through Chebyshev interpolation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Additionally, decentralized frameworks have been introduced to compute graph-based filters securely without centralizing sensitive user data, ensuring privacy and reducing communication costs \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16888)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in adaptive and interpretable graph filtering mechanisms that balance performance, efficiency, and privacy in modern recommendation systems and graph neural networks.

### <a id='category-non-linear-graph-filtering-techniques'></a>Non-Linear Graph Filtering Techniques: 3 papers found.
In the realm of non-linear graph filtering techniques, recent research has focused on improving the accuracy and efficiency of graph filter design. For instance, the use of Butterworth, Chebyshev, and elliptic functions has been explored to create universal IIR graph filters with low computational complexity \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14748)\]. These filters are designed by deriving zeros and poles from graph frequency responses and constructing conjugate filters to achieve high-pass, low-pass, band-pass, and band-stop responses \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14748)\]. Additionally, graph-based image enhancement techniques have leveraged Chebyshev polynomials and conjugate gradient iterations to perform spectral denoising, resulting in improved image quality with higher PSNR values compared to existing polynomial graph filters \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.01624)\]. Similarly, the development of Chebyshev wavelets, derived from Chebyshev polynomials, has enabled efficient signal denoising with compact support and good selectivity, although they lack orthogonality \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.2389)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in utilizing non-linear graph filtering methods to address complex signal processing tasks, offering more flexible and accurate solutions than traditional linear approaches.

### <a id='category-user-item-interaction-modeling'></a>User-Item Interaction Modeling: 3 papers found.
In the realm of user-item interaction modeling, recent advancements have focused on integrating deep learning techniques to enhance collaborative filtering (CF) systems. Traditional CF methods face challenges in scalability and capturing complex user-item relationships, prompting the exploration of deep neural networks (DNNs) such as Multilayer Perceptrons (MLP), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), and Graph Neural Networks (GNN) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01378)\]. These models offer greater flexibility in modeling non-linear interactions, with CNNs being particularly effective in extracting local features from user and item embeddings \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01376)\]. However, many existing systems struggle with handling missing data, often relying on simplistic negative sampling strategies that degrade recommendation performance \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03158)\]. To address this, novel approaches like the Collaborative Reflection-Augmented Autoencoder Network (CRANet) have been introduced, which leverages both observed and unobserved interactions to encode implicit user preferences more effectively \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03158)\]. Similarly, the Convolutional Transformer Neural Collaborative Filtering (CTNCF) model integrates CNNs and Transformer layers to capture both local and long-range dependencies, enhancing the ability to understand intricate interaction patterns \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01376)\]. These developments highlight a growing trend toward more sophisticated architectures that better align with the complexities of real-world recommendation tasks.

### <a id='category-graph-convolutional-network-variants'></a>Graph Convolutional Network Variants: 4 papers found.
Document 1 discusses the evolution of graph convolutional networks (GCNs), emphasizing the extension of graph convolution ideas to introduce a hyper-parameter that influences the receptive field of the filter \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10435)\]. This approach aims to improve the predictive performance of Deep Graph Convolutional Networks by adapting the filter size. Document 2 highlights the role of graph convolutional filters in GNNs, noting their properties of permutation equivariance and stability to topological changes, which contribute to the scalability and transferability of GNNs \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03777)\]. It also introduces extensions using edge-varying and autoregressive moving average (ARMA) graph filters, expanding the capabilities of GNNs in modeling complex network structures. Document 3 presents a novel graph convolutional layer inspired by ARMA filters, which offers a more flexible frequency response and better captures the global graph structure compared to polynomial-based filters \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.01343)\]. This layer is efficient to train, localized in node space, and transferable to new graphs, showing significant improvements over existing polynomial filter-based GNNs. Document 4 addresses challenges in GCNNs, such as over-smoothing and over-fitting, and proposes a method that incorporates node transition probabilities for more effective feature aggregation. It also introduces DropNode, a regularization technique that mitigates over-smoothing and over-fitting by randomly discarding parts of the graph, leading to improved performance on benchmark datasets \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12578)\]. These works collectively highlight the ongoing advancements in graph convolutional methods, focusing on improving filter design, addressing over-smoothing, and enhancing the flexibility and performance of GNNs in various applications.
