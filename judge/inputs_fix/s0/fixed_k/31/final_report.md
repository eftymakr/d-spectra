## Related Works

Recent advances in deep learning have led to the application of unsupervised pre-training paradigms to DNA sequence modeling, aiming to capture generalizable representations from pure DNA sequences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. However, these approaches are limited in their ability to fully capture the regulatory and functional context of DNA, as genomic functions are influenced by additional factors such as chromatin accessibility and other genomic profiles \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. This limitation has motivated the exploration of supervised pre-training methods that incorporate genomic profiles to improve the accuracy and robustness of DNA representation learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\].

Several studies have investigated the use of supervised learning for genomic profile prediction. For example, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08663)\] demonstrated the effectiveness of machine learning systems in predicting DNA-binding proteins by leveraging features such as overall charge, patch size, and amino acids composition. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\] introduced a method for predicting DNA methylation levels using features including neighboring CpG site methylation levels, genomic distance, and regulatory elements. These works highlight the importance of incorporating genomic context in DNA modeling, as pure sequence-based approaches may lack the necessary information to capture functional relationships \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\].

The integration of multiple genomic profiles and species has also been explored in recent research. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05415)\] proposed a supervised learning model called DNA-GAN, which disentangles different factors of variation in images, and \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\] introduced a self-supervised contrastive pre-training method for dense land cover classification, demonstrating the benefits of leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data. These approaches, while not directly focused on DNA, share common themes of utilizing structured data and multi-modal information to improve model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\].

In the context of genomic representation learning, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.05870)\] introduced a self-supervised pre-training method for 3D point cloud networks, combining image and point cloud modalities to reduce the need for manual annotations. This approach aligns with the idea of using auxiliary information to enhance model performance, a principle that is also central to the SPACE framework \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\].

Additionally, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08443)\] developed a method for predicting DNA methylation levels at single-CpG-site precision, achieving high accuracy by incorporating features such as CpG islands and regulatory elements. This work, along with \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\], which demonstrated the use of deep learning for classifying genomic sequences, underscores the importance of integrating diverse genomic features to improve predictive accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\].

The SPACE framework builds upon these insights by introducing a multi-species and multi-profile adaptive model that leverages the Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture to better capture the relationships between DNA sequences across different species and genomic profiles \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. This approach is supported by prior research on the benefits of using supervised training for genomic profile prediction, as well as the advantages of incorporating multiple modalities and data sources to enhance model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\].

### <a id='category-genomic-sequence-classification-methods'></a>Genomic Sequence Classification Methods: 3 papers found.
Genomic sequence classification methods have evolved significantly, with recent approaches leveraging computational techniques to overcome limitations of traditional experimental methods. For instance, nucleic acid reassociation kinetics, while historically used to determine prokaryotic genome relatedness, are technically challenging and not cost-effective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. In response, researchers have developed in silico pipelines that predict DNA-DNA hybridization values using whole-genome sequence comparisons, offering a more efficient and accurate alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. Additionally, deep learning frameworks have been applied to classify genomic sequences, such as in the case of Deep Motif (DeMo), which uses a deep convolutional/highway MLP to identify transcription factor binding sites and extract biologically meaningful motifs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1605.01133)\]. These motifs are not only comparable to existing ones but in some cases outperform them \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1605.01133)\]. Furthermore, machine learning models, including Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Cascade Correlation Neural Networks (CCNN), have been employed to predict DNA-binding proteins by analyzing features such as charge, amino acid composition, and structural characteristics \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1207.2600)\]. These models achieve high accuracy, with SVM demonstrating an accuracy of 86.7% and CCNN achieving 75.4% \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1207.2600)\]. Such advancements highlight the growing reliance on computational methods to enhance the precision and scalability of genomic sequence classification.

These approaches highlight the versatility of self-supervised pre-training across diverse domains, enabling models to generalize better and perform more efficiently with limited labeled data.
