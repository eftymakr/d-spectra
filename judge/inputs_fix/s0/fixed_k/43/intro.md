## Related Works

The field of large language model (LLM) training and optimization has seen significant advancements, particularly in the areas of performance modeling, distributed training, and hardware optimization. Early work focused on improving inference performance through hardware selection and configuration, such as the LLM-Pilot system \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\], which characterizes and predicts performance across different GPU architectures. This work demonstrated that optimized hardware configurations can improve performance requirements fulfillment by 33% and reduce costs by 60% \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\]. Similarly, distributed inference on CPUs has been explored to mitigate memory constraints, with solutions achieving token generation times of 140 ms, faster than human reading speed \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00029)\].

Performance modeling has also been a key area of research, particularly in the context of distributed training. The CM-DARE framework \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03072)\] analyzed distributed training performance across diverse cluster configurations, demonstrating the feasibility of predicting training speed and overhead using regression-based models. This work emphasized the importance of balancing training time, cost, and model accuracy when selecting hardware and cluster configurations. Additionally, the DisCo framework \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12769)\] proposed an automatic compilation module for distributed training, optimizing both computation and communication to achieve near-ideal training speed-up.

Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency of LLM deployment and inference, particularly in edge and resource-constrained environments. The PruneVid method \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.16117)\] introduced a visual token pruning technique for video understanding, reducing token count by over 80% while maintaining performance. Similarly, activation sparsity techniques \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12178)\] have been proposed to compress LLMs by reducing memory and computational requirements, achieving up to 50% reductions with minimal accuracy degradation. These methods highlight the growing interest in efficient model deployment and inference.

Instruction tuning has also emerged as a critical technique for improving LLM performance on specific tasks. The study \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16440)\] demonstrated that instruction-tuned models significantly outperform baseline models in the medical domain, particularly when domain-specific terminology is incorporated into the training data. Furthermore, the WizardLM framework \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\] introduced a method for generating complex instruction data using AI-evolved instructions, leading to improved performance on a range of tasks compared to human-created instructions.

In addition, the importance of failure analysis in training LLMs as agents has been highlighted. The Learning From Failure paper \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11651)\] demonstrated that incorporating negative trajectories into fine-tuning improves model performance on mathematical reasoning and strategic question-answering tasks. This approach provides valuable insights into model behavior and enables more robust training strategies.

These works collectively underscore the importance of performance modeling, hardware optimization, and efficient deployment strategies in the training and application of large language models. They also highlight the growing need for methods that improve model efficiency, adaptability, and performance across diverse tasks and environments.
