## Related Works

Dense retrieval (DR) has emerged as a powerful paradigm in information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT and LLaMA to encode text into dense representations that can be compared using simple similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. These representations are typically fine-tuned via contrastive learning to improve retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Recent research has questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in DR, arguing that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning does not significantly alter this knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. This claim was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) as a representative DR system \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. However, this analysis was limited to a specific model architecture and dataset, prompting further investigation into other representation approaches, backbone architectures, and datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different fine-tuning strategies for DR. For example, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14218)\] introduced coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00635)\] proposed ReContriever, which improves upon the SOTA unsupervised Contriever model by introducing relevance-aware contrastive learning, adaptively weighing the contrastive loss based on estimated relevance. These approaches highlight the importance of contrastive learning in DR and the potential for improving retrieval performance through more sophisticated fine-tuning strategies.

The use of different representation methods, such as CLS token versus mean pooling, has also been explored. For instance, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)\] showed that the CLS token, commonly used in BERT-based models, may not always yield the best retrieval performance, while mean pooling can lead to more discriminative representations. This was further validated in the context of the Contriever model, which uses mean pooling to generate more robust and discriminative representations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. Similarly, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\] demonstrated that the use of a lightweight multi-layer perception (MLP) decoder in autoencoder-based models can enhance the discriminative ability of learned representations, leading to better retrieval performance.

In addition to representation methods, the choice of backbone architecture has also been shown to impact retrieval performance. For example, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06663)\] compared encoder-only models like BERT with decoder-only models like LLaMA, finding that the latter can achieve better performance in certain scenarios. This was further supported by \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07841)\], which proposed MASTER, a multi-task pre-trained bottlenecked masked autoencoder that integrates various pre-training tasks to improve dense retrieval performance.

Overall, the literature suggests that while pre-training plays a foundational role in DR, fine-tuning can still have a significant impact on retrieval performance, particularly when using advanced techniques such as contrastive learning and mean pooling \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10464)\]. However, the extent of this impact may vary depending on the model architecture, representation method, and dataset used \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-in-retrieval'></a>Contrastive Learning in Retrieval: 5 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a critical technique in dense retrieval, enabling the development of more discriminative and robust text representations. Recent studies have shown that autoencoder-based language models, when combined with contrastive pre-training strategies, can significantly enhance retrieval performance by learning representations that suppress common words and highlight representative ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. This approach addresses limitations of weak decoders, which can introduce bypass effects and limit discriminative ability by treating all tokens equally \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Additionally, contrastive span prediction tasks have been introduced to pre-train encoders independently, allowing for more efficient learning of discriminative text representations while avoiding decoder-induced issues \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. Furthermore, contrastive dual learning frameworks have been proposed to refine query and document embeddings by aligning matched pairs and pushing negatives away, leading to more uniform and smooth embedding spaces \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. These methods also incorporate dual training objectives inspired by information retrieval principles, such as query likelihood, to improve ranking performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. Moreover, contrastive learning has been extended to handle robustness against typos by leveraging multiple positive samples simultaneously, enabling more effective alignment between original and typoed queries \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10939)\]. These advances highlight the growing importance of contrastive learning in improving dense retrieval, particularly in scenarios requiring fine-grained representation learning and robustness to input variations.

### <a id='category-unsupervised-contrastive-learning-approaches'></a>Unsupervised Contrastive Learning Approaches: 4 papers found.
Recent research has focused on improving dense retrievers through contrastive learning and unsupervised pre-training methods, aiming to reduce reliance on extensive data engineering and large batch training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Studies such as coCondenser \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\] and ReContriever \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\] have introduced techniques like contrastive losses and relevance-aware learning to enhance model performance without requiring heavily augmented or filtered datasets. These approaches demonstrate that unsupervised contrastive learning can effectively condense information and improve retrieval accuracy, as seen in benchmarks like MS-MARCO and BEIR \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. Additionally, the integration of encoder-decoder architectures, as seen in DeCon \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17526)\], highlights the potential of joint pre-training to improve dense prediction tasks by leveraging both encoder and decoder components simultaneously. These developments underscore the growing interest in leveraging contrastive learning for dense retrieval, emphasizing its capacity to achieve state-of-the-art results with reduced dependency on manual intervention and large-scale training data.

### <a id='category-model-adaptation-and-fine-tuning-strategies'></a>Model Adaptation and Fine-tuning Strategies: 16 papers found.
Fine-tuning pre-trained language models has been extensively studied for its impact on model performance and knowledge retention, with various approaches proposed to mitigate its limitations. However, fine-tuning also poses challenges, such as catastrophic forgetting, where models may lose previously learned knowledge during adaptation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. To address this, methods like Robust Informative Fine-Tuning (RIFT) have been introduced, aiming to retain pre-trained features while improving robustness against adversarial attacks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. Moreover, the effectiveness of fine-tuning on knowledge-intensive tasks has been questioned, with some studies suggesting that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) may outperform fine-tuning in acquiring new knowledge \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14218)\]. These findings highlight the complex interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning, emphasizing the need for careful design and evaluation of fine-tuning strategies to balance performance and knowledge retention.
