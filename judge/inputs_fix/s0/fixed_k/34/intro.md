## Related Works

Fund allocation in financial domains has attracted significant research interest, with a focus on improving the accuracy and robustness of predictive models while addressing the challenges of uncertainty and goal mismatch. Traditional approaches, such as prediction-only or Predict-then-Optimize (PnO) methods, often suffer from misalignment between forecasting and allocation objectives \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08853)\]. This issue is further compounded by the introduction of state-of-the-art time series forecasting models, which can introduce additional uncertainty into the prediction process \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08853)\]. To address these limitations, recent works have explored hybrid and deep learning models that integrate forecasting and optimization in a unified framework \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08853)\].

Several studies have investigated the use of advanced time series models for financial applications. For instance, the FinTSBridge evaluation suite was developed to bridge the gap between cutting-edge forecasting models and financial asset pricing, introducing new metrics such as msIC and msIR to assess model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06928)\]. Similarly, the use of multi-source transfer learning has been proposed to improve volatility forecasting for assets with limited historical data, such as new issues or spin-offs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.12648)\]. These approaches leverage complementary data sources to enhance forecasting accuracy, particularly in scenarios with sparse data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.12648)\].

In the realm of deep learning, CNNs and Transformers have been combined to model both short-term and long-term dependencies in financial time series, demonstrating superior performance compared to traditional statistical methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04912)\]. Additionally, large language models (LLMs) have been applied to financial time series forecasting, showing promise in handling high-dimensional and sparse data with minimal fine-tuning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19025)\]. This aligns with the broader trend of using pre-trained models for financial tasks, such as risk prediction and fraud detection \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11079)\]\[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04308)\].

Uncertainty calibration has also been a key area of focus, particularly in the context of domain drift and model robustness. Post-hoc calibration methods have been proposed to improve the reliability of confidence scores, but they often fail under domain shift \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.10988)\]. To address this, perturbation-based regularization techniques have been introduced to prevent co-adaptation between local and global parameters in time series forecasting models \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14630)\]. These methods aim to improve the generalizability and robustness of models in real-world settings.

In addition to forecasting, the importance of uncertainty quantification in financial decision-making has been emphasized. Techniques such as conformal prediction and diffusion models have been explored to provide calibrated and interpretable forecasts \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06390)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11494)\]. For example, JANET was proposed to construct valid prediction regions for both univariate and multivariate time series, enabling flexible adaptation to specific application needs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.06390)\]. Similarly, diffusion models have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in generative modeling tasks, with applications in time series forecasting and synthetic data generation \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03006)\].

Overall, the integration of forecasting and optimization in a unified framework, along with the use of advanced models and uncertainty quantification techniques, represents a significant advancement in financial time series analysis. These developments underpin the proposed RTS-PnO framework, which aims to address the challenges of goal mismatch and forecasting uncertainty in fund allocation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08853)\].
