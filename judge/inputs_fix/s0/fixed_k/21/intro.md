## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction from 2D images, leveraging implicit neural representations to model continuous 3D scenes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. These methods typically take multiple views as input and optimize the reconstructed scene by minimizing geometric and appearance uncertainty across views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often constrained by the number of input views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. In complex and dynamic scenes, where certain angles of objects are never captured, this limitation becomes more pronounced \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. To address this, several works have explored the use of video frame interpolation as a data augmentation strategy for neural rendering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\].

Video frame interpolation has been extensively studied, with various approaches focusing on improving the quality and efficiency of intermediate frame synthesis. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation followed by pixel synthesis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. More recent approaches have integrated these steps into a single convolutional process, using spatially adaptive kernels to account for motion and re-sampling simultaneously \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These methods, however, often require large kernels to handle large motion, which increases memory demands and limits scalability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. To address this, adaptive separable convolutions have been proposed, reducing the number of parameters and enabling efficient frame interpolation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\].

Another line of research has focused on leveraging neural fields for video interpolation, enabling continuous scene representation and spatial-temporal consistency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. The CURE method, for instance, represents videos as neural fields parameterized by spatiotemporal coordinates, allowing for high-quality interpolation across multiple videos \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. Additionally, methods such as the Video Frame Interpolation Transformer have introduced attention mechanisms to better capture long-range dependencies and content-aware aggregation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\].

Several works have also explored the use of depth information to improve interpolation quality, particularly in the presence of large object motion or occlusion \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Depth-aware flow projection layers have been proposed to synthesize intermediate flows that prioritize closer objects, enhancing the accuracy of frame synthesis \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Similarly, methods such as FILM have addressed large motion scenarios by using a unified network with multi-scale feature extractors and Gram matrix loss for better correlation between feature maps \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\].

In the context of 3D reconstruction, neural implicit modeling has shown promise, but it often requires dense input views to achieve high fidelity \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. Recent advances have introduced scene priors to reduce the dependency on extensive calibration and enable reconstruction from fewer views \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13817)\]. These priors, when integrated with neural fields, allow for more flexible and efficient 3D scene modeling \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13817)\].

In summary, while neural rendering has made significant strides, the challenge of improving reconstruction quality with limited input views remains an active area of research. Video frame interpolation, when combined with neural rendering, offers a promising direction for augmenting datasets and improving the robustness of 3D reconstruction in complex and dynamic scenes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\].
