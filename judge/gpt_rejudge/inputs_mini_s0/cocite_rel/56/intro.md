## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained considerable attention as a solution to data sparsity and cold-start issues in traditional sequential recommendation systems by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Most existing CDSR methods rely heavily on overlapping users who have interactions in all domains to transfer knowledge and learn cross-domain item relationships. This overlap assumption, however, limits their applicability in real-world scenarios where user overlap is often partial or absent, known as the overlap dilemma \[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Additionally, modeling complex transition patterns from mixed-domain user behavior sequences remains challenging due to the heterogeneity and temporal dynamics involved \[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\].

### Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation Approaches

Early CDSR models primarily employ sequential encoders such as RNNs or self-attention to extract intra-domain user preferences, followed by a knowledge transfer module to connect domains. For example, C2DSR utilizes graph neural networks to capture both intra- and inter-sequence item relationships while jointly learning single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44ecd5740798b8d90818fde07ea5bfceee22b83b](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44ecd5740798b8d90818fde07ea5bfceee22b83b)\]. Similarly, DDGHM introduces dual dynamic graph modeling to represent intra- and inter-domain sequential transitions combined with hybrid metric learning, improving user and item embeddings in cross-domain scenarios \[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. These methods, although effective, often depend on overlapping users and struggle with the transition complexity of mixed behavior sequences.

To address the overlap dilemma, some recent studies have explored non-overlapping cross-domain sequential recommendation (NCSR). These approaches avoid reliance on shared users by learning domain-invariant and domain-specific representations through prompt learning or federated learning frameworks, enabling cross-domain knowledge transfer without explicit domain alignment or user overlap \[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[1910.02448](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. For instance, PLCR leverages prompt learning to capture domain-specific and invariant features under non-overlapping settings, alleviating data sparsity problems \[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. Federated Mixture-of-Expert models allow privacy-preserving training across domains without sharing user data \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. Privacy-preserving CDSR techniques further incorporate differential privacy mechanisms to protect user information while maintaining recommendation accuracy \[[1910.02448](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].

### Modeling Complex Transition Patterns

Modeling complex user behavior transitions across domains is crucial for CDSR effectiveness. Mixed Information Flow networks explicitly model both behavioral and knowledge flows between domains, deciding adaptively when and what cross-domain information to transfer, which improves sequence representation and recommendation performance \[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[2103.01696](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. The Mixed Attention Network (MAN) enhances this by employing local and global attention modules to separately capture domain-specific and cross-domain sequential patterns, integrating item similarity and group-prototype attention to better understand user groups across domains \[[2311.08272](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08272)\]. Graph-based approaches such as DA-GCN and TiDA-GCN utilize domain-aware graph convolution networks with attention mechanisms to capture relationships among users and items in shared-account scenarios, further addressing transition complexity \[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[2206.08050](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\].

Shared-account cross-domain sequential recommendation (SCSR) presents additional challenges due to mixed behaviors from multiple users under one account. Parallel modeling networks like π-Net and PSJNet aim to disentangle user-specific behaviors and perform cross-domain information fusion via gating and attention modules, thereby improving recommendation accuracy in such settings \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[1910.02448](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. Reinforcement learning-based methods further refine user-specific account representations by focusing on recent behaviors and filtering irrelevant domain information through hierarchical RL tasks \[[2206.08088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08088)\].

### Disentangling User Preferences and Representation Learning

Another key line of research focuses on disentangling domain-shared and domain-specific user preferences to better leverage cross-domain information without negative transfer. DisenCDR proposes mutual-information-based regularizers to encourage exclusive and predictive domain-shared and domain-specific representations, enhancing performance especially in sparse domains \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa36b8d483b06e0bce366970fde747761151f411](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa36b8d483b06e0bce366970fde747761151f411)\]. DREAM adopts decoupled representation learning combined with supervised contrastive learning and focal loss to jointly model single- and cross-domain preferences, addressing class imbalance and data sparsity \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. IESRec utilizes optimal transport methods for internal multi-interest exploration and external domain alignment to reduce domain discrepancy and capture diverse user preferences \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44ecd5740798b8d90818fde07ea5bfceee22b83b](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44ecd5740798b8d90818fde07ea5bfceee22b83b)\].

### Integration of Large Language Models in Cross-Domain Recommendation

With the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), recent works have begun exploring their potential to address the overlap dilemma and transition complexity by leveraging LLMs’ powerful semantic representation and reasoning capabilities. LLMs can bridge the semantic gap between items across domains and capture user preferences from a unified semantic space, which is difficult for traditional embedding-based methods \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2307.02046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. Survey works highlight the use of LLMs for learning universal text-based item and user representations and enhancing recommendation models through pre-training, fine-tuning, and prompting paradigms \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2307.02046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. Chat-Rec exemplifies the integration of LLMs into recommender systems to improve interactivity, explainability, and cross-domain knowledge transfer, effectively handling cold-start scenarios and enabling zero-shot predictions \[[2303.14524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\].

Such LLM-enhanced frameworks are promising for CDSR as they can extract richer semantic item relationships and user profiles beyond behavior logs, facilitating better transfer learning even when user overlap is limited or absent. They also enable contrastive regularization and hierarchical profiling to capture cross-domain preferences more effectively, tackling both overlap and transition challenges in CDSR \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2303.14524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\].

### Summary

In summary, CDSR research has evolved from overlapping user-based transfer learning methods to models capable of handling non-overlapping users and complex behavioral transitions through graph networks, attention mechanisms, and disentangled representations \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa36b8d483b06e0bce366970fde747761151f411](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa36b8d483b06e0bce366970fde747761151f411)\]\[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44ecd5740798b8d90818fde07ea5bfceee22b83b](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/44ecd5740798b8d90818fde07ea5bfceee22b83b)\]. The integration of LLMs represents a novel and promising direction to overcome fundamental CDSR limitations by leveraging semantic knowledge and reasoning abilities inherent in large pretrained language models, thereby opening new possibilities for more robust and scalable cross-domain recommendation systems \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2307.02046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]\[[2303.14524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\].
