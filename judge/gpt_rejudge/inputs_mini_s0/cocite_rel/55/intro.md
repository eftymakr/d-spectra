## Related Works

Conversational recommender systems (CRSs) have emerged as a promising paradigm to overcome the limitations of static recommendation models by leveraging multi-turn interactions to explicitly elicit user preferences and provide personalized recommendations \[[2002.09102](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09102)\]. Unlike traditional systems that rely solely on historical user-item interactions, CRSs dynamically engage users in dialogue, enabling a more fine-grained and up-to-date understanding of user intent \[[2002.09102](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09102)\]\[[2101.09459](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09459)\]. However, accurately capturing complex and multifaceted user preferences during conversations remains a significant challenge, often requiring a careful balance between interaction effectiveness and user experience.

A core focus of CRS research has been on the design of interaction strategies that determine what questions to ask, when to recommend, and how to adapt based on user feedback. Early frameworks, such as the Estimation-Action-Reflection (EAR) model, formalize this process into stages of preference estimation, dialogue policy action, and model update upon user feedback, showing improvements in reducing conversation turns and increasing recommendation hits \[[2002.09102](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09102)\]. Reinforcement learning (RL) methods have been widely adopted to learn such policies, aiming to optimize the trade-off between asking attribute-based questions and making item recommendations at each turn \[[2105.09710](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09710)\]. However, many RL-based CRS methods treat conversation and recommendation components separately, which limits scalability and training stability. To address this, unified policy learning approaches leveraging graph-based RL have been proposed to integrate these decision problems into a single learning framework, improving both performance and generalization \[[2105.09710](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09710)\].

In addition to policy learning, preference modeling has evolved to incorporate online user feedback more effectively. Traditional models often treat attribute-level and item-level feedback independently, missing their interdependencies. Recent work introduces feedback-guided preference adaptation networks that use gating mechanisms to refine user embeddings by jointly considering fine-grained attribute feedback and coarse-grained item feedback, leading to more accurate preference estimation in multi-round conversations \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/33e0fe2d4cf01b16da89ab0c180cdcbf37b3be17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/33e0fe2d4cf01b16da89ab0c180cdcbf37b3be17)\]. This fine-grained feedback utilization is further enhanced by graph-based reasoning approaches, which explicitly traverse attribute-item relationships to prune irrelevant candidates and better capture user preferences \[[2007.00194](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194)\].

Another key challenge is reducing the burden on users during interaction. Frequent questioning can degrade user experience, motivating research into question selection strategies and user simulation for evaluation and training. Question-based recommender systems employ algorithms such as Generalized Binary Search to select the most informative questions that progressively narrow down user preferences with fewer interactions \[[2005.14255](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14255)\]. Simulated users have been developed to automate CRS evaluation and training, modeling realistic user responses by capturing preference dynamics and interaction flows. These simulators support assessing CRS performance without costly human studies and enable iterative system improvement \[[2006.08732](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08732)\]\[[2301.05544](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05544)\].

Incorporating external knowledge sources also plays a critical role in improving CRS understanding and recommendation quality. Knowledge graph (KG)-based approaches fuse entity-level and word-level semantics to align item features with natural language expressions in conversations. Such semantic fusion enhances both recommendation accuracy and the informativeness of generated dialog responses \[[2007.04032](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04032)\]\[[2105.04774](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04774)\]. Moreover, topic-guided conversational datasets and models introduce semantic transitions via topic threads to guide conversations naturally towards recommendation scenarios, improving the coherence and relevance of multi-turn dialogues \[[2010.04125](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04125)\].

Recent advances also explore generative models for question generation and feedback simulation. Knowledge-based question generation methods leverage KG relations to produce personalized and contextually relevant clarifying questions, accelerating preference elicitation while minimizing user effort \[[2105.04774](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04774)\]. Simulated users based on generative reward models provide both coarse-grained item scoring and fine-grained attribute critiques in an instruction-based unified format, enabling efficient multi-turn interaction with CRSs. This approach draws on reward-guided search techniques, such as beam search, to balance interaction effectiveness and efficiency, further improved by candidate ranking strategies \[[2002.09102](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09102)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ae5bef6b0c8317603a2deab2527383580904fd8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3ae5bef6b0c8317603a2deab2527383580904fd8)\].

Finally, the evaluation of CRSs has been significantly advanced through user simulation toolkits that incorporate persona and context modeling alongside natural language generation capabilities. These toolkits facilitate automatic, scalable evaluation of conversational strategies and system robustness, enabling benchmarking across different CRS architectures and datasets \[[2301.05544](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.05544)\]. The integration of such simulations with reinforcement learning and knowledge-enhanced components represents a promising direction toward robust, scalable, and user-friendly conversational recommendation systems.

In summary, existing work on CRSs spans key areas including multi-turn preference elicitation, unified policy learning, fine-grained feedback adaptation, knowledge graph integration, question generation, user simulation, and evaluation frameworks. Despite considerable progress, challenges remain in efficiently capturing complex user preferences with minimal user burden, ensuring transferability across domains, and seamlessly integrating diverse knowledge sources. The proposed GRSU framework builds upon these foundations by introducing a generative reward model-based simulated user that unifies coarse and fine-grained feedback actions via instruction tuning, enabling effective and efficient automatic multi-turn interaction with CRSs \[[2002.09102](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09102)\].
