## Related Works

Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) aims to leverage user interactions across multiple domains to enhance recommendation quality, particularly addressing data sparsity and cold-start problems inherent in single-domain settings \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e33f51c13728bedeeb54a76b398949a381d6152f](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e33f51c13728bedeeb54a76b398949a381d6152f)\]. Traditional CDSR approaches predominantly depend on overlapping users who have interactions in multiple domains, using their cross-domain behavior to capture item correlations and transfer knowledge \[[2410.13588](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. However, this overlap assumption is often unrealistic in practical scenarios where non-overlapping users constitute a large portion of the population, limiting the models' generalization and applicability \[[2410.13588](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588)\]\[[2304.04218](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]\[[2311.04760](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\].

To address the **overlap dilemma**, several studies have proposed methods that do not rely heavily on overlapping users. For example, prompt learning frameworks such as PLCR introduce domain-invariant and domain-specific representations without explicit alignment constraints, enabling cross-domain knowledge transfer in purely non-overlapping scenarios \[[2304.04218](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]. Federated learning and privacy-preserving techniques have also been explored to handle data isolation while maintaining performance \[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]\[[2406.03085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Moreover, adaptive frameworks like AMID incorporate multi-interest modeling and doubly robust estimators to handle open-world assumptions, where user overlap is partial or absent and data distributions shift over time \[[2401.04971](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\].

The **transition complexity** problem refers to the difficulty in modeling complex item transition patterns from mixed-domain user sequences. Many CDSR methods initially learn single-domain preferences and subsequently transfer knowledge to capture cross-domain dependencies \[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]\[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. However, such pipelines often overlook inter-sequence item relationships and the noisy nature of cross-domain sequences. Recent advances propose joint learning frameworks that simultaneously model intra-sequence and inter-sequence item interactions through graph neural networks and contrastive learning objectives, capturing more precise user preferences and alleviating data sparsity and class imbalance issues \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\].

Integrating **semantic information** via Large Language Models (LLMs) offers a promising direction to tackle both challenges. LLMs provide powerful semantic reasoning and representation capabilities, enabling richer item and user modeling beyond traditional ID-based embeddings \[[2411.19862](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19862)\]\[[2310.14304](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14304)\]. Frameworks like LLMCDSR and URLLM utilize LLMs to generate pseudo cross-domain interactions and integrate collaborative and textual features with contrastive learning, improving knowledge transfer especially in scenarios with limited overlap \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e33f51c13728bedeeb54a76b398949a381d6152f](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e33f51c13728bedeeb54a76b398949a381d6152f)\]\[[2406.03085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. LLM4CDR further demonstrates that leveraging user purchase histories and shared features with large LLMs enhances cross-domain recommendations, particularly when domains have smaller semantic gaps \[[2503.07761](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07761)\]. Additionally, semantic bias-aware frameworks improve zero-shot cross-domain sequential recommendation by aligning item embeddings and user behavior representations across domains without requiring target domain interactions \[[2501.19232](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19232)\].

Other notable approaches focus on capturing diverse user interests and mitigating negative transfer across domains. The Multi-Interest Bridge Recommender (MIBR) employs soft clustering to extract diverse interests and adaptively bridges domains to prevent irrelevant knowledge transfer \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7768e63d08be5df8ad7295b271d6bb11bc801cc4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7768e63d08be5df8ad7295b271d6bb11bc801cc4)\]. Similarly, hierarchical gating networks and multi-agent reinforcement learning frameworks dynamically model and fuse cross-domain user preferences, optimizing the transfer of relevant behaviors while filtering noise \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c13603992b90529918bd170e2f7a10858a340d43](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c13603992b90529918bd170e2f7a10858a340d43)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4124740c5376b23d87a1d539051b3f350aeb4326](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4124740c5376b23d87a1d539051b3f350aeb4326)\].

Graph-based methods have also been proposed to enhance cross-domain knowledge propagation. Dual dynamic graph models capture intra- and inter-domain item transitions through multi-level graphs and hybrid metric learning to alleviate data sparsity \[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Heterogeneous graph transfer learning incorporates item attributes and meta-path guided message propagation, enabling flexible knowledge transfer without excessive reliance on overlapped users \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fb369a92e2107b88000eb3511081e5a5c981281e](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fb369a92e2107b88000eb3511081e5a5c981281e)\]. Proxy-aware models utilize textual descriptions and time-aware attention to generate universal item representations and better capture user intents over time \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/27670ed18e96550c674e022e284fc5356a7458c2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/27670ed18e96550c674e022e284fc5356a7458c2)\].

Contrastive learning techniques are widely adopted to improve representation alignment and mutual information between single- and cross-domain embeddings, addressing domain gaps and enhancing robustness \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/11da511e9baafc1dfdf58a287c1ead787c9345bc](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/11da511e9baafc1dfdf58a287c1ead787c9345bc)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. Recent works emphasize the importance of adaptive alignment strategies, such as partial item representation alignment and feature augmentation, to avoid over-alignment and retain domain-specific characteristics \[[2405.12473](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12473)\].

In summary, existing CDSR research has progressively moved from assumption-heavy overlapped-user reliance towards more flexible, open-world settings that accommodate non-overlapped users and complex cross-domain transitions. The integration of LLMs introduces a semantic perspective that bridges item representations and user preferences effectively, promising to overcome core challenges of overlap dilemma and transition complexity. This evolving landscape highlights the importance of adaptive, semantically rich, and contrastively trained models for advancing CDSR.
