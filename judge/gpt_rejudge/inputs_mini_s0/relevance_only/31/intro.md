## Related Works

Representation learning has been central to advances in both machine learning and genomics, with significant efforts focusing on how best to capture informative features from complex biological data. Early work on unsupervised and self-supervised learning emphasized the importance of discovering effective data representations without relying on labeled data. For example, generic priors and architectures such as autoencoders and deep networks have been explored to disentangle explanatory factors in data representations \[[1206.5538](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) introduced an unsupervised learning approach based on predicting future latent representations and has been applied successfully across modalities, including DNA sequences, to capture meaningful structure \[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]\[[1905.09272](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. However, these purely unsupervised methods struggle to incorporate regulatory genomic context, limiting their performance in genomics where function depends heavily on epigenetic and chromatin features.

To address the limitations of unsupervised DNA sequence modeling, recent studies have turned to supervised approaches that leverage genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility, methylation, and transcription factor binding. Supervised training on such profiles provides richer biological signals and has been shown to improve DNA representation learning by encoding regulatory information directly relevant to gene expression and function \[[1609.08663](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08663)\]\[[1308.2134](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\]. This shift acknowledges that DNA sequence alone is insufficient to fully explain genomic function, as regulatory elements modulate activity in a context-dependent manner \[[2307.13708](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708)\]. For instance, models predicting genome-wide DNA methylation patterns achieve high accuracy by incorporating genomic position and regulatory element features, highlighting the importance of integrating multi-modal genomic data \[[1308.2134](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\].

Multi-species and multi-profile learning paradigms further enhance generalizability and biological insight by capturing relationships across diverse organisms and regulatory contexts. However, modeling these complex interdependencies requires architectural innovations. Mixture of Experts (MoE) frameworks, which dynamically route inputs to specialized subnetworks ("experts"), have shown promise in other domains such as natural language processing and computer vision by enabling models to adaptively focus on relevant features \[[2502.03499](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\]\[[2201.08443](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08443)\]. While MoE has been applied extensively outside genomics, its use in genomic representation learning remains limited. Our SPACE model extends this concept by integrating MoE with supervised genomic profile training, allowing the model to leverage species- and profile-specific expertise to capture nuanced cross-species regulatory relationships effectively.

Domain adaptation techniques also inform this approach, as they aim to learn representations invariant to domain shifts, such as differences between species or cell types, while maintaining task-specific discriminative power \[[1505.07818](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. By encouraging features that are indiscriminate to domain but informative for the learning task, these methods improve model transferability—a key challenge in multi-species genomic modeling. Similarly, semi-supervised and self-supervised learning methods that exploit unlabeled data through contrastive or context-prediction objectives have been shown to improve data efficiency in vision and language tasks and offer complementary benefits when combined with supervised signals \[[1901.09005](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09005)\]\[[1505.05192](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]\[[1606.04586](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\].

In the context of large-scale genomic foundation models, transformer-based architectures pretrained on DNA sequences have shown state-of-the-art performance in diverse genomic tasks \[[2502.03499](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\]. However, these models often rely solely on sequence data and require extensive fine-tuning for each downstream task. Incorporating supervised genomic profiles within a multi-task framework, as SPACE does, enables more effective cross-modal learning and improved performance across tasks without separate fine-tuning for each.

Finally, leveraging semantic supervision at fine spatial granularity has been demonstrated to improve dense prediction tasks in remote sensing image analysis, suggesting that point-level supervision can enhance feature localization and discrimination \[[2205.13769](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13769)\]. Analogously, in genomic representation learning, integrating fine-grained regulatory annotations can improve the spatial sensitivity of learned features, which is critical for capturing functional genomic landscapes.

In summary, prior work establishes that while unsupervised and self-supervised learning provide useful DNA representations, incorporating supervised genomic profile data yields more biologically meaningful features. The adoption of MoE architectures to capture multi-species and multi-profile dependencies is a novel and promising direction, combining advances from domain adaptation, multi-task learning, and contrastive representation learning to enhance DNA representation quality and downstream predictive performance \[[1206.5538](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]\[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]\[[1505.07818](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]\[[2205.13769](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13769)\]\[[1609.08663](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08663)\]\[[2502.03499](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\]\[[2307.13708](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708)\]\[[1905.09272](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]\[[1308.2134](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\].
