## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has attracted substantial research attention, particularly in balancing the trade-offs between ranking accuracy and fairness towards item providers and users. Early approaches focused on provider-side fairness by integrating user tolerance for provider diversity, iteratively balancing ranking quality and provider coverage \[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. These methods recognized that while fairness is desirable, users differ in their receptiveness to diversified recommendations, necessitating personalized fairness-aware re-ranking strategies \[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. Complementing this, resource allocation perspectives have modeled fairness as equitable distribution of exposure slots, with max-min fairness (MMF) shown to better protect disadvantaged providers than proportional fairness (PF) \[[2005.12974](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]\[[2005.14713](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\].

A significant advancement in understanding fairness mechanisms came from framing fair re-ranking through an economic lens, particularly as a taxation process. This perspective treats item-level fairness constraints analogously to commodity taxes imposed on suppliers, which, in turn, induce costs borne by consumers in the form of accuracy loss \[[1809.02921](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. The Tax-rank method exemplifies this approach by levying taxes based on utility differences between items and optimizing the objective using optimal transport techniques like the Sinkhorn algorithm. Tax-rank uniquely guarantees continuity and controllability over accuracy loss, addressing limitations of prior item-level tax policies \[[1809.02921](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. This economic analogy is pivotal for comprehending the elasticity of utility transfer between item groups, which governs the fairness-accuracy trade-off in re-ranking.

Fairness considerations extend beyond item providers to include multiple stakeholders in two-sided marketplaces. Approaches such as CPFair integrate fairness constraints from both consumer and producer perspectives within a joint optimization framework, demonstrating improvements in fairness without degrading recommendation quality \[[2412.00424](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00424)\]. Similarly, solutions like FairSort and P-MMF emphasize multi-stakeholder fairness by balancing user-side fairness, provider-side fairness, and personalized utility, often treating fairness constraints as resource allocation problems or knapsack-like optimization tasks \[[2005.12974](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]\[[2412.00424](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00424)\]\[[2005.14713](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\]. These works highlight the complexity of ensuring fairness in dynamic, multi-stakeholder environments and the necessity of algorithms that consider both sides simultaneously.

Several studies have also explored fairness through diversity and demographic representations. For instance, methods incorporating demographic group representations into diversity-based re-ranking can improve fairness while maintaining relevance, even with limited labeled data \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e)\]. Others have addressed fairness constraints in ranking as combinatorial optimization problems, enforcing constraints on the representation of sensitive attributes at various ranking depths with efficient algorithms that scale linearly with constraints \[[1704.06840](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]\[[1802.07281](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. Such constraints enable implementing demographic parity, disparate treatment, or disparate impact fairness notions in rankings.

The fairness-accuracy trade-off is further complicated by inherent tensions with robustness and personalization. Surveys highlight triangular trade-offs between robustness, accuracy, and fairness in deep neural networks, emphasizing that improving one often compromises others, and that these trade-offs lack comprehensive theoretical characterization \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e)\]. Personalization introduces additional challenges, as maximizing epistemic utility for individual users can conflict with fairness goals, motivating equity-based approaches that guarantee baseline access to information rather than relative fairness \[[2309.11503](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11503)\].

More recent frameworks have formalized fairness as an amortized allocation of exposure or attention over time, recognizing that single rankings cannot achieve perfect individual fairness due to position bias. Mechanisms that distribute exposure proportionally to relevance across multiple ranking instances can mitigate unfairness at the individual level while maintaining ranking quality \[[1805.01788](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]\[[2204.03046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03046)\]. These methods employ online optimization and allocation algorithms to balance fairness and accuracy dynamically.

Evaluating fairness in rankings also demands multifaceted metrics beyond single fairness criteria. The Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) framework introduces elasticity as a key concept to quantify how fairness constraints on items translate into accuracy loss for users, enabling comparative evaluation of algorithms across varying elasticity regimes \[[1809.02921](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]\[[1704.06840](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. This framework captures the transfer of "tax" costs in re-ranking and informs the design of algorithms like ElasticRank, which adjust inter-item distances in a curved space based on elasticity calculations to optimize the fairness-accuracy trade-off \[[1704.06840](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. Such economic analogies facilitate a deeper understanding of the structural mechanisms underlying fair re-ranking.

Finally, comprehensive empirical studies reveal that fairness-enhancing interventions often demonstrate sensitivity to data composition and preprocessing, suggesting brittleness in fairness guarantees \[[1802.04422](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04422)\]. There is also recognition that fairness notions predominantly focus on group similarity outcomes, which may obscure trade-offs and biases at the individual level \[[2306.01417](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01417)\]\[[2103.01335](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01335)\]. Addressing these concerns, recent algorithms incorporate personalized fairness tolerances and joint consumer-producer fairness objectives to better reflect real-world complexities \[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]\[[2412.00424](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00424)\]\[[2204.08085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]\[[2010.01470](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01470)\].

In summary, the literature on fairness in re-ranking spans diverse methodologies—from economic taxation analogies and resource allocation models to demographic representational fairness and amortized exposure mechanisms—each contributing to a nuanced understanding of the fairness-accuracy trade-off. The introduction of elasticity-based evaluation and algorithmic design offers promising directions for more principled and controllable fairness-aware re-ranking.
