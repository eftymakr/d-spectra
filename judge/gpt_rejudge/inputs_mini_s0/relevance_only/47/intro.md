## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) focuses on leveraging data and knowledge from multiple domains to improve recommendation quality across various services or scenarios \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Traditional Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR) methods often assume the existence of at least one rich domain to transfer knowledge to sparser ones, typically relying on domain-invariant user preferences to bridge domains \[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]\[[2103.01696](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. However, this assumption is often unrealistic in practice since sparsity is common across all domains, and user preferences can be domain-specific, leading to challenges in effective knowledge transfer \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\].

To address these challenges, recent MDR approaches have explored disentangling domain-shared and domain-specific knowledge. For instance, embedding disentangling frameworks separate shared and private embeddings at both model and representation levels to better capture domain nuances and improve transferability, especially when domain overlaps are limited \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. Similarly, hierarchical hypergraph networks model cross-domain interactions by capturing high-order connections among users and items, facilitating more effective preference transfer while preserving domain uniqueness \[[2211.11191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\].

Negative transfer, where irrelevant or conflicting information from certain domains degrades performance in others, remains a critical obstacle in MDR. This phenomenon arises due to heterogeneous domain distributions and conflicting user preferences \[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e776b35d50f07998e0c4fdca6bfe1f21c364fddf](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e776b35d50f07998e0c4fdca6bfe1f21c364fddf)\]. Cooperative game theory-based methods have been proposed to quantitatively assess and mitigate negative transfer by adaptively weighting domain contributions based on their marginal utility to model performance \[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Hierarchical contrastive learning techniques further alleviate negative transfer by exploiting coarse-to-fine category correlations across domains, enabling the model to capture domain-level relevance even when fine-grained item similarities are weak \[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\].

Domain alignment and adaptation strategies are widely employed to bridge domain gaps. Adversarial learning-based domain discriminators and domain adaptation networks seek to align user and item embeddings across domains, reducing distributional discrepancies and improving the transfer of user preferences \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cdc38867ade499e320e4ed3f93473a7cd67ecad4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cdc38867ade499e320e4ed3f93473a7cd67ecad4)\]. Sample and source distillation methods selectively filter source domain samples and rank source domains based on their relevance to the target domain, enhancing the quality of transferred knowledge and mitigating negative transfer effects \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6680377a1a0f73badea1de84c045de80b8368c69](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6680377a1a0f73badea1de84c045de80b8368c69)\].

Sequential and multi-target recommendations extend MDR by incorporating temporal dynamics and simultaneously improving recommendations in multiple domains. For example, multi-domain sequential recommendation models leverage domain-specific and shared sequential behaviors, supplementing sparse domain histories with cross-domain item information to enhance domain-specific preference learning \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\]. Multi-target CDR methods jointly improve recommendation accuracy in several domains by sharing embeddings and multi-source content features through multi-task learning frameworks \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. More recently, prompt-enhanced paradigms have been proposed to efficiently capture domain commonalities and distinctions with minimal parameter tuning, demonstrating scalability and effectiveness in multi-scenario recommendations \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/884f8a29a7979a1f05223f432d9a149ac4dcc6b4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/884f8a29a7979a1f05223f432d9a149ac4dcc6b4)\].

Graph-based methods also play a crucial role in MDR by modeling user-item interactions across domains as unified graphs, enabling preference propagation and alignment at multiple granularity levels. Preference Propagation GraphNet constructs cross-domain preference matrices to capture high-order connectivity and simplify cross-domain knowledge transfer into unified multi-task optimization \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\]. Similarly, tag tree-guided multi-grained alignment exploits hierarchical tag structures and influential publisher-user relations to facilitate feature space alignment in domains with sparse user or item overlaps, such as short video recommendation \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/161f9d8c3694ed32e07a716c63c109d60bf2d4ee](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/161f9d8c3694ed32e07a716c63c109d60bf2d4ee)\].

Despite these advances, current MDR models largely employ static or uniform knowledge transfer mechanisms that treat all domains equally or rely on fixed structures for domain sharing, which can cause interference and suboptimal transfer when domain qualities vary \[[2309.06217](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. Dynamic domain selection mechanisms remain underexplored. The problem of effectively measuring domain-level distances and dynamically selecting suitable domains to avoid negative transfer is crucial but not sufficiently addressed. Some frameworks attempt to mitigate domain conflicts via domain negotiation and regularization strategies to balance shared and domain-specific parameters \[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. However, explicit, lightweight methods for modeling complex inter-domain relationships and selectively transferring knowledge based on domain similarity have yet to be fully developed.

In summary, the MDR field has seen significant progress in disentangling domain-shared and specific knowledge, addressing negative transfer, and leveraging sequential and graph-based modeling. Yet, the dynamic selection of similar domains for adaptive knowledge transfer to alleviate negative transfer remains an open and promising research direction \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]\[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e776b35d50f07998e0c4fdca6bfe1f21c364fddf](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e776b35d50f07998e0c4fdca6bfe1f21c364fddf)\]\[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]\[[2309.06217](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\].
