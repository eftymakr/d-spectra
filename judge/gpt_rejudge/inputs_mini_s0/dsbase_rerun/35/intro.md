## Related Works

Protein dynamics underpin critical biological functions and are traditionally studied through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which provide detailed temporal trajectories but are computationally intensive and often limited by sampling inefficiencies \[[2403.14088v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088v2)\]\[[2505.17478v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17478v2)\]. To address these challenges, recent research has focused on generative modeling techniques, especially diffusion-based approaches, which learn distributions over protein structures from crystallographic data, enabling efficient sampling of diverse protein conformations.

Energy-based models (EBMs) have been employed to capture the physical realism of protein conformations at atomic resolution. Notably, an EBM trained solely on crystallized protein data demonstrated competitive performance with established physics-based energy functions such as Rosetta, effectively modeling relevant physicochemical properties without explicit physical tuning \[[2004.13167v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167v1)\]. However, standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization due to the complex energy landscapes of proteins, motivating alternative strategies to incorporate physical information into generative frameworks.

Diffusion models equivariant to SE(3) — the group of 3D rotations and translations — have gained prominence in protein backbone and conformation generation. ConfDiff, a force-guided SE(3) diffusion model, integrates physical priors by embedding force information into the generation process, yielding conformations that better adhere to equilibrium distributions and surpassing prior state-of-the-art methods in accuracy and diversity \[[2403.14088v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088v2)\]. Similarly, FrameDiff leverages SE(3)-equivariant score matching on multiple rigid bodies to generate large monomer backbones without reliance on pretrained prediction networks, demonstrating the ability to generalize beyond known natural structures \[[2302.02277v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02277v3)\]. The FoldFlow family of models employs flow-matching paradigms over SE(3) to produce stable and diverse protein backbones, offering faster and more stable training compared to diffusion-based methods, and enabling flexible mapping between invariant distributions \[[2310.02391v4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02391v4)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of incorporating geometric equivariance and physical constraints to improve generative modeling of protein structures.

Beyond purely geometric considerations, integrating temporal dynamics into protein conformation modeling has been explored. ConfRover introduces an autoregressive architecture combining temporal sequence modeling with an SE(3) diffusion decoder, allowing simultaneous sampling of both static conformations and dynamic trajectories from MD data, thus bridging static structure generation and dynamic behavior modeling \[[2505.17478v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17478v2)\]. This represents a significant step toward capturing protein dynamics in generative frameworks.

Structure Language Modeling (SLM) offers an alternative paradigm by encoding protein conformations into discrete latent spaces and applying conditional language models to capture sequence-specific conformational distributions. This approach achieves substantial runtime efficiency gains (20-100x speedup) compared to traditional diffusion models while enabling interpretable exploration of conformational ensembles, including intrinsically disordered proteins and conformational transitions \[[2410.18403v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403v2)\].

Controllable protein design through language models has also advanced, leveraging the analogy between protein sequences and natural language. Transformer-based models pretrained on large protein sequence datasets can generate novel sequences with desired properties, and prompt-based methods enable task-aware conditioning by injecting conformational or interaction knowledge into pretrained models \[[2201.07338v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.07338v2)\]\[[2202.02944v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02944v1)\]. While primarily sequence-focused, these methods complement structure generation by providing control over functionally relevant sequence variation.

Knowledge-based potentials, such as Potentials of Mean Force (PMFs), have long been used to incorporate statistical insights from known protein structures into energy functions. Recent formalizations interpret PMFs probabilistically, clarifying their scope and enabling generalizations beyond pairwise distances to arbitrary structural features, which can be iteratively refined to sculpt energy funnels guiding folding and design \[[1008.4006v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1008.4006v3)\]. However, these potentials alone may be insufficient to ensure physical plausibility in generative models without integration with explicit physical forces or constraints.

Other related works address the distinction between physically realistic protein conformations and decoys. Studies have identified key features of native protein cores—such as hydrophobic packing and residue distributions—that distinguish real structures from computational models, informing the development of deep learning-based scoring methods to evaluate model quality without reliance on target structures \[[2001.01161v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.01161v1)\].

Finally, diffusion models have been broadly reviewed for autonomous protein engineering, highlighting their theoretical foundations, including denoising diffusion probabilistic models and score-based models, and emphasizing the role of E(3)-equivariance in maintaining physical stability during generation \[[2501.02680v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.02680v1)\]. These reviews underscore the growing consensus that embedding physical symmetries and energy-based feedback into generative models is essential for advancing protein design and conformation prediction.

In summary, the landscape of protein conformation generation and dynamics modeling is rapidly evolving, with energy-based alignment methods such as the proposed Energy-based Alignment (EBA) building upon these advances by efficiently integrating physical supervision into generative models to balance conformational states according to energy differences, thereby improving the physical plausibility and predictive quality of generated protein ensembles.
