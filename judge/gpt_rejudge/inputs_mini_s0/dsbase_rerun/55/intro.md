## Related Works

Conversational recommendation systems (CRSs) have gained increasing attention for their ability to engage users through multi-turn dialogues to elicit preferences and provide personalized recommendations. These systems address challenges related to dynamic and multifaceted user preferences, which are often difficult to capture accurately through static or single-turn interactions \[[2405.17740v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17740v1)\]. Existing CRSs primarily rely on two paradigms: historical user-item interactions and conversational feedback, the latter allowing explicit solicitation of user needs and iterative refinement of recommendations \[[2405.17740v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17740v1)\].

A key difficulty in CRSs lies in effectively understanding and modeling user preferences from conversations, especially when preferences are complex or evolve during interaction. Prior approaches have leveraged reinforcement learning frameworks to simulate user behaviors and optimize recommendation policies through reward signals extracted from user feedback, enabling adaptation to user preferences without explicit supervision \[[2112.05197v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05197v1)\]\[[1708.04378v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04378v1)\]. However, these methods often require extensive training and human involvement, which can degrade user experience due to frequent or prolonged interactions.

To reduce dependency on real user input, simulated users have been introduced as efficient proxies for automatic evaluation and training of CRSs. User simulation methods enable the identification and diagnosis of conversational breakdowns by generating synthetic dialogues, thus facilitating system robustness improvements without costly human trials \[[2405.14249v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14249v1)\]. Simulated users can mimic realistic interaction patterns and provide feedback that helps systems better capture latent user preferences during multi-turn conversations.

Recent advances in natural language processing, particularly large language models (LLMs), have further enhanced the capabilities of conversational recommenders. Systems such as VideolandGPT employ LLMs to integrate personalized candidate selection based on user chat interactions, improving recommendation accuracy and user satisfaction \[[2309.03645v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03645v1)\]. However, challenges remain in ensuring fairness and availability consistency across recommended items, pointing to the complexity of generating reliable and context-aware recommendations in real-world settings.

In addition to user simulation and LLM integration, improving recommendation quality through rich item metadata and attribute-aware representations has shown promise. Approaches that encode item attributes and contextual information directly into embeddings allow models to incorporate semantic alignment with dialog context, leading to more accurate recommendations and coherent responses without the overhead of maintaining large knowledge graphs \[[2112.08140v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08140v1)\]\[[2312.06145v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06145v1)\]. Similarly, proxy-based item representation techniques address issues of data sparsity and infrequent item learning by compositional encoding, enabling effective attribute and context-aware recommendations with improved parameter efficiency \[[2312.06145v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06145v1)\].

The transformation of user critiques into explicit preferences represents another avenue for refining conversational recommendations. By interpreting subjective user feedback (e.g., critiques of item features) into positive preference statements, systems can retrieve better-suited items and iteratively improve recommendation quality \[[2109.07576v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07576v1)\]. This critique-to-preference transformation leverages large neural language models in few-shot settings, enhancing the system’s ability to reason about nuanced user inputs without requiring extensive explicit preference elicitation.

Cold-start and sparse data scenarios pose additional challenges for CRSs. Pre-training item-attribute graphs that integrate fine-grained item attributes, historical purchase sequences, and review texts has been effective in bridging gaps between known and new items, enabling strict cold-start item recommendation with superior performance \[[2306.14462v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14462v1)\]. Multi-source augmentation strategies also enhance embedding learning for novel content discovery, particularly in domains like podcasts where item correlation aids user exploration despite limited interaction data \[[2301.01737v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.01737v2)\].

In balancing effectiveness and efficiency, research has explored reward-guided search strategies and knowledge distillation techniques to accelerate on-device recommendation inference while preserving model capacity \[[2209.13422v4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13422v4)\]. These methods support real-time interaction without sacrificing recommendation quality, which is critical for conversational recommenders deployed in resource-constrained environments.

Overall, the proposed generative reward model-based simulated user (GRSU) framework builds upon these lines of research by unifying coarse-grained generative item scoring and fine-grained attribute-based critique feedback into an instruction-tuned simulated user. This approach enables automatic multi-turn interaction with CRSs, inspired by reward-guided search paradigms and beam search techniques to achieve efficient and effective recommendation refinement \[[2112.05197v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05197v1)\]\[[2405.14249v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14249v1)\]\[[2109.07576v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07576v1)\]. By leveraging instruction tuning on synthesized data, GRSU facilitates seamless integration with CRSs, addressing the challenge of balancing user involvement and recommendation accuracy.
