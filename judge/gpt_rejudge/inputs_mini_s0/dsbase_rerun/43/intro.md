## Related Works

Distributed training of large language models (LLMs) presents complex challenges involving communication overhead, deployment heterogeneity, and the need for accurate performance modeling to guide optimization. Early research in distributed deep neural network (DNN) training highlighted communication as a major bottleneck, proposing algorithmic and network-level strategies to reduce communication volume and overlap communication with computation, thereby accelerating training in bandwidth-limited clusters \[[2003.03009v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03009v2)\]. Techniques such as selective synchronization dynamically reduce communication frequency by deciding whether to aggregate updates based on their significance, achieving substantial speedups (up to 14×) without accuracy loss compared to bulk-synchronous parallel methods \[[2307.07950v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07950v2)\].

The heterogeneity of device topologies and DNN architectures further complicates distributed training. Systems like TAG integrate DNN computation graphs with device topology graphs using graph neural networks and search-based methods to generate optimized deployment strategies that minimize communication and training time. This approach yields up to 4.56× speedups across diverse models and hardware configurations without extensive manual tuning \[[2302.06126v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06126v1)\]. These advances demonstrate the importance of topology-aware scheduling and automatic optimization in large-scale LLM training.

Performance modeling and estimation frameworks are essential to characterize LLM training behaviors and enable efficient exploration of deployment configurations. While some prior work focused on optimizing inference performance on CPUs or characterizing inference service efficiency, there remains a gap in trace-driven, fine-grained performance modeling for large-scale LLM training \[[2305.14982v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14982v2)\]\[[2407.01505v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01505v1)\]. Self-supervised learning methods have also been applied to accelerate optimization solvers in constrained settings, offering improved accuracy and speed over traditional methods, which may inspire similar approaches for performance estimation in training pipelines \[[2409.08066v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08066v3)\].

Frameworks that facilitate interaction with distributed model internals, such as FlexModel, are valuable for bridging machine learning expertise and distributed system complexities. By providing user-friendly interfaces and hooks for models distributed across multi-GPU and multi-node environments, such tools democratize access to model interpretability and facilitate system-level analysis \[[2312.03140v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03140v1)\]. Quantization techniques, including weight and key/value cache quantization, reduce memory footprints and computational demands during LLM deployment, which indirectly impact training efficiency through resource savings \[[2402.12065v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12065v2)\].

Recent efforts in instruction data generation and multimodal reasoning highlight the evolving complexity of LLM tasks and the corresponding need for adaptable training and evaluation frameworks. For example, AI-evolved instructions have been used to fine-tune models like WizardLM, enhancing instruction-following capabilities beyond human-created datasets \[[2304.12244v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244v3)\]. Multimodal large language models extend LLMs’ capacity to process and reason over diverse input types, which may further increase training and deployment complexity \[[2306.13549v4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549v4)\].

Overall, these works underscore the multifaceted challenges and solutions relevant to distributed LLM training, including communication optimization, topology-aware deployment, trace-driven performance modeling, and tooling for distributed model interaction. However, existing solutions often focus on isolated aspects such as communication reduction or inference optimization, lacking comprehensive, accurate trace-driven performance estimation to guide large-scale training configuration exploration. The proposed Lumos toolkit addresses this gap by leveraging execution traces to predict runtime behavior with high accuracy across varied models and configurations, enabling systematic and efficient optimization of distributed LLM training workflows.
