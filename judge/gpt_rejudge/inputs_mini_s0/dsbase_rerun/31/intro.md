## Related Works

Pre-training approaches have become foundational in modeling DNA sequences, with early efforts predominantly focusing on unsupervised methods that leverage large-scale unlabeled genomic data through next-token prediction or masked language modeling. Models such as Omni-DNA exemplify this by pretraining on DNA sequences before fine-tuning for multiple genomic tasks, achieving state-of-the-art results on diverse benchmarks \[[2502.03499v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499v1)\]. However, unsupervised DNA sequence pre-training often lacks the ability to capture regulatory information critical to genomic function, such as chromatin accessibility and epigenetic modifications, which are essential for understanding gene expression and phenotype manifestation \[[2101.05870v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.05870v1)\]\[[2307.13708v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708v1)\].

Recognizing these limitations, recent work has shifted toward supervised learning paradigms that incorporate genomic profiles—functional annotations reflecting DNA accessibility, methylation, and other regulatory signals—to guide representation learning. This supervised training has been shown to improve the prediction of complex phenotypes and disease traits by integrating multi-omic data, thereby enhancing biological interpretability beyond pure sequence models \[[2101.05870v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.05870v1)\]\[[2407.19385v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19385v1)\]\[[1301.5948v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1301.5948v3)\]\[[2307.13708v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708v1)\]. For example, genomic profile prediction tasks that include acetylation and methylation signals have been addressed jointly through multi-task learning frameworks, leading to improved generalization across profiles and species \[[2502.03499v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499v1)\].

In parallel, the Mixture of Experts (MoE) model architecture has gained traction for efficiently modeling complex, heterogeneous data by dynamically routing inputs to specialized sub-networks or “experts.” Traditional MoE approaches employ homogeneous experts with uniform capacity, which can limit specialization and computational efficiency \[[2408.10681v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10681v1)\]\[[2402.14800v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800v2)\]. Recent advancements propose heterogeneous MoE models with experts of varying sizes and capabilities, improving parameter utilization and enabling specialization tailored to input complexity \[[2408.10681v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10681v1)\]. Additionally, MoE variants with adaptive gating mechanisms have been theoretically analyzed to ensure sample-efficient expert estimation and robust convergence \[[2503.03213v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.03213v1)\]. These developments underscore the suitability of MoE architectures for modeling diverse genomic inputs and multi-profile tasks.

While MoE has been extensively applied in domains such as natural language processing and computer vision, its application in genomic representation learning remains nascent. Some studies have explored expert pruning and skipping to enhance deployment efficiency in large language models \[[2402.14800v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800v2)\], but the integration of MoE for capturing cross-species and multi-profile genomic relationships is novel. GraphMETRO, for instance, employs MoE to handle complex distributional shifts in graph data, highlighting the potential for MoE to model biological heterogeneity \[[2312.04693v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04693v3)\]. Our work extends this idea by leveraging MoE to disentangle species-specific and profile-specific patterns in genomic data, facilitating more effective representation learning.

Furthermore, the multi-species and multi-profile nature of genomic data necessitates models capable of collaborative learning across tasks and domains. Previous approaches often focus on single species or individual profiles, limiting transferability and biological insight \[[2502.03499v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499v1)\]\[[1301.5948v3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1301.5948v3)\]. Supervised multi-task frameworks have demonstrated that simultaneous modeling of related genomic profiles can enhance performance compared to training separate models \[[2502.03499v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499v1)\]. However, these methods typically do not explicitly model expert specialization or adaptive collaboration across species and profiles. The SPACE framework addresses this gap by incorporating MoE to adaptively route information to specialized experts, capturing complex interdependencies in genomic data.

Finally, the importance of integrating genomic variation and functional annotations for phenotype prediction has been emphasized by consortia such as IGVF, which aim to map how genetic variants affect genome function and disease phenotypes \[[2307.13708v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708v1)\]. Such comprehensive datasets and predictive modeling frameworks reinforce the utility of supervised profile prediction over unsupervised sequence-only approaches. Combining these insights with efficient MoE architectures provides a promising direction for advancing DNA representation learning.

In summary, prior research has established the value of supervised genomic profile prediction and multi-task learning in capturing functional genomic complexity, while recent advances in heterogeneous Mixture of Experts architectures offer computational and representational advantages. Our work uniquely integrates these strands by developing a species-profile adaptive collaborative MoE model that effectively learns DNA representations across diverse species and genomic profiles, outperforming purely sequence-based pre-training methods.
