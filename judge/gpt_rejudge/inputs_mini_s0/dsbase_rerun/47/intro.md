## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) aims to leverage user interactions across multiple domains to enhance recommendation performance, addressing challenges such as cold-start problems and knowledge transfer \[[2302.05990v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05990v2)\]. Traditional MDR approaches often assume uniform knowledge transfer by using a single shared structure to capture domain-shared information. However, this assumption overlooks the complex and heterogeneous relationships among domains, which may lead to negative transfer when domain conflicts or poor-quality domains exist \[[2311.13188v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188v1)\]\[[2303.01003v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01003v1)\].

Early MDR models primarily focused on treating domains as separate sources or relied on explicit parameter sharing mechanisms, which can cause mutual interference and limit adaptability due to static parameters \[[2309.06217v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217v2)\]. Recent advances have introduced dynamic parameter generation strategies, such as hyper-networks that produce domain-specific adapter parameters, improving flexibility and mitigating interference \[[2309.06217v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217v2)\]. Similarly, graph neural network-based methods have been proposed to capture both intra- and inter-domain user interactions sequentially, learning domain-guided and general representations to balance recent and long-term user interests, thereby reducing negative transfer \[[2302.05990v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05990v2)\].

Negative transfer, the degradation of performance due to conflicting or irrelevant cross-domain information, remains a significant challenge in MDR. To address this, cooperative game-theoretic approaches estimate the marginal contribution of each domain to selectively weight their influence, reducing the impact of harmful domains \[[2311.13188v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188v1)\]. Hierarchical contrastive learning has also been utilized to leverage coarse-level category information, improving cross-domain relevance even when fine-level item correlations are weak \[[2311.13188v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188v1)\]. In speech recognition and domain adaptation contexts, data selection based on relevance metrics has been effective in limiting negative transfer by filtering out outlier samples \[[1509.02409v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.02409v1)\]. Reinforced transfer networks that incorporate both high-level and pixel-level information further improve selective knowledge transfer in partial domain adaptation scenarios \[[1905.10756v4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10756v4)\].

Besides selective knowledge transfer, modeling domain distinctions through domain-sensitive features has shown promise. Identifying and explicitly modeling features that vary significantly across domains enables better capture of domain-specific information, which can be stored in memory modules for retrieval during recommendation, thus improving domain differentiation and mitigating negative transfer \[[2405.12892v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12892v2)\]. This approach highlights that many MDR systems overlook domain-sensitive features, leading to insufficient learning of domain distinctions.

Knowledge enhancement via external resources, such as knowledge graphs, combined with multi-domain interaction modeling, has been explored to enrich recommendation signals. Unifying knowledge graph information with multi-domain user behavior can yield additive improvements, especially in conversational AI settings involving multiple content domains \[[2306.06302v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06302v2)\]. Complementary to this, universal multi-modal pre-training frameworks integrate various item content modalities (e.g., text, images) and user interactions across domains, enabling efficient transfer to new domains and robustness to missing or noisy item data \[[2311.01831v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01831v1)\].

Domain similarity or distance measurement is crucial for effective selective transfer. Some MDR methods rely on explicit domain similarity metrics or prototype-based domain representations to quantify relationships among domains, facilitating dynamic selection of relevant domains and reduction of negative transfer \[[1812.06229v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06229v1)\]. Such strategies align with findings in transferability surveys, emphasizing the need for adaptive knowledge reuse and the avoidance of negative transfer through careful domain relevance assessment \[[2201.05867v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05867v1)\].

There is also an emerging focus on lightweight, plug-and-play modules that can be integrated into existing MDR backbones to dynamically adapt to domain heterogeneity without incurring significant computational overhead \[[2309.06217v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217v2)\]. This aligns with practical demands for scalable and efficient MDR solutions.

In summary, existing works address MDR challenges through various strategies: dynamic parameter adaptation, graph-based multi-domain representation learning, cooperative game theory to assess domain contributions, selective data/sample filtering, domain-sensitive feature modeling, and knowledge-enhanced multi-domain learning. However, most approaches either do not explicitly measure domain-level gaps or lack mechanisms to dynamically select appropriate source domains, leading to persistent negative transfer issues \[[2302.05990v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05990v2)\]\[[2311.13188v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188v1)\]\[[2405.12892v2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12892v2)\]\[[2303.01003v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01003v1)\]. The proposed Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) addresses this gap by introducing a prototype-based domain distance measure and supervised signals to dynamically select similar domains, offering a novel and lightweight solution to mitigate negative transfer effectively.
