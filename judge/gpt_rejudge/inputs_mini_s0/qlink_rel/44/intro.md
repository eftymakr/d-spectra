## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been extensively studied with the evolution from traditional matrix factorization methods to advanced graph-based neural models that capture complex user-item interactions. Early CF approaches largely relied on matrix factorization, which models latent user and item embeddings from interaction data but often falls short in capturing high-order relationships and suffers from sparsity issues \[[1205.2618](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\]. The introduction of neural architectures, such as Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF), enhanced interaction modeling by replacing inner products with nonlinear neural networks, improving expressiveness and recommendation accuracy \[[1708.05031](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. However, these models primarily focus on direct user-item feature interactions without explicitly leveraging the underlying graph structure.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for CF by explicitly modeling the user-item interaction graph. Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) propagates embeddings over the bipartite user-item graph to capture high-order connectivity, effectively injecting collaborative signals into embeddings \[[2002.02126](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This approach demonstrated significant improvements over prior methods but still faced challenges including training complexity and over-smoothing in deeper layers. Subsequent works like LightGCN simplified GCN designs by removing feature transformation and nonlinear activations, focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation. LightGCN achieved superior performance and training efficiency by linearly propagating embeddings and aggregating multi-layer representations \[[2002.02126](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]\[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Further enhancements, such as residual connections in linear GCNs, were proposed to mitigate over-smoothing and enable deeper architectures for large-scale CF \[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\].

Scalability of GNNs to web-scale recommendation scenarios has been addressed by combining efficient random walks with graph convolutions, enabling embedding learning on graphs with billions of nodes and edges, as demonstrated in Pinterest's recommender system \[[1806.01973](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. This highlights the potential of graph convolution approaches to handle massive real-world datasets while maintaining recommendation quality.

Beyond basic graph convolution, recent methods have focused on disentangling user intents and refining interaction representations. Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) models multiple user intents by learning intent-specific embeddings and refining interaction graphs iteratively, leading to more interpretable and accurate recommendations \[[2007.01764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. This addresses the limitation of uniform modeling of user-item relationships, which can obscure diverse user motivations.

Representation binarization has attracted interest as a means to compress embeddings for efficient online inference, reducing memory and computational costs. However, typical quantization methods often neglect the information loss introduced, which can degrade recommendation quality. While there is limited prior work directly on binarized graph embeddings for CF, related fields have explored loss-aware binarization techniques to preserve model performance in deep networks \[[1708.05031](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. For example, loss-aware binarization minimizes the reconstruction loss of weights to retain accuracy, and near-lossless binarization of word embeddings has demonstrated that semantic information can be preserved with minimal accuracy degradation \[[1802.05814](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. These insights are crucial when adapting binarization to graph-based embeddings, where preserving collaborative signals is essential.

Self-supervised learning techniques have also been applied to graph-based recommendation to enhance robustness and accuracy. By generating multiple views of nodes via dropout and random walks, self-supervised graph learning (SGL) reinforces node representations through contrastive objectives, improving performance on long-tail items and noisy interaction data \[[2010.10783](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. This auxiliary supervision complements traditional supervised tasks and can mitigate information loss during embedding compression.

Inductive representation learning approaches, such as GraphSAGE, enable generating embeddings for unseen nodes by learning aggregation functions over local neighborhoods instead of learning fixed embeddings per node. This inductive capability is important for dynamic recommendation systems where new users or items continuously appear \[[1706.02216](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Moreover, probabilistic models like Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) have been extended for CF to model nonlinear user-item relationships with Bayesian inference, offering principled ways to handle uncertainty and improve generalization \[[1802.05814](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\].

Overall, existing works highlight the effectiveness of graph-based collaborative filtering and neural representation learning, while pointing to challenges in embedding efficiency, information preservation during binarization, and interpretability of learned embeddings. Our work builds upon these foundations by explicitly addressing information loss in graph embedding binarization, leveraging pseudo-positive supervisory signals and embedding sample synthesis, thus advancing the state of the art in efficient and accurate recommender systems.
