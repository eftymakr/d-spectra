## Related Works

Active learning (AL) aims to reduce annotation costs by selectively querying the most informative unlabeled data samples for labeling, which has been extensively studied in centralized settings \[[1912.04977](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04977)\]. However, the limited data diversity and annotation budget in distributed environments pose additional challenges, motivating the development of Federated Active Learning (FAL). FAL integrates AL with federated learning (FL), enabling multiple clients to collaboratively select data and train models without sharing raw data, thus preserving privacy and complying with data regulations \[[1806.00582](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00582)\]\[[2102.02079](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.02079)\]. Despite this progress, existing FAL approaches often overlook the heterogeneity of data distributions among clients—a fundamental characteristic of federated systems—which leads to fluctuations in both global and local model parameters and consequently degrades model accuracy \[[1812.06127](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06127)\]\[[1806.00582](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00582)\]\[[2102.02079](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.02079)\].

Federated learning itself has been widely studied as a distributed paradigm where clients collaboratively train a global model while keeping data localized, addressing privacy concerns and communication constraints \[[1912.04977](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04977)\]\[[1602.05629](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.05629)\]. The core challenges in FL arise from statistical heterogeneity (non-IID local data distributions) and systems heterogeneity (varying client resources), which affect convergence and model quality \[[1812.06127](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06127)\]\[[2003.00295](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00295)\]. Methods such as FedProx introduce proximal terms to stabilize training under heterogeneity, providing convergence guarantees and improved robustness compared to FedAvg \[[1812.06127](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06127)\]. Adaptive federated optimization techniques employing federated versions of adaptive optimizers like Adam and Adagrad have also shown to improve convergence and performance under heterogeneous data \[[2003.00295](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00295)\].

To address statistical heterogeneity, several personalization and clustering strategies have been proposed. Personalization approaches like FedPer incorporate client-specific layers to mitigate the impact of non-IID data on model accuracy \[[1912.00818](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.00818)\]. Meta-learning techniques, including model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML), have been adapted for FL to enable fast local adaptation, improving personalized performance \[[1909.12488](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12488)\]. Clustered federated learning (CFL) methods group clients with similar data distributions to learn specialized models that outperform a single global model on heterogeneous data \[[1910.01991](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]\[[2006.04088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04088)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of capturing client diversity but do not inherently solve the problem of selecting informative samples for annotation in active learning scenarios.

Communication efficiency and client selection strategies are critical in FL due to limited network capabilities and heterogeneous client resources. Techniques such as structured and sketched updates reduce communication overhead by compressing model updates \[[1610.05492](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.05492)\]. Client selection algorithms like FedCS and Power-of-Choice prioritize clients based on resource availability and local losses, accelerating convergence and improving final model accuracy \[[2010.01243](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01243)\]\[[1804.08333](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.08333)\]. However, these methods focus on training efficiency rather than actively selecting data samples for labeling.

The intersection of active learning and federated learning remains nascent. Some work attempts to integrate active learning into FL by selecting clients or data points that maximize model improvement. For example, correlation-based client selection strategies consider loss correlations to improve convergence and data efficiency \[[2006.07242](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07242)\]. Model distillation methods enable personalized federated training by transferring knowledge from the global to local models, offering an alternative aggregation mechanism that can handle model heterogeneity \[[2006.07242](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07242)\]. Ensemble distillation further improves model fusion in heterogeneous federated settings by training a global classifier on the outputs of diverse client models, reducing communication rounds and enhancing robustness \[[2006.07242](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07242)\]. Nevertheless, these approaches primarily address model aggregation and client selection rather than epistemic uncertainty or decision boundary dynamics critical for effective data selection in active learning.

Recent studies underscore the challenge that non-IID data distributions pose for federated learning accuracy and convergence, showing that none of the existing FL algorithms uniformly outperform others across diverse heterogeneous scenarios \[[2102.02079](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.02079)\]. Moreover, weight divergence caused by data heterogeneity can severely reduce model accuracy, which can be partially mitigated by sharing a small amount of globally accessible data \[[1806.00582](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.00582)\]. However, such global data sharing may be impractical due to privacy concerns, highlighting the need for heterogeneity-aware data selection mechanisms that operate fully locally.

In summary, while federated learning research has extensively addressed statistical and systems heterogeneity through optimization, personalization, clustering, and client selection methods \[[1812.06127](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06127)\]\[[2003.00295](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00295)\]\[[1912.00818](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.00818)\]\[[1910.01991](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]\[[2010.01243](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01243)\]\[[1804.08333](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.08333)\]\[[2006.04088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04088)\], and active learning effectively reduces annotation costs in centralized contexts \[[1912.04977](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04977)\], there remains a critical gap in federated active learning: effectively selecting the most informative samples in the presence of client heterogeneity and fluctuating model parameters. CHASe addresses this gap by focusing on samples with high epistemic variation—those whose model predictions oscillate around decision boundaries during training—using novel techniques for tracking inference inconsistencies, calibrating decision boundaries, and efficient data selection via freezing and awakening mechanisms. This approach builds upon and complements the rich body of federated optimization and personalization literature, advancing the state-of-the-art in federated active learning \[[1905.10497](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10497)\].
