## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention as a solution to improve recommendation accuracy by leveraging users’ sequential interactions across multiple domains, particularly addressing data sparsity and cold-start issues prevalent in single-domain settings \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Most existing CDSR methods rely heavily on the presence of overlapping users who interact in multiple domains to transfer knowledge, which limits their applicability in practical scenarios where such user overlap is scarce or nonexistent—referred to as the overlap dilemma \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Furthermore, accurately modeling the complex transition patterns inherent in mixed cross-domain behavior sequences poses a substantial challenge, known as transition complexity \[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]\[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\].

Early CDSR approaches commonly learn single-domain user preferences independently and then transfer information via designed modules, often ignoring inter-sequence item relationships and leading to suboptimal cross-domain preference modeling \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. To address these limitations, graph-based and attentive mechanisms have been introduced to jointly capture intra- and inter-sequence item relationships, enabling more precise user preference representations \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. For example, Contrastive Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (C2DSR) integrates graph neural networks and sequential attentive encoders alongside contrastive learning objectives to enhance cross-domain user representation and overcome prior dataset issues \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Similarly, Dual Dynamic Graph frameworks model intra- and inter-domain sequential transitions through dynamic graphs combined with hybrid metric learning, improving alignment and uniformity of user-item embeddings \[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\].

To alleviate the reliance on overlapping users, recent research has proposed frameworks that work under open-world assumptions, where user overlap is partial or absent. The Adaptive Multi-Interest Debiasing framework (AMID) introduces a multi-interest information module and a doubly robust estimator to handle biases and distribution shifts in real-world datasets, demonstrating superior performance on large-scale financial platforms \[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. Other works explore federated learning and privacy-preserving mechanisms for non-overlapping cross-domain sequential recommendation, enabling knowledge transfer without compromising user privacy \[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\].

Addressing the transition complexity, methods such as Mixed Information Flow networks explicitly model both behavioral and knowledge transfer flows across domains, dynamically selecting relevant cross-domain information to enrich sequence representations \[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. Attention-based models, including Mixed Attention Networks (MAN) and Dual Attentive Sequential Learning (DASL), capture both local and global dependencies to better represent complex user transitions across domains \[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[2304.05027](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\]. Triple sequence learning further extends this by simultaneously modeling source, target, and mixed behavior sequences, employing triple cross-domain attention and contrastive learning to preserve information diversity and improve alignment \[[2304.05027](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\].

Beyond traditional neural architectures, there is increasing interest in leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for CDSR. LLMs offer powerful semantic representation and reasoning capabilities that can bridge item relationships and capture user preferences from a semantic perspective, potentially overcoming the overlap dilemma and transition complexity \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2303.14524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\]. Recent LLM-augmented recommender systems convert user behavior and profiles into prompts to enable conversational, interactive, and explainable recommendations, showing improvements in zero-shot and cross-domain settings \[[2303.14524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\]. Surveys on LLMs in recommendation highlight their advantages for embedding textual features and external knowledge, which can be integrated into CDSR frameworks for enhanced performance \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]. However, integrating LLMs with CDSR tasks requires careful adaptation, such as using trainable adapters with contrastive regularization and hierarchical profiling modules, to handle domain-specific characteristics and mixed user behaviors effectively \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\].

Contrastive learning has also emerged as a critical technique to improve sequential and cross-domain recommendations by generating self-supervised signals that enhance user representation quality and mitigate data sparsity \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. Models like DREAM employ supervised contrastive learning combined with focal loss to address class imbalance and inter-sequence relationships simultaneously \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. Similarly, CL4SRec improves sequential recommendation by leveraging multiple data augmentations and contrastive objectives \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\].

In summary, while existing CDSR approaches have made progress in modeling cross-domain user preferences and mitigating data sparsity, challenges remain in handling non-overlapping users and complex transition dynamics. The integration of LLMs and contrastive learning into CDSR presents promising avenues to address these challenges, by providing rich semantic item representations and robust user preference modeling across domains. Future research may further explore the synergy between LLMs, graph-based models, and contrastive frameworks to advance cross-domain sequential recommendation performance and applicability \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]\[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\].
