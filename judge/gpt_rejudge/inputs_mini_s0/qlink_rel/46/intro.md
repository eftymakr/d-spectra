## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention as a means to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems by leveraging user interaction data across multiple domains \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[2401.04971](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\]. Traditional single-domain sequential recommendation models, such as SASRec \[[1808.09781](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\] and BERT4Rec \[[1904.06690](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\], which rely heavily on self-attention mechanisms within Transformer architectures, capture user preferences by modeling intra-sequence item dependencies effectively. However, these models often do not explicitly address cross-domain knowledge transfer and the complex inter-sequence relationships present in multi-domain user behaviors.

Early CDSR methods typically follow a two-step approach: first learning single-domain user preferences from intra-sequence interactions and then transferring knowledge across domains via designed modules or latent space mapping \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. While effective to some extent, these approaches often suffer from limitations such as ignoring inter-sequence item relationships and the bottleneck effect caused by explicit knowledge transfer modules \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. To overcome these challenges, graph-based models have been introduced to capture both intra-domain and inter-domain relationships explicitly. For example, DA-GCN and TiDA-GCN represent users and items as nodes in domain-aware graphs and employ attention mechanisms to model user-specific preferences and temporal information, including time intervals between interactions \[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[2206.08050](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\]. Similarly, multi-view graph contrastive learning frameworks have been proposed to jointly learn user preferences from intra- and inter-domain views, improving representation quality and mitigating sparsity \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206)\].

Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful auxiliary technique to enhance sequential recommendation by deriving self-supervised signals from user behavior sequences. Models like CL4SRec and S3-Rec leverage contrastive objectives to improve user representation learning and mitigate data sparsity, while DREAM combines supervised contrastive learning with focal loss to address class imbalance and jointly learn single- and cross-domain preferences \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]\[[2008.07873](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. Such approaches have demonstrated substantial gains in recommendation accuracy by encouraging robust and discriminative embeddings.

Another prominent line of work focuses on capturing fine-grained cross-domain correlations through multi-sequence or multi-objective learning frameworks. Tri-CDR, for instance, models source, target, and mixed behavior sequences jointly with triple contrastive learning to align and preserve the diversity of user preferences across domains \[[2304.05027](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\]. Dual learning frameworks, such as DASL and DDTCDR, iteratively transfer knowledge bidirectionally between domains to enhance recommendations on both sides simultaneously \[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods address the limitations of unidirectional transfer and help reduce negative transfer effects.

Shared-account cross-domain sequential recommendation (SCSR) presents additional challenges due to multiple users sharing a single account, requiring disentangling of user-specific behaviors within and across domains. Graph-based solutions with domain-aware attention, such as DA-GCN and π-Net, as well as parallel split-join networks like PSJNet, have been proposed to tackle these challenges by filtering user-specific information and facilitating cross-domain knowledge sharing \[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[1910.02448](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]\[[2206.08050](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\].

While many CDSR approaches introduce domain-specific modules or auxiliary components to enhance model capacity, recent studies highlight the overlooked potential of the core self-attention mechanism itself. Models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec demonstrate that self-attention can effectively capture long-range dependencies and contextual item correlations within sequences \[[1808.09781](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]\[[1904.06690](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Recent works have begun to explore optimizing self-attention directly, for instance by incorporating time interval information (TiSASRec) or improving representation distribution via contrastive regularization (DuoRec) \[[2110.05730](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. These methods show that refining the self-attention module can lead to better performance without heavy architectural modifications.

Beyond model design, open-world assumptions are gaining traction, recognizing that real-world systems face non-overlapping users and dynamic data distributions. Frameworks like AMID propose adaptive multi-interest debiasing and doubly robust estimators to mitigate biases and handle long-tailed user groups effectively, enhancing CDSR robustness in practical settings \[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]\[[2311.04760](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\].

Finally, transfer learning and adversarial learning methods like CoNet and RecGURU aim to unify user representations across domains, reducing domain gaps and improving recommendation quality even with minimal user overlap \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[2111.10093](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.10093)\]. Bi-directional transfer graph collaborative filtering networks (BiTGCF) further fuse domain-specific and common user features to enhance cross-domain knowledge transfer \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\].

In summary, existing CDSR research spans a diverse set of techniques, including graph neural networks, contrastive learning, dual and multi-sequence learning, shared-account modeling, and self-attention enhancements. However, many approaches rely on auxiliary domain-specific components or explicit transfer modules, potentially limiting flexibility and incurring additional costs. Our work differentiates itself by focusing on directly improving the self-attention mechanism through a multi-objective optimization framework that dynamically balances recommendation performance and cross-domain attention, enabling automated, efficient knowledge transfer with minimal overhead \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[1808.09781](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]\[[1904.06690](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\].
