## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation for semantic segmentation (UDA-SS) focuses on transferring knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain to mitigate the domain shift problem caused by differences in data distribution. Traditional UDA-SS methods generally assume that the category sets in the source and target domains are identical and known, which is often unrealistic in practice. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes exist exclusively in the target domain, causing confusion in the segmentation outputs \[[1910.13049](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. Addressing this challenge requires methods capable of robust adaptation without prior knowledge of category overlap, a problem known as universal domain adaptation for semantic segmentation (UniDA-SS).

Early UDA methods aimed at reducing domain discrepancy through adversarial learning and feature alignment. Approaches such as adversarial training in the output or feature space have been effective in aligning global and category-level distributions, thereby improving cross-domain generalization \[[1809.09478](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]\[[1811.12833](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12833)\]\[[2007.09222](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.09222)\]. For example, category-level adversarial networks enforce semantic consistency and adapt class-specific feature distributions to reduce misalignment between source and target domains \[[1910.13049](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]\[[1809.09478](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]\[[2007.09222](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.09222)\]. Similarly, methods incorporating pixel-level adversarial loss and constraint-based adaptation leverage spatial structural similarities for domain alignment \[[1612.02649](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02649)\]. However, these methods typically presume known category sets, limiting their effectiveness under category shift scenarios.

Self-training with pseudo-labeling has emerged as a powerful strategy to adapt segmentation models to unlabeled target domains. These methods iteratively generate pseudo-labels on the target data and retrain the model to better fit the target distribution \[[2004.05498](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\]\[[2101.10979](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\]\[[2008.12197](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12197)\]. Recent advances have enhanced pseudo-label quality by integrating uncertainty estimation to rectify noisy labels \[[2003.03773](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03773)\], prototype-based denoising to refine class representations \[[2101.10979](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\], and instance-adaptive selectors to balance pseudo-label confidence \[[2008.12197](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12197)\]. While effective, these approaches often rely on the assumption that source and target share a common label space, which is violated in UniDA-SS contexts.

To tackle unknown or partially overlapping category settings, curriculum learning and prototype-based strategies have been proposed. Curriculum domain adaptation progressively learns domain properties, starting from global label distributions and moving towards finer-grained constraints, helping the model to adapt without explicit category alignment \[[1707.09465](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\]\[[1908.09547](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09547)\]. Prototype-guided methods explicitly model class centers or feature prototypes to guide domain alignment and pseudo-label refinement, offering robustness to category shifts \[[1910.13049](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]\[[2101.10979](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\]. For example, category anchor-guided adaptation utilizes source domain centroids as anchors for pseudo-labeling target features, improving discrimination between common and private classes \[[1910.13049](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\].

Techniques exploiting auxiliary tasks or multi-modal data, such as self-supervised depth estimation, have also shown promise for domain adaptation by providing complementary cues to semantic segmentation \[[1904.01886](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]\[[2104.13613](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13613)\]. Depth-aware adaptation frameworks leverage structured information to better align features across domains, facilitating more robust segmentation under domain shifts \[[1904.01886](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. Additionally, image translation and Fourier domain adaptation methods reduce low-level appearance discrepancies by manipulating image statistics or transforming style components, thus easing feature alignment \[[2004.05498](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\]\[[1711.03213](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.03213)\]\[[1712.00479](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00479)\].

Recent works highlight the benefits of progressive, fine-grained, and class-aware adaptation strategies that consider domain-specific feature distributions. For example, separating semantic features and applying class-wise adversarial reweighting addresses imbalance and unstable adversarial training observed in prior works \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\]\[[2003.08040](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08040)\]. Bidirectional learning frameworks that iteratively refine image translation and segmentation models further enhance adaptation performance \[[1904.10620](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. Moreover, source-free adaptation frameworks enable model transfer without accessing source data, which is practical when source data privacy is a concern \[[2103.16372](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\].

Despite these advances, most existing UDA-SS methods focus on scenarios with known or partially known category sets. The proposed UniMAP framework extends this line of research by explicitly addressing the UniDA-SS problem through domain-specific prototype distinction and target-based image matching, enabling effective discrimination of common and private classes without prior category knowledge. This approach complements and builds upon prototype-based adaptation and self-training paradigms while introducing novel mechanisms for matching and separating domain-specific features, thereby improving robustness and generalization in challenging universal adaptation scenarios.
