## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking and recommendation systems has attracted significant research interest, particularly focusing on the inherent trade-offs between ranking accuracy and fairness. Early studies identified that optimizing for user utility alone often leads to unfair exposure distribution among items or providers, motivating fairness-aware ranking and re-ranking algorithms that explicitly balance these competing objectives.

A foundational line of work addresses fairness by modeling exposure as a limited resource to be allocated fairly among items or providers. For example, P-MMF formulates provider fairness as a max-min fairness problem, prioritizing providers with the least exposure to improve market stability, outperforming previous proportion fairness-based approaches through an online re-ranking model that dynamically balances user preferences and provider fairness \[[1802.07281](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]\[[2204.08085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. Similarly, CPFair integrates fairness constraints for both consumers and producers in a joint optimization framework, improving two-sided fairness without compromising recommendation quality \[[1903.0078](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]\[[2104.09024](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\].

Several approaches adopt fairness-aware re-ranking as a post-processing step to mediate between accuracy and fairness. The Fairness-Aware Re-ranking (FAR) algorithm iteratively trades off accuracy and provider coverage, incorporating personalized user tolerance for provider diversity to tailor fairness interventions according to user receptivity \[[1805.01788](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]\[[1809.02921](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Other work extends this by introducing fairness representations for demographic groups, using diversity-based re-ranking methods such as fairness-aware Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) to improve fairness while maintaining relevance \[[2005.14713](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\]\[[1809.03577](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03577)\].

The conceptual framing of fairness in rankings has also been linked to economic theories, notably through analogies to taxation. Re-ranking fairness constraints can be viewed as a "commodity tax" imposed on item providers, which translates into a cost borne by consumers in the form of reduced accuracy. This trade-off is governed by the elasticity of utility between item groups, analogous to elasticity in economic tax incidence. Building upon this insight, recent work developed the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) as an evaluation framework for fair re-ranking algorithms, enabling performance comparison across different elasticity levels. ElasticRank, a novel algorithm, leverages elasticity calculations to adjust inter-item distances in a curved space, achieving improved fairness-accuracy trade-offs informed by this economic perspective \[[1104.3913](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]\[[2002.10764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\].

Beyond individual fairness metrics, several studies emphasize the importance of multi-stakeholder or two-sided fairness, recognizing that recommender systems serve both consumers and producers simultaneously. FairRec guarantees fairness through Maximin Share and Envy-Free up to One Good (EF1) criteria for producers and customers, respectively, ensuring equitable exposure and satisfaction across both sides \[[2002.10764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]\[[2110.15781](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15781)\]. Other frameworks formulate fairness constraints as part of dynamic learning-to-rank models, incorporating merit-based group fairness guarantees while adapting to implicit feedback data, thus maintaining fairness throughout evolving recommendation environments \[[2005.14713](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\]\[[2101.03584](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03584)\].

The dynamic and long-term nature of fairness in recommendation systems has also been explored. Approaches such as fairness-constrained reinforcement learning model recommendation as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP), enabling continuous adaptation to shifting item popularity and user engagement patterns to maintain fairness over time \[[2101.03584](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03584)\]. This contrasts with static, one-shot fairness solutions that may fail to capture temporal fairness dynamics.

In addition, fairness has been studied from the standpoint of individual fairness and awareness. The principle of treating similar individuals similarly, formalized through task-specific similarity metrics, provides a foundation for classification and ranking fairness that subsumes group-level fairness notions \[[1104.3913](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. Other works focus on fairness through exposure allocation, proposing frameworks that implement a broad range of fairness constraints, including demographic parity and disparate impact, while optimizing for user utility \[[1802.07281](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]\[[1905.01989](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01989)\].

Recent advances also propose fairness metrics based on pairwise comparisons derived from randomized experiments, which offer tractable means to quantify and improve fairness during model training via regularization, demonstrating practical improvements in large-scale recommender systems \[[1903.0078](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]\[[1705.08804](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08804)\]. Furthermore, some work considers user-group fairness, highlighting disparities in recommendation quality between active and inactive users, and proposes re-ranking methods that improve fairness for disadvantaged user groups without harming overall system performance \[[2104.10671](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10671)\].

Finally, fairness-aware ranking algorithms have been successfully deployed at scale, such as LinkedIn Talent Search, where fairness constraints were integrated without sacrificing business metrics, demonstrating the feasibility and societal impact of fairness-aware ranking in real-world applications \[[1905.01989](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01989)\].

Collectively, these works demonstrate a rich landscape of fairness-aware ranking and re-ranking methods, spanning theoretical frameworks, algorithmic solutions, dynamic adaptations, and practical deployments. The current research on elasticity-informed fairness evaluation and ElasticRank builds on and extends these foundations by providing a nuanced understanding of the fairness-accuracy trade-off through an economic lens, thereby addressing limitations in existing fairness assessments and enabling more informed algorithm selection \[[1104.3913](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]\[[2002.10764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\].
