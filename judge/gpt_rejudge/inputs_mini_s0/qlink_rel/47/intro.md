## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a pivotal approach in recommender systems, aiming to leverage knowledge from multiple domains to enhance recommendation quality across various scenarios \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Early cross-domain recommendation (CDR) methods primarily focused on single-target or dual-target settings, often assuming the existence of a richer source domain or domain-invariant user preferences \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. However, these assumptions are often unrealistic in real-world applications where sparsity pervades all domains, and user preferences vary significantly across domains \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]\[[2103.01696](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\].

To address the limitations of single-target CDR, recent research has explored multi-target and multi-domain recommendation frameworks that jointly improve recommendations across multiple domains. For example, the Multi-Sparse-Domain Collaborative Recommendation (MSDCR) model treats all involved domains as sparse and simultaneously enhances recommendation performance by combining domain-specific and domain-invariant user preferences, preserving domain uniqueness through adversarial training \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Similarly, frameworks like DTCDR and GA extend dual-target CDR by exploiting multi-task learning and graph-based embedding integration to improve recommendations on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]\[[2108.07976](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07976)\].

Another important line of work focuses on modeling the complex transfer relationships between domains to avoid the negative transfer problem (NTP), where knowledge from irrelevant or conflicting domains harms performance. Negative transfer is a well-recognized challenge in MDR and CDR, arising from domain conflicts or poor-quality domains \[[2103.01696](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]\[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb919954e93726aff490458bd61d6b3d8155fbd7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb919954e93726aff490458bd61d6b3d8155fbd7)\]\[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. To mitigate this, selective knowledge transfer strategies have been proposed. For example, some methods utilize domain similarity metrics or domain-specific discriminators to identify and transfer knowledge only from relevant domains, reducing the risk of NTP \[[2103.01696](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]\[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]\[[1203.3535](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.3535)\]. The recent method EDDA explicitly disentangles embeddings into inter-domain and intra-domain components and applies domain alignment via random walks to enhance knowledge transfer while avoiding interference \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\].

Parameter sharing strategies in MDR models also significantly impact negative transfer. Many approaches explicitly share parameters across domains, which may cause mutual interference due to distribution differences \[[2309.06217](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]\[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. To overcome this, models like HAMUR introduce domain-specific adapters whose parameters are dynamically generated by a shared hyper-network, allowing flexible and adaptive knowledge sharing \[[2309.06217](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. Moreover, the STAR model factorizes domain networks into shared and domain-specific components combined multiplicatively, enabling simultaneous capture of commonalities and domain-specific characteristics \[[2101.11427](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11427)\].

Graph-based methods have also been employed to capture high-order structural information and facilitate cross-domain knowledge transfer. BiTGCF uses graph collaborative filtering with bi-directional transfer layers to fuse domain-specific and common user features, improving bi-directional recommendations \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]. Preference Propagation GraphNet (PPGN) simplifies cross-domain recommendation into a unified multi-task learning framework, leveraging a cross-domain preference matrix to propagate user preferences across domains while maintaining optimization efficiency \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\].

In addition, deep transfer learning and dual learning mechanisms have been explored. CoNet employs collaborative cross networks with cross mappings in hidden layers for dual knowledge transfer across two domains \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. DDTCDR introduces a dual learning framework combined with latent orthogonal mapping and autoencoders to iteratively transfer user and item information between domains, preserving latent relations \[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Further, CAT-ART extends this concept to multi-target CDR by learning global user embeddings via contrastive autoencoders and adaptively transferring domain-specific embeddings with attention mechanisms \[[2211.11964](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11964)\].

Multi-task learning (MTL) techniques also play a crucial role in MDR by jointly optimizing domain-specific and shared objectives. Progressive Layered Extraction (PLE) explicitly separates shared and task-specific components with a progressive routing mechanism to alleviate negative transfer and improve joint representation learning \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0182197c3996d11867ef650b66a2ddf1efa6f631](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0182197c3996d11867ef650b66a2ddf1efa6f631)\]. Similarly, frameworks like DTCDR and GA employ adaptable embedding-sharing and element-wise attention mechanisms to balance domain conflicts and enhance generalizability across domains \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]\[[2108.07976](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07976)\].

Finally, side information such as reviews and item contents have been fused with rating data to improve cold-start and cross-domain recommendation performance. For instance, RC-DFM integrates review texts and item contents via stacked denoising autoencoders and multi-layer perceptrons to enrich latent factors for both auxiliary and target domains \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/54dedd4d06f36a00cde7084c1cd1a92ad8cf9168](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/54dedd4d06f36a00cde7084c1cd1a92ad8cf9168)\]. Adversarial feature translation methods like AFT further enhance domain-specific feature learning by translating features between domains within a generative adversarial network framework \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59)\].

In summary, the literature demonstrates extensive efforts to tackle the challenges of knowledge transfer, domain heterogeneity, and negative transfer in multi-domain recommendation. However, explicit and dynamic domain-level selection mechanisms to measure domain relationships and selectively transfer knowledge remain underexplored. Our proposed Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) addresses this gap by introducing a prototype-based domain distance measure and dynamic domain selection, complementing and advancing existing MDR approaches.
