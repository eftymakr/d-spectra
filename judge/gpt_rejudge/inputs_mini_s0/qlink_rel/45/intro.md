## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has gained traction as an important extension of traditional sentiment analysis, which often categorizes text into broad classes such as positive, negative, or neutral. While sentiment polarity provides a coarse understanding of user opinions, fine-grained emotion classification seeks to identify specific emotional states—such as joy, anger, fear, or surprise—offering richer insights into user feedback \[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. This distinction is particularly relevant in app reviews, where detailed emotional cues can inform software maintenance, feature prioritization, and user experience improvements \[[1909.05504](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.05504)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572)\].

Several large-scale datasets have been developed to facilitate fine-grained emotion detection from text. For example, GoEmotions provides a manually annotated dataset of 58,000 English Reddit comments labeled with 27 emotion categories plus neutral, enabling models to learn nuanced emotional distinctions \[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Similarly, EmoNet introduced a large dataset and deep learning models achieving state-of-the-art accuracy on 24 fine-grained emotions, expanding the task to Plutchik’s primary emotion dimensions with accuracies exceeding 95% \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. These datasets demonstrate the feasibility of fine-grained emotion classification but mostly focus on social media or general text rather than app reviews specifically.

In the domain of app reviews, automated classification of user feedback traditionally centers on broader categories such as problem reports, feature requests, or irrelevant content, often using supervised machine learning or deep learning methods \[[1909.05504](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.05504)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3fb416be9e59b35c7cc91cd11b5b3f3f5491f13a](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3fb416be9e59b35c7cc91cd11b5b3f3f5491f13a)\]. While these approaches efficiently handle large volumes of reviews, they rarely address fine-grained emotional nuance. Some works apply sentiment analysis or polarity detection to app reviews, revealing the potential of emotion as an informative feature for developers \[[1703.02256](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\]. However, challenges remain due to the noisy, informal nature of app review text and the complexity of accurately interpreting emotional content in this context.

To tackle the volume and noisiness of app reviews, frameworks like AR-Miner have been proposed, combining filtering, topic modeling, and ranking to extract informative reviews for developers \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572)\]. Keyword-based approaches such as MARK assist in semi-automated analysis by identifying and ranking relevant review content based on keywords associated with negative sentiment or specific issues \[[1505.04657](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04657)\]. These techniques complement emotion classification by focusing on extracting actionable feedback but do not explicitly model emotion categories.

Emotion annotation frameworks have been developed for various text domains, emphasizing the need for clear guidelines and high-quality annotated data to train reliable models. For example, PO-EMO explored aesthetic emotions in poetry, highlighting the complexity of mixed and subtle emotions and the challenges of inter-annotator agreement \[[2003.07723](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]. Similarly, EmoBank uses dimensional emotion representations (Valence-Arousal-Dominance) alongside categorical labels to capture nuanced affective states, showing the benefits of multi-perspective annotation \[[2205.01996](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01996)\]. These frameworks inform annotation strategies for app reviews, where emotional expressions may be ambiguous or mixed.

Large language models (LLMs) and transformer-based architectures have shown promise in automating emotion detection. BERT-based models achieve moderate F1-scores on emotion classification tasks, demonstrating the potential but also the limitations of current techniques \[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]\[[2003.07723](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]. While LLMs can substantially reduce manual annotation effort and achieve reasonable agreement with human annotators, full automation remains challenging due to the subjective and context-dependent nature of emotional interpretation \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]\[[1703.02256](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\]. This gap motivates hybrid approaches combining human expertise with automated methods.

Lexical resources such as SentiWordNet and crowdsourced emotion lexicons support emotion and sentiment analysis by providing word-level affective scores, aiding model generalization and interpretability \[[1308.6297](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96761dc3d8adaeff44eb9c07501eb3109802ee4b](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96761dc3d8adaeff44eb9c07501eb3109802ee4b)\]. However, these resources typically capture polarity or basic emotions rather than fine-grained or mixed emotional states, which are more prevalent in user-generated content like app reviews.

Overall, while extensive research exists on sentiment and coarse emotion detection in text, fine-grained emotion classification in app reviews is underexplored. Existing datasets and models from social media and literary domains provide valuable insights and methodologies, but adapting these to the specific challenges of app review text—such as informal language, domain-specific jargon, and mixed emotions—requires tailored annotation frameworks and evaluation. Recent advances in LLMs offer promising avenues for semi-automated annotation but necessitate careful consideration of annotation guidelines and model limitations to ensure reliable emotion classification in app review mining.
