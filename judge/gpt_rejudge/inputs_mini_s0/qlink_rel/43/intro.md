## Related Works

The training and optimization of large language models (LLMs) in distributed environments have been extensively studied, focusing on scalability, efficiency, and performance modeling. Early distributed training approaches prioritized communication efficiency and system scalability but often lacked detailed models to predict complex execution behaviors accurately \[[2005.14165](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. Techniques such as asynchronous distributed optimization with redundancy in cost functions have improved resilience and communication by allowing partial agent participation without degrading convergence, which is critical in large-scale setups \[[2005.14165](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. Gradient-based optimization methods like Gumbel-Softmax have introduced flexible ways to handle mixed categorical and continuous variables in system tuning, further advancing distributed training strategies \[[2305.14314](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)\].

Recent advances have underscored the importance of performance modeling frameworks that accurately capture the runtime characteristics of LLM training and inference. For instance, distributed inference optimization techniques tailored to specific hardware architectures (e.g., CPUs) demonstrate significant reductions in latency by exploiting hardware-aware strategies \[[2304.12244](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. Systems like LLM-Pilot provide frameworks for characterizing and predicting inference performance across hardware and deployment configurations, enabling cost-performance trade-off analyses \[[2304.08485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. These efforts highlight the growing need for models that can generalize across different architectures and workloads.

The evolution of LLMs themselves has been closely linked to scaling model parameters and instruction tuning to improve generalization and task adaptability. Scaling studies, such as those involving GPT-3 and PaLM, reveal that increasing model size and training data volume leads to significant improvements in few-shot and zero-shot learning capabilities, including complex reasoning and multilingual tasks \[[2005.14165](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]\[[2204.02311](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. Instruction tuning approaches, exemplified by FLAN and Flan-PaLM, show that fine-tuning on large collections of natural language instructions substantially boosts zero-shot generalization, outperforming larger models without instruction tuning \[[2109.01652](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]\[[2210.11416](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416)\]. Moreover, fine-tuning methodologies like QLoRA enable efficient adaptation of quantized models on limited hardware while preserving performance, facilitating broader experimentation with large models \[[2305.14314](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)\].

The integration of AI-generated instruction data has emerged as a promising direction to circumvent the bottleneck of manual data creation. Models like WizardLM leverage iterative AI-driven instruction evolution to fine-tune LLMs effectively, achieving competitive performance relative to strong baselines such as ChatGPT \[[2304.12244](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. Similarly, visual instruction tuning has extended these concepts to multimodal models, demonstrating that language-only instruction data generated by GPT-4 can train models like LLaVA to perform robust multimodal understanding and reasoning \[[2304.08485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. These data-centric approaches provide valuable context for modeling execution behavior since instruction tuning and fine-tuning affect the runtime characteristics of LLM workloads.

Performance evaluation and benchmarking of LLMs have also evolved, with frameworks like MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena employing LLMs themselves as judges to approximate human preferences reliably. These methods offer scalable and explainable evaluation alternatives, crucial for assessing system optimizations in distributed training environments \[[2306.05685](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)\]. Such evaluation techniques complement traditional benchmarks by providing nuanced insights into model behavior and user-aligned performance.

Finally, large-scale LLM training requires sophisticated infrastructure and optimization systems that can predict and manage execution across diverse hardware configurations. The GPT-4 technical report emphasizes the role of infrastructure and optimization methods in achieving predictable performance scaling at large compute budgets \[[2303.08774](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\]. Surveys of LLM development further identify open challenges related to efficient scaling, alignment, and evaluation that intersect with performance modeling needs \[[2303.18223](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18223)\].

In this context, the proposed Lumos toolkit contributes a trace-driven approach to performance modeling that captures detailed execution dynamics of modern LLMs across large GPU clusters. By enabling accurate replay and estimation of runtime behavior for different models and configurations, Lumos fills a gap in existing performance characterization tools, facilitating more effective optimization and system-level studies for distributed LLM training.
