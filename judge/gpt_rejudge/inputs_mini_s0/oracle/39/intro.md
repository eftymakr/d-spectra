## Related Works

De novo genome assembly is a critical bioinformatics task that reconstructs unknown genomes from sequencing reads, enabling advancements in personalized medicine and disease research. Traditional assemblers typically rely on de Bruijn graph or overlap-layout-consensus methods, but these approaches face significant challenges in managing large datasets due to high memory consumption and computational complexity \[[1409.7208](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7208)\]. Addressing these limitations has motivated research into scalable algorithms, hardware acceleration, and system-level optimizations.

MEGAHIT is a notable example of a single-node assembler optimized for large and complex metagenomics datasets. It employs a succinct de Bruijn graph representation to efficiently assemble data without prior partitioning or normalization, thus preserving assembly integrity. MEGAHIT demonstrated the ability to assemble a 252 Gbp soil metagenomics dataset in under two days on a single node and achieved a larger and more contiguous assembly compared to previous methods, with significantly higher read alignment rates \[[1409.7208](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7208)\]. This approach highlights the potential of algorithmic innovations to improve assembly quality while maintaining resource efficiency.

In parallel, alignment tools such as Minimap2 have been developed to handle ultra-long reads and large reference sequences efficiently. Minimap2 supports mapping of diverse data types including short accurate reads, noisy long reads, and full-length transcript sequences with high speed and accuracy. It introduces heuristics optimized for split-read alignments and long indels, surpassing traditional short-read mappers by 3-4 times in speed at comparable accuracy and over 30 times faster at higher accuracy levels. Such alignment advancements are critical for downstream assembly and validation tasks, especially as sequencing technologies produce increasingly long and noisy reads \[[1708.01492](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01492)\].

Beyond software, distributed-memory parallelization has been explored to scale genome assembly across clusters, as seen in frameworks like HipMER and PPA-assembler, which leverage graph processing models to partition data and parallelize computations. Although effective in handling large datasets, these systems still contend with memory-bound operations and irregular data access patterns intrinsic to de novo assembly, which limits their overall efficiency and scalability.

To overcome these bottlenecks, recent research has turned to hardware-software co-design and near-memory processing (NMP) architectures to both reduce memory footprint and accelerate computation. Custom processing elements integrated close to memory resources can mitigate data movement overheads and exploit parallelism inherent in graph-based assembly algorithms. Software strategies such as customized batch processing and hybrid CPU-NMP execution help manage dynamic, interdependent data structures and irregular workloads, addressing underutilization issues common in traditional CPU-centric pipelines.

The proposed NMP-PaK system exemplifies this approach by achieving a 14-fold reduction in memory footprint and a 16-fold performance improvement over CPU baselines, with a 2.4-fold reduction in memory operations. This results in an 8.3-fold throughput gain compared to state-of-the-art de novo assemblers under equivalent resource constraints, demonstrating the effectiveness of channel-level NMP architectures combined with tailored software optimizations for scalable genome assembly.

In summary, while tools like MEGAHIT and Minimap2 have advanced algorithmic efficiency and alignment capabilities for large-scale sequencing data \[[1409.7208](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7208)\]\[[1708.01492](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01492)\], and distributed systems provide parallel scalability, fundamental challenges remain due to memory bottlenecks and irregular data patterns. Hardware-software co-design approaches such as NMP-PaK address these issues by tightly coupling processing and memory, offering promising avenues for future scalable, efficient de novo genome assembly.
