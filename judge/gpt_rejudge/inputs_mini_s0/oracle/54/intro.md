## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become fundamental in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems by leveraging user-item interaction graphs to encode complex preference patterns \[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]\[[1905.08108](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]\[[2102.10044](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10044)\]. However, traditional GCN-based CF models often rely on learned embeddings that map users and items into a latent space with limited capacity, which can overlook locally observed but potentially valuable preference signals \[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]\[[1905.08108](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Moreover, conventional neighborhood aggregation schemes are typically linear and uniform, restricting the ability to capture diverse and fine-grained preference patterns \[[1810.00826](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)\]\[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]\[[2102.10044](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10044)\].

Several works have tackled these limitations by improving model expressiveness and addressing over-smoothing, a prevalent problem where node embeddings become indistinguishable with deeper layers. For instance, residual network structures have been proposed to alleviate over-smoothing in CF by explicitly modeling user-item interactions and enabling deeper propagation without degrading performance \[[1810.00826](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)\]\[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. LightGCN and LR-GCN simplify GCN architectures by removing non-linearities and collapsing weight matrices, demonstrating that linear models can outperform more complex ones while scaling efficiently to large datasets \[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]\[[2102.10044](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10044)\]. Further studies confirm that linear propagation often yields better accuracy than non-linear variants in recommender systems, especially when combined with mechanisms that adaptively balance linear and non-linear components based on node centrality \[[2111.07265](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07265)\].

Graph spectral filtering, which views GCN operations as graph signal processing (GSP), has offered a principled perspective on CF. Spectral methods interpret GCNs as low-pass filters smoothing user-item interaction signals on graphs, which explains the empirical success of many CF models as variants of graph filtering with different frequency responses \[[2108.07567](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07567)\]\[[2302.02113](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02113)\]\[[2406.08827](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08827)\]. These insights have motivated novel CF methods that directly manipulate graph signals without relying on learned embeddings. For example, GF-CF computes recommendations via closed-form graph filters, achieving competitive or superior performance compared to trained GCNs \[[2108.07567](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07567)\]. Personalized graph signal processing extends this by combining low- and high-frequency components to better preserve informative signals that are otherwise lost in pure low-pass filtering \[[2302.02113](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02113)\]. Recent advancements propose adaptive graph normalization and individualized graph filters to redistribute noise across the spectral domain and enable flexible representation generation, overcoming limitations of classic linear GCNs \[[2406.08827](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08827)\].

Chebyshev polynomial approximations have been widely adopted to efficiently realize flexible, non-linear graph filters that approximate spectral convolutions without expensive eigendecompositions \[[1607.00653](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. Their ability to capture higher-order neighborhood information with controllable filter smoothness makes them suitable for complex graph signal processing tasks. While earlier works applied Chebyshev filters mainly in network embedding and image denoising, their potential for CF remains underexplored \[[1607.00653](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. Integrating Chebyshev interpolation with ideal pass filters and degree-based normalization, as proposed in our framework, enables approximating more expressive non-linear filters that can leverage the full frequency spectrum of user interactions beyond the typical low-pass assumption.

Besides spectral filtering, various graph neural architectures have been proposed to enhance collaborative filtering by incorporating user intent disentanglement \[[2007.01764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\], interest-aware message passing \[[2102.10044](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10044)\], and collaboration-aware convolution mechanisms \[[2207.06221](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06221)\]. Contrastive learning techniques have also been leveraged to improve representation robustness and alleviate data sparsity by constructing informative positive pairs based on neighborhood and semantic similarity \[[2202.062](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]\[[2305.10837](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10837)\]. Furthermore, some studies revisit collaborative filtering as a link prediction problem on bipartite graphs, achieving remarkable gains by focusing on edge-level modeling rather than node embeddings \[[2203.15789](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15789)\]. Diffusion models combined with graph signal processing have been recently introduced, effectively modeling user-item correlations and enabling noise-free reconstruction of interaction signals \[[2311.08744](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08744)\].

Overall, while previous GCN-based CF methods have primarily focused on linear or simplified graph convolution and embedding learning, the integration of spectral graph theory, flexible polynomial filtering, and adaptive normalization presents a promising direction to overcome expressiveness and capacity bottlenecks. Our approach builds on these advances by employing Chebyshev spectral filtering to fully exploit the frequency spectrum of user interactions, enhancing the model’s ability to capture diverse and fine-grained preference patterns.
