## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking and recommendation has garnered significant attention, particularly in addressing the inherent trade-offs between ranking accuracy and fairness toward both consumers and producers. Early research primarily focused on fairness from a single side—either customers or providers—often leading to imbalanced outcomes where improving fairness for one side diminished it for the other \[[2104.09024](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]\[[2002.10764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]. Recognizing this limitation, recent works adopt a two-sided fairness perspective, aiming to balance the interests of consumers and producers simultaneously in recommendation and ranking systems \[[2104.09024](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]\[[2201.0118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\]\[[2502.11883](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11883)\]\[[2002.10764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\].

Two-sided fairness models such as TFROM and FairRec formalize fairness guarantees for both parties. TFROM explicitly considers provider exposure fairness alongside the preservation of recommendation quality for customers, demonstrating improved personalization with balanced fairness \[[2104.09024](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]. Similarly, FairRec guarantees Maximin Share (MMS) exposure for producers and Envy-Free up to One item (EF1) fairness for customers, framing the recommendation problem as a fair allocation of indivisible goods \[[2201.0118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\]\[[2002.10764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]. Extensions like FairRecPlus further optimize customer utility while retaining these fairness guarantees at the expense of additional computation \[[2201.0118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\].

Optimization-based re-ranking approaches have been developed to integrate fairness constraints from both sides within a joint objective framework. CPFair employs such an optimization method to enhance consumer and producer fairness without sacrificing overall recommendation quality, supported by large-scale experimental validation \[[2502.11883](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11883)\]. Other frameworks extend these ideas by incorporating demographic fairness and group representation to assure equitable outcomes in ranked results \[[2204.08085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].

The trade-off between accuracy and fairness in rankings has been examined through economic analogies, particularly the concept of taxation. Tax-rank models fairness constraints as item-level taxes where ranking resources are reallocated to achieve fairness, optimized efficiently via the Sinkhorn algorithm. This approach ensures desirable properties such as continuity (small fairness adjustments lead to small accuracy changes) and controllability over accuracy loss, addressing limitations of prior methods \[[2201.0118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\]\[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. These economic perspectives have been extended by axiomatic approaches that use fair division principles. For example, frameworks based on Nash Social Welfare optimize rankings to achieve envy-freeness and Pareto optimality, overcoming the shortcomings of exposure-based fairness metrics that can cause envy and disparate impacts \[[2206.07247](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07247)\].

Recent works also emphasize the importance of multi-stakeholder fairness, recognizing recommender systems as two-sided or multi-sided marketplaces where the interests of multiple parties must be balanced \[[1907.13158](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.13158)\]. This perspective aligns with the need for fairness-aware policy learning in ranking, where stochastic ranking policies are optimized to satisfy fairness constraints over items while maintaining utility to users, as demonstrated by algorithms like Fair-PG-Rank \[[1902.04056](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04056)\].

Beyond theoretical formulations, practical algorithms have been proposed to improve the fairness-performance trade-off. VerFair, for example, uses vertical allocation techniques to amortize fair exposure in rankings, leveraging consumer prior knowledge to outperform existing fair ranking methods both at individual and group levels \[[2204.03046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03046)\]. Similarly, optimizing generalized Gini indices as fairness criteria enables recommender systems to focus on the least satisfied users or minimize inequality in item exposure, employing non-smooth optimization techniques to handle non-differentiability challenges \[[2204.06521](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06521)\].

While many evaluations of fair re-ranking focus on single fairness metrics, recent research highlights the need for more comprehensive frameworks. The concept of elasticity, drawn from economics, offers a nuanced understanding of how fairness constraints (viewed as commodity taxes on suppliers) translate into user accuracy loss (commodity taxes on consumers). The Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) evaluation framework quantifies this relationship across different elasticity levels, enabling more informed comparisons of fair re-ranking algorithms and guiding the design of elasticity-aware methods like ElasticRank, which adjusts inter-item distances in a latent space to balance fairness and accuracy effectively \[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].

Finally, standardized toolkits such as FairDiverse have emerged to unify benchmarking of fairness and diversity algorithms across information retrieval and recommendation tasks. These toolkits facilitate consistent evaluation and development of fairness-aware models, promoting reproducibility and comparability in the field \[[2502.11883](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11883)\].

In summary, the landscape of fairness-aware re-ranking and recommendation is marked by a shift from single-sided fairness to holistic two-sided and multi-stakeholder fairness frameworks. Economic analogies and optimization-based methods have enriched the theoretical foundation, while practical algorithms and evaluation frameworks continue to advance the understanding and application of fairness in ranking systems.
