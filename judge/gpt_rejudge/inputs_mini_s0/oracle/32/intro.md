## Related Works

Understanding how feature representations evolve across layers in large language models (LLMs) has been a focal point for improving interpretability, robustness, and transferability. Early efforts mostly relied on data-dependent probing of fine-tuned models, which provided valuable insights but were limited to post-hoc analyses after task-specific adaptation \[[2412.09563](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09563)\]\[[2109.08406](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08406)\]. These studies revealed that intermediate layers often hold richer semantic information than final layers and that layers cluster into groups with distinct representational roles. However, they did not fully address the intrinsic properties of pre-fine-tuned models or offer methods to identify critical layers independent of downstream tasks.

To overcome these limitations, data-oblivious approaches have gained traction, notably using Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) to measure similarity and shifts in representation spaces across layers and training phases \[[1905.00414](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00414)\]\[[1706.05806](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.05806)\]\[[2109.08406](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08406)\]. CKA has proven effective in reliably identifying correspondences between representations in networks trained from different initializations and architectures, outperforming canonical correlation analysis (CCA) variants, which often fail in high-dimensional settings \[[1905.00414](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00414)\]\[[1806.05759](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05759)\]. Recent work has applied CKA to fine-tuned Transformers, uncovering block diagonal similarity structures and demonstrating that later layers contribute marginally to task performance, enabling pruning without significant loss \[[2109.08406](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08406)\].

The identification of critical layers—those layers that undergo significant representational shifts during fine-tuning—has been linked to their functional importance in LLMs. Empirical evidence shows that these layers mediate key semantic transitions, such as moving from rationale-like representations to conclusion-oriented embeddings \[[2412.09563](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09563)\]\[[2404.07066](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07066)\]. Spectral analysis of these shifts reveals that top principal components in these layers encode such semantic transitions, aligning with findings that complex concepts and higher-level abstractions manifest in deeper layers, while simpler features are encoded earlier \[[2404.07066](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07066)\]. This layered progression of concept complexity supports the notion that critical layers are intrinsic to the model’s architecture and play a central role in task adaptation.

Beyond interpretability, understanding critical layers informs practical applications. For domain adaptation, selectively fine-tuning critical layers leads to more substantial reductions in loss compared to tuning non-critical layers, consistent with the observed sensitivity of these layers to knowledge injection and adaptation \[[2410.02330](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02330)\]\[[2404.07066](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07066)\]. In security contexts, freezing critical layers during backdoor defense substantially reduces attack success rates—up to 40% in reported experiments—demonstrating that these layers are also vulnerable points for adversarial manipulation and thus targets for robustification \[[https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.293](https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.293)\].

Complementary analyses using representation dissimilarity measures further elucidate how model depth and width affect learning dynamics and generalization properties, revealing asymmetries linked to activation functions and architectural choices \[[2310.14993](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14993)\]\[[https://openreview.net/forum?id=KJNcAkY8tY4](https://openreview.net/forum?id=KJNcAkY8tY4)\]. Such findings emphasize the nuanced interplay between architectural design and representational evolution, reinforcing the importance of targeting specific layers for interventions like pruning, editing, or fine-tuning.

Additionally, studies into the internal mechanisms of LLMs highlight the role of mid-layer feed-forward modules in storing and recalling factual knowledge, which can be directly manipulated for model editing and factual association updates \[[2202.05262](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05262)\]. This aligns with evidence that shallow layers are critical for knowledge injection, as demonstrated in recent work on selective layer enhancement and pruning strategies that prioritize shallow layers for efficiency gains \[[2410.02330](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02330)\].

Finally, the capability of internal states to encode meta-information, such as truthfulness of generated statements, suggests that intermediate representations carry rich semantic and factual signals that can be exploited for reliability improvements and interpretability \[[2304.13734](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13734)\]. This further underscores the multifaceted importance of understanding representation dynamics across layers.

In summary, prior work has established the foundational role of intermediate and critical layers in LLMs’ representational geometry, their evolution during training and fine-tuning, and their impact on downstream task performance and robustness. Our approach advances this line of research by leveraging CKA to identify intrinsic critical layers in pre-fine-tuned LLMs, linking representation shifts to practical applications in domain adaptation and security defense.
