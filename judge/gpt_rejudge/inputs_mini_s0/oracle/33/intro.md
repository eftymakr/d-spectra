## Related Works

Catastrophic forgetting remains a fundamental challenge in continual learning (CL), where models are expected to learn a sequence of tasks without losing performance on previously learned ones. Rehearsal-based methods, which store and replay subsets of past data, have been widely adopted due to their empirical effectiveness in mitigating forgetting. These methods typically train on a mixture of old and new data concurrently, maintaining a memory buffer to support knowledge retention \[[1705.0869](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]\[[1611.07725](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]\[[1706.0884](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. However, the optimality of concurrent rehearsal has been questioned, motivating investigations into alternative strategies such as sequential rehearsal.

Sequential rehearsal, inspired by human learning processes where tasks are revisited in sequence rather than simultaneously, has been theoretically and empirically explored in recent works. Theoretical analyses in overparameterized linear models demonstrate that sequential rehearsal—training new data first, followed by revisiting past data—can outperform concurrent rehearsal when tasks exhibit low similarity, by better balancing forgetting and generalization \[[2205.09588](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09588)\]\[[2405.17583](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\]. These findings are supported by empirical experiments on deep neural networks, which confirm that sequential rehearsal reduces forgetting and enhances generalization, especially for dissimilar tasks \[[2405.17583](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\].

The hybrid rehearsal approach builds upon these insights by combining concurrent rehearsal for similar tasks and sequential rehearsal for dissimilar tasks, aiming to leverage the advantages of both strategies. This approach has shown improved performance over standard concurrent rehearsal, as it adaptively manages task relationships to optimize knowledge retention and forward transfer \[[2202.02931](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02931)\]\[[2006.11942](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11942)\]\[[2010.04003](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04003)\]. Such hybrid strategies reflect a growing recognition that task similarity and ordering critically influence forgetting dynamics and learning efficacy in CL.

Beyond rehearsal, a rich body of work addresses catastrophic forgetting through alternative mechanisms. Regularization-based methods, such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), mitigate forgetting by penalizing changes to parameters important for prior tasks, formulated via second-order approximations of task-specific loss functions \[[2006.10974](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. These methods provide theoretical guarantees but often require careful tuning and may struggle with scalability or task interference compared to rehearsal-based approaches \[[2006.10974](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]\[[2302.05836](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\].

Gradient projection techniques offer another orthogonal approach by constraining weight updates to directions minimally interfering with previous tasks. For example, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) projects gradients orthogonally to subspaces corresponding to prior tasks, effectively mitigating forgetting without network growth or replay \[[2103.09762](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.09762)\]. Trust Region Gradient Projection (TRGP) further enhances this by identifying and reusing related task subspaces, facilitating knowledge transfer while preventing forgetting \[[2202.02931](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02931)\]. Theoretical analyses using Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) frameworks provide formal guarantees on forgetting reduction and generalization for orthogonal gradient methods, highlighting their robustness in continual learning \[[2006.11942](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11942)\]\[[2010.04003](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04003)\].

Generative replay methods, which synthesize past data via generative models, represent another rehearsal alternative. These approaches circumvent the need for explicit data storage by generating pseudo-examples of previous tasks during training, thereby alleviating forgetting while controlling memory usage \[[1705.0869](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Incremental classifier and representation learning (iCaRL) similarly balances retention and plasticity by combining exemplar rehearsal with representation learning, enabling learning of many classes incrementally \[[1611.07725](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\].

Theoretical studies have increasingly focused on understanding the interplay between overparameterization, task similarity, and task sequence on forgetting and generalization in CL. Overparameterization often improves generalization and reduces forgetting by providing abundant capacity to represent multiple tasks \[[2207.06475](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06475)\]\[[2408.16939](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16939)\]. Yet, task ordering effects are nuanced; training tasks with larger eigenvalues later tends to increase forgetting, while careful step size selection can mitigate it across under- and overparameterized regimes \[[2205.09588](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09588)\]\[[2405.17583](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\]. Recent works provide explicit bounds and generalization guarantees for forgetting, elucidating how task similarity and replay buffer size impact performance \[[2302.05836](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\]\[[2408.16939](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16939)\].

In summary, rehearsal-based continual learning has matured beyond simple concurrent replay, with sequential and hybrid rehearsal strategies offering promising improvements grounded in theoretical and empirical results. Complementary lines of work in regularization, gradient projection, and generative replay provide diverse mechanisms to combat forgetting. Theoretical advances notably clarify how overparameterization, task similarity, and ordering jointly shape continual learning dynamics, informing the design of more effective algorithms.
