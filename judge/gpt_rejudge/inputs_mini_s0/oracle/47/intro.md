## Related Works

Multi-domain recommendation (MDR) aims to improve recommendation accuracy by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains, addressing challenges such as data sparsity and cold-start problems. A fundamental difficulty in MDR is to effectively capture both shared and domain-specific user preferences while mitigating negative transfer, where irrelevant or conflicting domain knowledge degrades performance.

Early MDR approaches often assume domain-invariant user preferences and transfer knowledge uniformly across domains \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. However, this assumption overlooks domain-specific nuances, as user interests and item characteristics can vary significantly between domains. To address this, recent methods focus on disentangling shared and domain-specific representations. For example, EDDA separates embeddings into inter-domain and intra-domain parts and aligns domain embeddings via graph-based random walks, thus enhancing knowledge transfer across domains with limited overlap \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\].

Another line of work employs adaptive mechanisms to personalize the knowledge transfer process. PEPNet introduces parameter and embedding personalization by dynamically scaling embeddings and DNN parameters based on user-specific priors, allowing fine-grained control over multi-domain and multi-task recommendations \[[2302.01115](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01115)\]. Similarly, STAR utilizes a star topology where shared parameters capture commonalities and domain-specific parameters model distinctions, enabling adaptive CTR prediction across domains and yielding significant improvements in practice \[[2101.11427](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11427)\].

Cross-domain recommendation (CDR), closely related to MDR, leverages multi-view and multi-task frameworks to disentangle diverse user preferences and adaptively combine embeddings from different views. The MDAP framework uses multiview encoders and gated decoders to generate comprehensive user representations, improving adaptability and avoiding negative transfer by selective feature fusion \[[2410.05877](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05877)\]. Similarly, cooperative frameworks like Pacer and Runner dynamically estimate negative transfer levels between single-domain and cross-domain sequential recommendation tasks, weighting losses to control gradient flows and maximize mutual information between task representations, thereby mitigating negative transfer effects \[[2407.11245](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11245)\].

Negative transfer remains a critical challenge in multi-domain and multi-task learning. Studies highlight that unselective knowledge transfer from all domains can cause performance degradation when domain conflicts or poor-quality data exist. Solutions include selective knowledge transfer guided by domain similarity metrics or supervised signals. For instance, adaptive task grouping frameworks assign cooperating tasks to the same network while separating competing tasks to avoid interference, balancing accuracy and inference efficiency \[[1905.07553](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07553)\]. Furthermore, methods that explicitly measure domain-level gaps and dynamically select relevant domains based on learned prototypes and distance measures represent a promising direction to alleviate negative transfer \[[2407.11245](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11245)\].

In summary, existing MDR methods can be broadly categorized into those focusing on disentangling domain-shared and domain-specific knowledge \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\], those employing adaptive personalization and parameter modulation \[[2302.01115](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01115)\]\[[2101.11427](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11427)\], and those integrating selective domain or task transfer to prevent negative transfer \[[2410.05877](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05877)\]\[[1905.07553](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07553)\]\[[2407.11245](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11245)\]. Despite progress, most approaches rely on fixed structures or heuristics to transfer knowledge, lacking dynamic mechanisms to model complex transfer relationships among multiple domains. This limitation motivates research toward lightweight, dynamic domain selection principles that explicitly measure domain similarity and adaptively select appropriate source domains for knowledge transfer, enhancing MDR robustness and scalability.
