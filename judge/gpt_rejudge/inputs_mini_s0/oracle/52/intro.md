## Related Works

Dense retrieval methods have become foundational in open-domain question answering and information retrieval by leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT and LLaMA. These models are typically fine-tuned using contrastive learning objectives to encode queries and passages into dense vector representations that can be efficiently compared using simple similarity metrics like inner product \[[2004.04906](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. The Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework exemplifies this approach, demonstrating substantial improvements over traditional sparse retrieval models like BM25 by learning contextualized embeddings that better capture semantic relevance \[[2004.04906](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

A key question in dense retrieval research concerns the respective roles of pre-training and fine-tuning in shaping retrieval effectiveness. Recent analyses argue that retrieval knowledge is largely acquired during the pre-training phase, and that fine-tuning mainly adjusts activation patterns without substantially reorganizing or adding to the underlying knowledge embedded in PLMs \[[1908.10084](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)\]. This claim was primarily investigated in the context of BERT-based encoders using the DPR model on datasets such as Natural Questions \[[2104.08821](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\]. However, this initial focus on a single model architecture and dataset limits the generalizability of these findings.

To address these limitations, subsequent work has extended the analysis across different backbone architectures—including encoder-only models like BERT and decoder-only models like LLaMA—and different representation strategies, such as CLS token-based embeddings versus mean pooling of token embeddings (e.g., Contriever). This broader investigation, applied to multiple datasets including MS MARCO and Natural Questions, confirms that the DPR tuning paradigm relies heavily on pre-trained knowledge, with fine-tuning primarily modulating neuron activations rather than fundamentally restructuring learned information \[[1611.09268](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09268)\]. Notably, this pattern is not universal; mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based architectures like LLaMA exhibit a more significant influence of fine-tuning on retrieval performance, suggesting that representation choices and model designs affect the balance between pre-training and fine-tuning contributions .

Complementary to these findings, research in sentence embedding methods highlights the importance of training strategies to produce semantically meaningful dense representations suitable for retrieval. Sentence-BERT (SBERT) introduces siamese and triplet network architectures to efficiently generate sentence embeddings that preserve semantic similarity and enable fast nearest neighbor search, drastically reducing inference time compared to vanilla BERT while maintaining accuracy on semantic textual similarity benchmarks \[[1908.10084](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)\]. SimCSE further advances embedding quality with a simple contrastive learning framework that leverages minimal data augmentation via dropout noise and incorporates supervised natural language inference data to improve alignment of positive pairs, achieving state-of-the-art results in semantic textual similarity tasks \[[2104.08821](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\]. These advances in embedding learning complement dense retrieval methods by providing robust representations that can be fine-tuned or used as strong initializations for retrieval models.

In addition, large-scale datasets such as MS MARCO have played a critical role in advancing dense retrieval research by providing millions of realistic question-answer pairs and associated passages extracted from real web documents. MS MARCO supports tasks ranging from answerability prediction to passage ranking, offering a challenging and representative benchmark for evaluating retrieval models beyond synthetic or limited datasets . The scale and diversity of MS MARCO enable more comprehensive assessments of how model architectures and training procedures impact retrieval effectiveness in real-world scenarios.

Recent studies have also explored the impact of fine-tuning strategies on dense retrievers. For example, fine-tuning with very high dropout rates has been shown to enrich representations and improve robustness, particularly for out-of-distribution data, by encouraging diverse neuron activation patterns without overfitting . This aligns with observations that fine-tuning can modulate activation patterns rather than fundamentally altering pre-trained knowledge in encoder-only models. Conversely, combining fine-tuning with data augmentation can enhance performance when training data is limited, indicating that the fine-tuning role varies depending on data availability and model architecture . The quality of pre-training remains a critical factor influencing the effectiveness of subsequent fine-tuning, reinforcing the notion that dense retrieval success depends on the interplay between these two stages .

Overall, this body of work emphasizes that while pre-training lays the foundational knowledge for dense retrieval, the influence of fine-tuning depends on the model design, representation strategy, and data context. Our study builds on these insights by systematically evaluating multiple architectures, pooling methods, and datasets to clarify when and how fine-tuning modifies retrieval performance, thereby advancing understanding of knowledge acquisition in dense retrievers.
