## Related Works

Active learning (AL) aims to reduce annotation costs by selecting the most informative samples for labeling, thereby improving model efficiency and performance with fewer labeled data points \[[2505.06825](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06825)\]. Early AL strategies for image classification often leverage uncertainty metrics or core-set selection to identify samples near decision boundaries or representative of the data distribution, enhancing training efficiency for convolutional neural networks (CNNs) \[[2505.06825](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06825)\]\[[1708.00489](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.00489)\]. Techniques such as loss prediction modules have been proposed to predict which samples are likely to be misclassified, providing task-agnostic and computationally efficient selection strategies \[[1905.03677](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03677)\]. However, these traditional AL methods typically assume centralized data availability and do not directly address challenges posed by distributed, heterogeneous data environments.

Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across distributed clients without sharing raw data, thus preserving privacy and reducing communication overhead \[[1602.05629](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.05629)\]\[[1511.03575](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.03575)\]. FL settings are characterized by non-IID data distributions and uneven data volumes across clients, which pose significant challenges for model convergence and performance \[[2002.05038](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05038)\]\[[1602.05629](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.05629)\]. Several works have focused on mitigating data heterogeneity in FL by devising client selection strategies or normalization techniques to improve convergence and model accuracy under non-IID conditions \[[2002.05038](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05038)\]\[[1909.12641](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12641)\]. These approaches, however, primarily address model training and aggregation rather than active sample selection.

Federated Active Learning (FAL) emerges as a promising paradigm that integrates AL principles with FL to reduce both annotation and communication costs \[[2202.00195](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00195)\]\[[2211.13579](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13579)\]\[[2303.12317](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12317)\]. FAL methods enable clients to collaboratively select informative unlabeled samples for annotation in a privacy-preserving manner, improving global model performance without centralizing data. Existing FAL approaches vary in their query selection strategies, ranging from client-level separate active learning to global selection mechanisms. However, many methods struggle to reconcile the mismatch between local client objectives and global model goals, especially under data heterogeneity and asynchronous training \[[2211.13579](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13579)\]\[[2303.12317](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12317)\].

Addressing client heterogeneity is a critical and open challenge in FAL. Knowledge-Aware Federated Active Learning (KAFAL) introduces specialized active sampling that accounts for discrepancies between local and global models, improving the informativeness of selected samples and compensating for class imbalance due to non-IID data \[[2211.13579](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13579)\]. Similarly, LoGo proposes a hybrid approach integrating global and local selector models to handle varying inter-class diversity across clients, thereby balancing data heterogeneity and improving sample selection robustness \[[2303.12317](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12317)\]. These advances highlight the importance of epistemic uncertainty and model disagreement as signals for active sampling in federated settings.

Beyond sample selection, client selection in FL has been optimized through strategies like Active Federated Learning, which prioritizes clients based on their data informativeness and model state to reduce communication rounds and improve training efficiency \[[1909.12641](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12641)\]. Other works explore client adaptation and personalized FL techniques to mitigate heterogeneity effects, including client-specific conditioning and correlation-based client selection to enhance convergence \[[2303.12317](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12317)\]\[[1505.05424](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05424)\]\[[1905.03677](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03677)\]. While these methods improve model training dynamics, they do not inherently integrate active learning's focus on annotation efficiency.

The integration of uncertainty estimation techniques, such as Bayesian approximations with dropout or weight uncertainty modeling, further enhances AL and FAL by quantifying epistemic uncertainty critical for identifying ambiguous samples near decision boundaries \[[1505.05424](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05424)\]\[[1506.02142](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)\]. These probabilistic approaches complement active sampling by providing principled measures of model confidence, which are essential for selecting samples that most improve the global model.

In summary, the literature on AL and FL has evolved toward combining sample and client selection strategies to address challenges of data heterogeneity, communication efficiency, and annotation cost. However, existing FAL solutions often inadequately address the dynamic fluctuations in epistemic uncertainty and decision boundary alignment caused by client heterogeneity and asynchronous updates. Our proposed approach, CHASe, builds upon this foundation by explicitly tracking epistemic variations across training epochs, calibrating decision boundaries with alignment loss, and optimizing data selection efficiency through a freeze-and-awaken mechanism. This addresses key limitations in current FAL methods and advances the state-of-the-art in heterogeneity-aware federated active learning \[[2505.06825](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06825)\]\[[2002.05038](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05038)\]\[[2202.00195](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.00195)\]\[[2211.13579](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13579)\]\[[2303.12317](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12317)\]\[[1909.12641](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12641)\].
