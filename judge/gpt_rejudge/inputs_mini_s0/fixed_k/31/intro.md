## Related Works

Recent advances in DNA representation learning have been shaped by the broader success of unsupervised and self-supervised pre-training paradigms. Early efforts focused on unsupervised learning from raw DNA sequences, leveraging large unlabeled genomic datasets to learn useful sequence embeddings \[[1202.5211](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. However, pure sequence-based methods often overlook critical regulatory information embedded in genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, and transcription factor binding, which modulate DNA function beyond the primary sequence \[[1308.2134](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\]\[[2307.13708](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708)\]. This has motivated research into supervised training paradigms that incorporate these functional genomic signals to learn richer representations.

Self-supervised learning approaches have been applied to various biological domains with the goal of reducing reliance on costly annotations. For example, contrastive learning methods have been developed for land cover classification leveraging massive unlabeled satellite imagery \[[2203.06041](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\], and in medical genomics for clinical outcome prediction from high-dimensional genomic profiles \[[1609.08663](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08663)\]. In genomics specifically, efforts like DNA-GAN have aimed to disentangle multi-attribute image representations, demonstrating the value of supervised signals in learning interpretable latent factors \[[1711.05415](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05415)\]. Nevertheless, these approaches typically do not explicitly integrate multi-species or multi-profile information, limiting their ability to capture complex inter-species genomic relationships and the diversity of functional genomic marks.

Supervised learning using genomic profiles has shown promise in improving prediction accuracy and biological relevance. For instance, classifiers predicting DNA methylation at single-CpG resolution have achieved high accuracy by incorporating not only sequence context but also regulatory features such as CpG islands, DNase hypersensitive sites, and transcription factor binding \[[1308.2134](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\]. Similarly, statistical learning models trained on genome-wide SNP profiles have yielded robust predictions of complex diseases like celiac disease, highlighting the power of supervised genomic data for phenotype prediction \[[1301.5948](http://arxiv.org/abs/1301.5948)\]. These results suggest that supervised genomic profile prediction can serve as a more effective pre-training task than pure sequence modeling.

The mixture of experts (MoE) paradigm has been explored in various domains to improve model capacity and specialization. While MoE architectures have been successfully applied in natural language processing and computer vision to handle multi-modal or multi-task learning \[[2502.03499](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\]\[[2006.10803](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10803)\], their application to genomic data remains limited. The ability of MoE to dynamically route inputs to specialized subnetworks is particularly well suited to handle multi-species and multi-profile genomic data, where distinct experts can capture species-specific or profile-specific patterns. This motivates frameworks like SPACE, which leverage MoE to learn DNA representations that jointly model diverse genomic profiles across species, thereby improving generalization and interpretability.

Other related work includes generative models for DNA sequences, such as latent diffusion models designed for multi-species DNA generation \[[2402.06079](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06079)\], and transformer-based foundation models like Omni-DNA that perform multi-task learning across genomic modalities \[[2502.03499](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\]. However, these models often focus on sequence generation or single-task predictions without explicitly incorporating supervised genomic profile prediction or MoE architectures to handle multi-profile complexity. Additionally, feature summarization tools like featureCounts facilitate downstream genomic analysis but do not directly address representation learning \[[1305.3347](http://arxiv.org/abs/1305.3347)\].

In summary, the field has progressed from unsupervised DNA sequence modeling toward incorporating supervised genomic profile data, which better reflects the functional state of the genome. Existing supervised methods have improved predictive performance but generally lack mechanisms to explicitly capture multi-species and multi-profile relationships. The integration of MoE for this purpose is novel and addresses these gaps by enabling specialized experts to learn nuanced representations across diverse genomic contexts. This approach aligns with recent trends in leveraging multi-task and multi-modal learning to enhance model expressiveness and accuracy in genomics \[[2502.03499](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\]\[[1308.2134](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2134)\]\[[1301.5948](http://arxiv.org/abs/1301.5948)\]\[[2402.06079](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.06079)\].
