## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking and recommendation systems has garnered substantial attention, particularly focused on balancing the trade-off between ranking accuracy and fairness towards item providers or user groups. Early approaches emphasized provider-side fairness by integrating diversity and fairness metrics into the ranking process while maintaining acceptable accuracy levels. For instance, personalized fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms were developed to balance recommendation quality and provider coverage, incorporating individual user tolerance to provider diversification to avoid degrading user experience \[[2005.12974](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. This approach iteratively adjusts rankings to promote fairness without severely sacrificing accuracy.

Expanding on provider fairness, resource allocation perspectives have been applied to fair re-ranking. Methods like provider max-min fairness (P-MMF) treat exposure slots as limited resources that must be fairly distributed among providers, prioritizing the utility of the worst-off groups to achieve equitable exposure \[[1809.02921](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]\[[2405.1612](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120)\]. This framework addresses fairness as a constrained optimization problem and has demonstrated superior performance over traditional proportion fairness measures.

A novel conceptualization of fairness in re-ranking draws from economic theory, modeling fairness constraints as item-level tax policies analogous to commodity taxation. The Tax-rank method exemplifies this approach by levying taxes based on the utility differences between items, optimizing the redistribution of ranking slots via the Sinkhorn algorithm from optimal transport theory. This method ensures continuity and controllability over accuracy loss, overcoming limitations of earlier tax-inspired re-ranking models \[[2204.08085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. Such economic analogies highlight the inherent coupling between item fairness (supplier-side constraints) and user-side accuracy loss, reflecting elasticity concepts from economics.

Demographic fairness has also been incorporated into re-ranking through the use of fairness representations derived from labeled data. Variations of Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) have been adapted to jointly consider relevance and demographic fairness, showing that even limited labeled data can effectively guide fairness-aware post-processing steps in recommendation systems \[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. This approach balances relevance and fairness while preserving precision.

Two-sided fairness in recommender systems acknowledges the interdependent roles of consumers and producers. CPFair introduced an optimization-based re-ranking framework that simultaneously integrates fairness constraints for both users and providers, improving fairness on both sides without compromising overall recommendation quality \[[1809.03577](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03577)\]. Building on this, BankFair and its extension BankFair+ propose dynamic re-ranking algorithms inspired by economic bankruptcy problems and regret theory, respectively. BankFair employs the Talmud rule to manage fairness over fluctuating user traffic, ensuring consistent user accuracy and sustained provider fairness \[[2405.1612](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120)\]. BankFair+ further addresses individual fairness disparities by incorporating regret-aware fuzzy programming, balancing individual user fairness with provider fairness more effectively \[[2504.1455](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14550)\].

Research has also highlighted challenges in fairness-aware representative ranking, such as biases that emerge when minority or less active groups have limited platform engagement, leading to both individual and group unfairness. Methods have been proposed to generate ideal fair representative rankings when universal representation ratios are known, though open challenges remain in ensuring fairness at multiple granularities \[[2103.01335](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01335)\].

Beyond fairness trade-offs, several studies examine the foundational assumptions and limitations of fair machine learning. One analysis argues that the common focus on group outcome similarity inherently involves a trade-off with statistical accuracy, posing fundamental challenges to equitable fairness implementations \[[2306.01417](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01417)\]. Moreover, data biases such as under-representation and label bias significantly impact the performance of fairness-aware classifiers, with some simple techniques like reweighing providing more robust fairness guarantees under biased training data \[[2302.05906](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05906)\]. The robustness disparity in deep learning models also introduces fairness concerns, as certain subgroups may be more vulnerable to adversarial attacks, revealing a dimension of fairness beyond static output metrics \[[2006.12621](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\].

In clustering and classification contexts, flexible and scalable algorithms have been developed to integrate fairness constraints with conventional objectives, enabling control over demographic proportions and trade-offs without significant losses in clustering quality or accuracy \[[1906.08207](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08207)\]\[[2111.08878](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08878)\]. These methods illustrate that fairness need not always come at a high cost to performance.

Overall, the analogy between fairness-aware re-ranking and economic taxation extends the understanding of the fairness-accuracy trade-off by introducing elasticity as a core concept governing this relationship. Recent frameworks like the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) formalize this trade-off, enabling comparative evaluation of algorithms across different elasticity regimes. ElasticRank operationalizes this insight by adjusting inter-item distances based on elasticity calculations, enhancing fairness while controlling accuracy loss \[[2204.08085](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]\[[2504.1455](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14550)\]. This economic perspective offers a unifying lens to analyze and design fair re-ranking algorithms, addressing limitations of prior single-metric evaluations and paving the way for more nuanced and effective fairness interventions.
