## Related Works

Performance modeling and optimization of large language models (LLMs) in distributed training environments have attracted considerable attention due to the complexity of resource management, communication overhead, and model scalability. Early efforts primarily targeted communication efficiency and fault tolerance in distributed optimization frameworks, such as asynchronous distributed gradient descent with redundancy mechanisms to improve resilience and reduce waiting time for straggler nodes \[[2410.02425](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\]. However, these methods often faced challenges balancing efficiency with accuracy and flexibility in heterogeneous environments.

More recent work has explored hardware-aware performance optimization for both training and inference of LLMs. For example, distributed inference optimization on CPUs demonstrated significant speedups by leveraging multi-node parallelism and efficient resource utilization, achieving inference speeds surpassing human reading rates even for very large models (e.g., 72B parameters) \[[2407.00029](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00029)\]. Similarly, LLM-Pilot provided a system for benchmarking and predicting inference service performance across various GPUs, enabling cost-effective hardware recommendations and configuration tuning that improved performance fulfillment by 33% while reducing costs by 60% \[[2408.1644](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16440)\].

Distributed training optimization has also been advanced through compiler-level techniques. DisCo introduced a joint operator and tensor fusion strategy optimized by a GNN-based simulator, which significantly reduced communication overhead and improved training throughput in multi-GPU setups by approximating full computation-communication overlap \[[2209.12769](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12769)\]. These compiler optimizations address the inefficiencies inherent in single-device execution strategies when scaled to distributed environments.

In addition to hardware and compiler optimizations, model-level sparsification and pruning methods have been proposed to reduce resource demands during inference and training. For instance, expert pruning and skipping in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models enable substantial model size reduction and inference speedup without major performance loss, facilitating more efficient deployment of otherwise massive MoE LLMs \[[2402.148](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800)\]. Similarly, activation sparsity techniques targeting Feed-Forward Network (FFN) components in LLMs can reduce memory and computation by approximately 50% with negligible accuracy degradation, which is particularly beneficial for edge deployment scenarios \[[2412.12178](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12178)\].

From a system perspective, cloud-based frameworks have been used to empirically study distributed training performance under various GPU cluster configurations, including transient and revocable GPU servers, revealing trade-offs among training speed, cost, and robustness \[[2004.03072](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03072)\]. Such empirical datasets and predictive regression models provide valuable insights for cluster design and scheduling.

Despite these advances, accurately characterizing and predicting the complex execution behavior of large-scale LLM training remains challenging. Existing solutions often emphasize inference services or partial aspects of training, lacking comprehensive trace-driven modeling. The proposed Lumos toolkit distinguishes itself by leveraging detailed execution traces from large GPU clusters (up to 512 NVIDIA H100 GPUs) to accurately replay and predict LLM training performance with low error (3.3%) across multiple models and configurations. This approach facilitates efficient exploration of training strategies and system setups, addressing gaps in prior work that focused either on inference characterization \[[2408.1644](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16440)\] or compiler optimization \[[2209.12769](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12769)\] without comprehensive trace-based performance estimation.

Furthermore, instruction tuning and data-driven fine-tuning approaches have been explored for improving LLM capabilities in specialized domains and tasks, highlighting the importance of dataset composition and instruction complexity for model behavior \[[2304.12244](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]\[[2312.10793](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10793)\]. While these studies focus on model quality rather than system performance, they underscore the multifaceted nature of LLM optimization that spans algorithmic, data, and system-level considerations.

In summary, prior research has contributed valuable methods and insights into distributed LLM training and inference optimization, including redundancy in distributed optimization, hardware-aware inference tuning, compiler fusion techniques, sparsity-based compression, and empirical cluster performance modeling \[[2410.02425](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\]\[[2407.00029](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00029)\]\[[2004.03072](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03072)\]\[[2209.12769](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12769)\]\[[2412.12178](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12178)\]\[[2408.1644](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16440)\]\[[2402.148](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14800)\]. The Lumos toolkit builds upon and integrates these directions by providing a comprehensive, trace-driven performance modeling framework tailored to large-scale LLM training, enabling more accurate and cost-effective system design and deployment decisions.
