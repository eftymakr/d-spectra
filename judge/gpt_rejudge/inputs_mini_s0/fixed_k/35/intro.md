## Related Works

Protein dynamics underpin a protein’s biological functions and properties, traditionally studied through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations that are computationally intensive and often limited by sampling efficiency \[[2411.08154](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08154)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling, especially denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs), have enabled more efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformational ensemble generation by learning distributions directly from crystallographic data \[[2209.15611](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]\[[2403.14088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]\[[2304.02198](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]\[[2408.12419](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12419)\]\[[2305.0412](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\]. These models have shown promise in capturing the conformational heterogeneity that static structure prediction methods cannot address.

Energy-based models (EBMs) provide a principled framework to incorporate physical realism into modeling protein conformations. For example, an atomic-scale EBM trained solely on crystallographic data achieved comparable performance to the widely used Rosetta energy function for rotamer recovery, indicating its ability to capture relevant physicochemical properties without handcrafted potentials \[[2004.13167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. EBMs have also been applied in molecular graph generation with permutation invariance to respect molecular symmetries \[[2102.00546](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00546)\], and their utility in continual learning suggests adaptability for evolving protein conformational landscapes \[[2011.12216](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12216)\].

Integrating physical priors into generative models remains a significant challenge, as pure data-driven approaches may yield physically implausible structures or deviate from equilibrium distributions. To address this, force-guided SE(3) equivariant diffusion models have been proposed, leveraging rotational and translational symmetries intrinsic to protein structures. ConfDiff, a force-guided SE(3) diffusion model, incorporates physical forces alongside data-driven scores, producing diverse and high-fidelity conformations that surpass previous state-of-the-art methods \[[2403.14088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. Similarly, P2DFlow uses SE(3) flow matching with a physically informed prior to generate protein conformational ensembles consistent with molecular dynamics simulations, effectively capturing dynamic fluctuations observed experimentally \[[2411.17196](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17196)\].

Diffusion models have also been adapted to capture conformational distributions from sequences. EigenFold models protein structure generation as a harmonic oscillator diffusion process along eigenmodes of the structure, improving ensemble sampling quality and enabling the study of conformational heterogeneity such as fold switching and ligand-induced changes \[[2304.02198](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Latent diffusion models have been developed to reduce modeling complexity by embedding proteins into condensed latent spaces, facilitating efficient generation of novel backbone structures while preserving designability \[[2305.0412](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\].

Beyond diffusion, alternative frameworks such as structure language models (SLM) embed protein conformations into discrete latent spaces and leverage conditional language modeling to efficiently sample diverse ensembles with significant speedups over traditional diffusion approaches \[[2410.18403](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403)\]. Autoregressive approaches like ConfRover combine sequence modeling with SE(3)-equivariant diffusion to jointly learn protein conformation and dynamics from MD trajectories, supporting both time-dependent and independent sampling of protein ensembles \[[2505.17478](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17478)\]. Recent 4D diffusion models extend this further by integrating temporal coherence and motion guidance to predict dynamic protein trajectories across multiple time steps, capturing both local flexibility and major conformational changes \[[2408.12419](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12419)\].

Enhanced sampling methods complement generative approaches by extracting collective variables from anharmonic low-frequency vibrations to accelerate MD simulations and generate accurate free energy landscapes, thereby enabling rapid exploration of conformational transitions \[[2411.08154](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08154)\]. These methods highlight the critical role of physically meaningful coordinates in efficient sampling.

The role of physical supervision in generative models is further emphasized in recent reviews, which note the importance of preserving E(3) equivariance to maintain physical stability during protein generation and suggest diffusion models as a mathematically robust framework for autonomous protein engineering \[[2501.0268](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.02680)\]. Incorporating physical alignment or calibration mechanisms to balance conformational states according to energy differences could bridge the gap between purely data-driven generation and physically grounded modeling, a direction aligned with the proposed Energy-based Alignment (EBA) method.

In summary, the landscape of protein conformation generation is increasingly dominated by diffusion-based generative models enhanced by physical priors and equivariance, energy-based frameworks capturing atomic-level realism, and hybrid approaches integrating temporal dynamics. These advances collectively address challenges in sampling efficiency, physical plausibility, and conformational diversity, forming a foundation for improved protein ensemble generation and downstream applications in structural biology and drug discovery \[[811.3094](http://arxiv.org/abs/0811.3094)\]\[[2209.15611](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]\[[2004.13167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]\[[2411.17196](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.17196)\]\[[2403.14088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]\[[2304.02198](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]\[[2410.18403](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403)\]\[[2411.08154](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08154)\]\[[2408.12419](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.12419)\]\[[2501.0268](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.02680)\]\[[2505.17478](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17478)\]\[[2305.0412](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\]\[[2011.12216](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12216)\]\[[2102.00546](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00546)\].
