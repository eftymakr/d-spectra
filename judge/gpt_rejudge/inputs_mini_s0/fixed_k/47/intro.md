## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has attracted considerable attention due to its ability to leverage knowledge from multiple domains simultaneously, improving recommendation quality across diverse scenarios \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Traditional cross-domain recommendation (CDR) methods often rely on assumptions such as at least one domain being rich in data or on strong domain-invariant preferences, which are not always realistic in practice where data sparsity is prevalent across all domains \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. To address this, models like MSDCR enhance the learning of both domain-specific and domain-invariant user preferences by transferring complementary aspect preferences while preserving domain uniqueness, thereby improving performance in multiple sparse domains \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\].

A key challenge in multi-domain settings is the negative transfer problem (NTP), which occurs when knowledge from dissimilar or conflicting domains degrades model performance. This problem has been studied extensively in both MDR and cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR). For example, recent CDSR approaches estimate the marginal contribution of each domain using cooperative game theory to adaptively weigh domain knowledge, thereby mitigating negative transfer \[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Hierarchical contrastive learning further helps by leveraging coarse-to-fine category relationships to capture generalized preferences despite low fine-level relevance between domains \[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Similarly, graph-based methods like MAGRec model both domain-guided and general user interests through temporal intra- and inter-domain interactions, effectively reducing negative knowledge transfer \[[2302.0599](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05990)\].

Embedding disentanglement and domain alignment are also important strategies to handle domain heterogeneity. EDDA separates inter-domain and intra-domain embeddings and aligns user/item embeddings across domains using graph random walks to facilitate knowledge transfer even with small overlaps \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. Hypergraph-based methods represent multi-domain user-item interactions in unified graphs, using dynamic item transfer and adaptive user aggregation to capture high-order correlations and reduce domain discrepancy \[[2211.11191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\]. These approaches emphasize maintaining domain-specific features while enabling effective knowledge sharing.

Selective knowledge transfer has been proposed to explicitly address the heterogeneity and conflicts among domains. Early works introduced criteria based on empirical prediction error and variance to select consistent source domains for transfer learning, embedding these into boosting frameworks to avoid negative transfer \[[1210.7056](http://arxiv.org/abs/1210.7056)\]. More recent methods incorporate prototype-based domain distance measures or domain similarity metrics to dynamically select suitable source domains for each target domain, thus alleviating the negative transfer problem \[[2302.0599](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05990)\]\[[1812.06229](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06229)\]. This selective transfer principle aligns with partial domain adaptation research where reinforced data selectors filter out irrelevant or outlier source samples based on both high-level and pixel-level features, improving transfer effectiveness \[[1905.10756](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10756)\].

Domain-specific feature modeling is another crucial aspect. Studies have highlighted the importance of identifying domain-sensitive features that carry domain distinctions, which are often overlooked by implicit multi-domain architectures. Memory architectures that extract and retrieve domain-sensitive features have shown improvements in capturing domain differences and boosting recommendation accuracy \[[2405.12892](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12892)\]. Furthermore, model architectures that incorporate domain-specific adapters with dynamically generated parameters enable better flexibility and reduce interference among domains caused by static parameter sharing \[[2309.06217](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\].

Scalability and generalizability are also significant concerns in MDR. Frameworks like MAMDR propose model-agnostic learning with domain negotiation and regularization strategies to alleviate domain conflicts and overfitting, enabling deployment across thousands of domains without specialist intervention \[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. Multi-task learning frameworks such as M3oE integrate multi-domain information with disentangled mixture-of-experts modules to capture common, domain-aspect, and task-aspect preferences effectively, further improving model adaptability \[[2404.18465](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18465)\].

Graphical and adversarial modeling have been employed for multi-source domain adaptation (MSDA), which shares conceptual challenges with MDR. Graphical models like CRF-MSDA and MRF-MSDA jointly model source and target domains through relational graphs or energy-based formulations to learn domain combinations and enhance label prediction \[[2104.13057](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13057)\]. Adversarial domain aggregation networks generate adapted domains with pixel-level and feature-level alignment to better bridge domain shifts and aggregate multiple sources \[[2003.0082](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00820)\]. These methods inspire MDR research in explicitly modeling domain relationships and addressing domain shifts.

Overall, existing MDR research shows a clear trend toward dynamic, selective, and disentangled multi-domain knowledge transfer to mitigate negative transfer and capture domain-specific characteristics. However, most approaches still rely on static or uniform domain sharing mechanisms that do not fully reflect the complex, varying transfer relationships across domains. The proposed Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP), with prototype-based domain distance and dynamic domain selection, represents a novel direction by explicitly quantifying domain-level gaps and adaptively selecting relevant domains to avoid negative transfer without heavy computational overhead. This approach complements and extends prior work by enabling fine-grained, supervised and unsupervised domain relationship modeling for robust multi-domain recommendation \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]\[[2311.13188](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]\[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]\[[2309.06217](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]\[[1210.7056](http://arxiv.org/abs/1210.7056)\]\[[2405.12892](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12892)\]\[[2302.0599](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05990)\]\[[1905.10756](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10756)\].
