## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) aims to leverage user and item interactions across multiple domains to improve recommendation quality for each domain simultaneously. Traditional MDR methods typically employ shared or unified structures to transfer domain-shared knowledge, often ignoring domain-specific nuances and the complex relationships among domains. This can lead to suboptimal performance or even negative transfer when knowledge conflicts or domain quality varies significantly \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]\[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]\[[2101.11427](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11427)\].

Early cross-domain recommendation (CDR) efforts primarily focused on single-target scenarios, transferring knowledge from a richer source domain to a sparser target domain to alleviate data sparsity. For example, matrix factorization and deep transfer learning approaches such as CoNet introduced cross connections between domain-specific networks to facilitate dual knowledge transfer \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Similarly, DDTCDR employed dual learning and latent orthogonal mapping to capture bidirectional relations between users and items across two domains, enhancing recommendations by integrating feature information beyond ratings \[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods, however, generally assume a fixed transfer relationship and are limited in handling more than two domains.

More recent works have expanded to dual-target and multi-target CDR frameworks. DTCDR proposes a multi-task learning approach to simultaneously improve recommendation accuracy in both source and target domains by sharing embeddings for common users \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. CAT-ART advances this further by combining self-supervised contrastive learning for global user embeddings with attention-based domain-specific embedding transfer, enabling knowledge sharing in multi-target setups involving multiple domains \[[2211.11964](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11964)\]. GA further unifies dual-target, multi-target, and cross-system recommendations into a graph embedding and attention-based framework with personalized training to minimize negative transfer between richer and sparser datasets \[[2108.07976](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07976)\].

Multi-domain recommendation methods have also explored disentangling domain-shared and domain-specific knowledge. EDDA separates embeddings and models for inter-domain and intra-domain components and uses domain alignment via graph random walks to enhance knowledge transfer between similar user/item pairs across domains \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. Similarly, hierarchical hypergraph networks model multi-domain user-item interactions to capture correlative preferences and high-order connections, improving user representation while reducing domain discrepancy \[[2211.11191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\]. MAMDR adopts a domain negotiation strategy and domain regularization to alleviate domain conflicts and improve generalizability of domain-specific parameters, enabling scalable MDR for thousands of domains \[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\].

A critical challenge in MDR and CDR is the negative transfer problem (NTP), where indiscriminate knowledge transfer from irrelevant or low-quality domains harms performance. Several approaches address this by selective knowledge transfer. For instance, adversarial feature translation methods learn domain-specific feature mappings to capture inter-domain feature interactions while preserving uniqueness, thereby mitigating negative transfer \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59)\]. MSDCR introduces multi-domain separation networks and gated aspect preference modules to preserve domain-specific preferences and adaptively transfer complementary preferences from other domains, effectively handling scenarios where all domains are sparse \[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. CDRIB applies the information bottleneck principle to enforce domain-shared representations that capture unbiased, relevant information for cold-start users, reducing domain-specific noise that leads to negative transfer \[[2203.16863](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16863)\].

Graph-based methods have been widely adopted to model user-item interactions and cross-domain relations. BiTGCF uses graph collaborative filtering with bi-directional transfer leveraging common users as bridges to fuse common and domain-specific features \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]. Preference Propagation GraphNet constructs a joint cross-domain preference matrix to capture high-order information propagation and simplifies cross-domain recommendation into a unified multi-task link prediction problem \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\]. These graph-based approaches better capture complex cross-domain structural relations but do not explicitly measure domain similarity or perform dynamic domain selection.

Domain similarity measurement and dynamic domain selection remain underexplored in MDR. Some prior works incorporate domain similarity metrics to identify relevant domains and reduce negative transfer by selectively transferring knowledge \[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]\[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. However, these methods often rely on heuristic or static similarity measures and do not explicitly model complex domain relationships or adaptively select domains during training. The proposed Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) addresses this gap by introducing a prototype-based domain distance metric that jointly leverages supervised domain metrics and unsupervised learned prototypes to dynamically identify similar domains for each domain, thereby alleviating negative transfer without imposing significant computational overhead.

In summary, the field has progressed from single-target, single or dual-domain transfer approaches to more sophisticated multi-target and multi-domain frameworks that disentangle domain-shared and domain-specific knowledge, employ graph neural networks for interaction modeling, and address negative transfer via selective knowledge transfer. Nevertheless, scalable, lightweight methods that explicitly quantify domain-level gaps and dynamically select appropriate domains for knowledge transfer remain an important research direction to improve MDR robustness and effectiveness \[[2308.05508](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]\[[2211.11964](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11964)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]\[[2108.07976](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07976)\]\[[2211.11191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\]\[[2203.16863](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16863)\]\[[1910.05189](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59)\]\[[2202.12524](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]\[[2201.05973](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\].
