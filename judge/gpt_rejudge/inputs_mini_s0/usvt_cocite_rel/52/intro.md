## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a pivotal technique for information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT and LLaMA to encode text into dense, low-dimensional vectors that facilitate efficient similarity comparisons via inner products \[[2004.04906](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. The foundational work on BERT introduced deep bidirectional transformers pre-trained on large unlabeled corpora, enabling powerful contextual embeddings that could be fine-tuned for various downstream tasks including retrieval and question answering \[[1810.04805](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Subsequent studies demonstrated that optimizing pre-training strategies, such as those employed by RoBERTa, which improved hyperparameter tuning and training data scale, further enhances the quality of language representations \[[1907.11692](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Similarly, ALBERT proposed parameter-reduction techniques to scale model size efficiently while improving downstream task performance \[[1909.11942](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)\].

Recent work has focused on understanding the distinct roles of pre-training and fine-tuning in dense retrieval. Early dense retrievers, such as DPR, showed that fine-tuning a BERT-based dual encoder on question-passage pairs significantly improves retrieval accuracy \[[2004.04906](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\], but later analyses suggested that most retrieval-relevant knowledge is already captured during pre-training, with fine-tuning mainly adjusting neuron activations rather than reorganizing the underlying knowledge \[[2108.05540](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This observation highlights the fragility of dense retrievers to training data noise and the need for carefully engineered fine-tuning pipelines \[[2108.05540](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[2010.08191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. However, such conclusions were initially limited to encoder-only architectures using CLS token representations and specific datasets, raising questions about their generality.

Extensions to this line of inquiry have explored various backbone architectures and representation approaches. For example, decoder-only models like LLaMA and mean-pooling-based encoders such as Contriever exhibit different fine-tuning dynamics, where fine-tuning plays a more substantial role in shaping retrieval performance, suggesting that the contribution of pre-training versus fine-tuning depends on model design and representation choices \[[2104.08253](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]\[[2010.08191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. This aligns with findings from contrastive learning approaches, where unsupervised or self-supervised objectives can substantially improve dense retrieval without supervised fine-tuning, indicating that contrastive pre-training can encode retrieval knowledge effectively \[[2112.09118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]\[[2006.03659](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]\[[2104.08821](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\].

Pre-training architectures tailored for dense retrieval, such as Condenser and coCondenser, have been proposed to better align language model pre-training with the requirements of dense encoders by condensing information into dense vectors through specialized contrastive losses and conditioning mechanisms \[[2108.05540](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[2104.08253](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. These methods reduce reliance on large batch sizes and heavy data engineering, achieving competitive or superior results compared to heavily engineered systems like RocketQA, which relies on sophisticated training strategies including cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives, and data augmentation \[[2108.05540](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[2010.08191](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. Similarly, RetroMAE leverages a masked auto-encoder paradigm with asymmetric masking and model structures to improve retrieval-oriented pre-training, further demonstrating the benefits of retrieval-specific pre-training objectives \[[2205.12035](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\].

The role of negative sampling in fine-tuning dense retrievers has been extensively studied. Techniques such as Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE) dynamically select challenging negative examples during training to better align training and inference distributions, improving retrieval effectiveness \[[2007.00808](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Other works propose stable and dynamic training algorithms that combine random and hard negatives to optimize ranking performance and training stability \[[2104.08051](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. Efficient training methods like TAS-Balanced introduce topic-aware sampling and dual-teacher supervision to train dense retrievers on consumer-grade hardware while achieving state-of-the-art results \[[2104.06967](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\].

Contrastive learning frameworks have proven particularly effective for learning robust sentence and passage embeddings. Models like SimCSE and DeCLUTR use contrastive objectives with minimal or no supervision to learn noise-invariant embeddings that transfer well across tasks \[[2006.03659](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]\[[2104.08821](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\]. These methods highlight the importance of augmentation strategies, batch size, and loss design in enhancing embedding quality \[[2012.15466](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15466)\]\[[2002.05709](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. Furthermore, unsupervised dense retrievers employing contrastive learning can outperform classical sparse methods like BM25 on diverse benchmarks and enable strong cross-lingual transfer \[[2112.09118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\].

Beyond encoder-only models, retrieval-augmented architectures such as REALM and RAG integrate explicit retrieval mechanisms during pre-training and fine-tuning, combining parametric and non-parametric knowledge sources to achieve superior performance in knowledge-intensive NLP tasks \[[2002.08909](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]\[[2005.11401](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. These approaches underscore the limitations of implicit knowledge storage in PLMs and the benefits of modular retrieval components.

Lastly, there is ongoing research on combining sparse and dense retrieval paradigms to leverage their complementary strengths. For instance, SPAR introduces a dense retriever augmented with lexical matching capabilities to imitate sparse models like BM25, achieving improved generalization and phrase-level matching \[[2110.06918](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06918)\]. Similarly, hybrid retrievers combining BM25 and dense models have shown enhanced zero-shot and supervised retrieval performance \[[2112.09118](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]\[[2112.07708](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07708)\].

In summary, the interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning in dense retrieval depends on model architecture, representation strategy, pre-training objectives, and training data. While pre-trained knowledge forms a strong foundation, fine-tuning can still play a critical role, especially in less conventional architectures or when enhanced training strategies are applied. Advances in contrastive pre-training, negative sampling, and retrieval-specific architectures continue to push the state of the art in dense retrieval.
