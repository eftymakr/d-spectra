## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have established themselves as a fundamental approach in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommender systems by effectively leveraging the user-item interaction graph structure to capture complex user preferences \[[2002.02126](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]\[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Early works adapted spectral graph theory to design convolution operations on graphs, enabling the integration of node features and graph topology into the recommendation process \[[1609.02907](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]\[[1606.09375](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. However, existing GCN-based CF methods commonly rely on embedding layers that map users and items into latent spaces with fixed dimensionality, which may restrict the model’s capacity to capture locally observed yet significant preference signals \[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Moreover, the standard neighborhood aggregation in these models often applies uniform or linear aggregations, limiting their ability to model diverse and fine-grained user intents and interaction patterns \[[2007.01764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]\[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\].

To overcome such limitations, several lines of research have emerged. LightGCN simplifies the GCN architecture by removing feature transformations and nonlinear activations, focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation, which improves scalability and performance \[[2002.02126](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, residual graph convolutional approaches alleviate over-smoothing problems inherent in deep GCNs by incorporating skip connections tailored for CF scenarios \[[2001.10167](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. These works demonstrate that linear and simplified GCN variants can yield better embeddings and mitigate training difficulties on large, sparse user-item graphs.

Beyond structural simplifications, recent studies emphasize enriching the embedding process itself. Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) explicitly propagates embeddings on the user-item bipartite graph to exploit high-order connectivity and inject collaborative signals directly into the representation learning process \[[1905.08108](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) further refines this idea by modeling user-item interactions through multiple latent intents, enabling the disentanglement of user preferences and improving interpretability and recommendation accuracy \[[2007.01764](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of modeling diverse user intents and interaction heterogeneity, which standard neighborhood aggregation often overlooks.

In parallel, the integration of side information via knowledge graphs has been explored to enhance recommendation beyond direct user-item interactions. RippleNet propagates user preferences over knowledge graphs to alleviate sparsity and cold-start problems, effectively extending user interests along linked entities \[[1803.03467](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\]. KGAT introduces attention mechanisms over knowledge graph neighbors to model high-order relations with adaptive importance weighting, improving recommendation accuracy and interpretability \[[1905.07854](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. These methods underscore the value of leveraging rich relational data to capture complex user-item associations.

Spectral methods for collaborative filtering have also attracted attention. Spectral Collaborative Filtering formulates user-item relations as bipartite graphs and applies spectral convolution operations in the graph spectral domain, capturing connectivity patterns beyond local neighborhoods and addressing cold-start issues \[[1808.10523](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. This spectral perspective aligns with theoretical insights that GCNs perform graph filtering operations akin to Laplacian smoothing, which can cause over-smoothing and limit expressiveness if restricted to low-frequency components \[[1801.07606](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\]. Recent advances propose to learn more flexible graph spectral filters, including polynomial approximations like Chebyshev polynomials, to better capture a wider frequency spectrum and complex, nonlinear filtering effects on graph signals \[[2106.10994](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10994)\]. Bernstein polynomial-based methods further allow learning arbitrary spectral filters with constrained optimization for stability and expressiveness \[[2106.10994](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10994)\].

The application of Chebyshev polynomials for graph filtering, initially explored in graph signal processing, provides an efficient means to approximate non-linear graph filters and localize operations on graphs \[[1606.09375](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. This approach has shown efficacy in tasks such as image denoising on graphs and is promising for recommendation systems to overcome the limitations of linear, fixed-form filters used in standard GCNs \[[1606.09375](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. Incorporating such spectral filtering techniques in CF can enable capturing richer and more diverse user preference patterns encoded in the full spectrum of the user-item interaction graph.

In addition to graph-based approaches, various neural network architectures have been proposed to enhance collaborative filtering. Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) replaces the traditional inner product with multi-layer perceptrons to model non-linear user-item interactions, yielding improved recommendation performance \[[1708.05031](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. Deep item-based collaborative filtering models capture higher-order and nonlinear item relationships to better represent user interests \[[1811.04392](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04392)\]. Variational autoencoders (VAEs) have been adapted to CF to leverage non-linear probabilistic modeling and handle implicit feedback, improving recommendation quality \[[1802.05814](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\].

Furthermore, self-supervised learning paradigms have been introduced to reinforce graph-based recommendation models by generating auxiliary tasks that improve robustness and capture long-tail item preferences \[[2010.10783](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. Graph attention networks (GATs) apply attention mechanisms on graph neighborhoods to assign adaptive importance to neighbors, enhancing representation learning \[[1710.10903](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]. Jumping Knowledge networks adaptively aggregate information from various neighborhood ranges, mitigating the limitations of fixed aggregation scopes \[[1806.03536](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.03536)\].

Overall, while GCNs have advanced CF by incorporating graph structure, their reliance on fixed embeddings and limited linear aggregation restricts their ability to fully exploit the rich, diverse signals present in user-item interactions. Approaches that leverage spectral graph filtering techniques, especially those employing polynomial approximations like Chebyshev filters to flexibly model complex, nonlinear user preferences, offer a promising direction to overcome these bottlenecks and achieve superior recommendation performance.
