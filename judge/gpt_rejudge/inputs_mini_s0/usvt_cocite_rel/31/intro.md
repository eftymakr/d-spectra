## Related Works

Pre-training paradigms have profoundly impacted representation learning across domains, including DNA sequence modeling. Initial approaches adopted unsupervised or self-supervised learning to leverage large unlabeled DNA datasets, aiming to extract meaningful features without explicit annotations \[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) demonstrated the potential of unsupervised contrastive objectives to learn representations by predicting future samples in latent space, achieving strong performance across multiple modalities such as speech, images, and text \[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. However, such unsupervised methods often do not explicitly incorporate biological regulatory information, which is crucial for understanding DNA function.

In computer vision and natural language processing, supervised and self-supervised pre-training have shown complementary strengths. For example, supervised contrastive learning improves upon traditional cross-entropy loss by clustering samples from the same class closer while pushing apart different classes, leading to more robust and semantically rich embeddings \[[2004.11362](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11362)\]. Similarly, large self-supervised models with appropriate architectural choices, such as Vision Transformers combined with momentum encoders and multi-crop training, have achieved state-of-the-art results on image classification benchmarks, highlighting the importance of architecture and training strategy in representation learning \[[2104.14294](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\]\[[1911.05722](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\].

Unsupervised visual representation learning methods frequently leverage contrastive learning frameworks like SimCLR and MoCo, which utilize data augmentations, negative sampling, and momentum encoders to learn effective features without labels \[[2002.05709](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]\[[2003.04297](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\]\[[1911.05722](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. These methods have been extended to dense prediction tasks by aligning pixel-level features, showing improved transfer to object detection and segmentation \[[2011.09157](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09157)\]. Such advances underline the importance of incorporating domain-specific structure—whether spatial in images or regulatory in genomics—into representation learning objectives.

In the context of DNA modeling, pure sequence-based unsupervised learning approaches face limitations because DNA function is heavily influenced by epigenetic and genomic profiles, such as chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding, which are not directly encoded in nucleotide sequences \[[1905.09272](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. This motivates supervised pre-training on genomic profile prediction tasks, where models learn to predict experimentally measured regulatory signals from sequence inputs. Such supervised objectives provide richer supervision that better captures functional relevance, improving downstream predictive performance \[[1805.01978](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\]\[[2002.05709](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\].

Multi-task and multi-species learning frameworks have been explored to leverage shared information across related domains. Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures have been proposed in vision and language domains to dynamically route inputs to specialized subnetworks (experts), enabling models to capture diverse patterns and relationships more effectively \[[1611.09842](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09842)\]\[[1512.03385](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]\[[2104.14294](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\]. Applying MoE to DNA representation learning allows adaptation to heterogeneous genomic profiles and species-specific characteristics, enhancing model flexibility and predictive power.

Prior studies have applied MoE in computer vision and natural language models, demonstrating gains in performance and parameter efficiency by enabling collaborative expert specialization \[[1611.09842](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09842)\]\[[1512.03385](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. However, integrating MoE with supervised genomic profile prediction remains underexplored. Our SPACE framework is novel in combining these elements to jointly model multi-species and multi-profile genomic data, yielding DNA representations that are both biologically informed and generalizable.

Other unsupervised and self-supervised approaches for DNA representation include context-based prediction tasks such as solving jigsaw puzzles or inpainting missing image regions, adapted analogously to genomic contexts \[[1603.09246](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\]\[[1604.07379](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07379)\]. These methods encourage learning of semantic features but often lack explicit incorporation of regulatory profile supervision or multi-domain adaptation. Similarly, pretext-invariant representation learning aims to produce embeddings invariant to data augmentations, enhancing robustness but without direct supervision from functional genomic data \[[1912.01991](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01991)\].

In summary, while unsupervised contrastive and self-supervised learning have advanced representation learning broadly \[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]\[[2002.05709](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]\[[2003.04297](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\]\[[1911.05722](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\], pure sequence-based DNA models underperform due to missing regulatory context \[[1905.09272](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. Supervised genomic profile prediction offers richer, functionally relevant supervision \[[1805.01978](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\], and the use of MoE architectures facilitates capturing complex multi-species, multi-profile relationships \[[1611.09842](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09842)\]\[[1512.03385](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. Our work builds on these insights by introducing a supervised, MoE-based framework tailored for DNA representation learning, advancing beyond prior methods that either lack regulatory supervision or multi-domain adaptability.
