## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification in textual data has attracted significant research attention due to its ability to capture nuanced emotional states beyond coarse sentiment polarity categories such as positive, negative, and neutral \[[2005.11882](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\]. While sentiment analysis traditionally focuses on valence detection, fine-grained emotion classification aims to identify discrete emotions like joy, anger, fear, and anticipation, which provide richer insights into user attitudes and experiences \[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]\[[2005.11882](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\]. This distinction is critical in domains such as app reviews, where understanding specific emotional responses can inform better product development and user satisfaction strategies.

Several datasets and annotation efforts have advanced the study of fine-grained emotions in text. GoEmotions presents a large-scale dataset of 58k Reddit comments annotated with 27 emotion categories plus neutral, enabling the training and evaluation of models on varied and nuanced emotional states \[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Similarly, the Dataset for Emotions of Narrative Sequences (DENS) extends emotion analysis to long-form narratives, emphasizing the importance of context and demonstrating that fine-tuning pretrained language models like BERT can achieve significant performance improvements on multi-class emotion tasks \[[1910.11769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11769)\]. The PO-EMO dataset further explores complex and mixed aesthetic emotions in poetry, highlighting challenges in emotion classification where multiple emotions co-occur within the same textual units \[[2003.07723](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]. These resources collectively underscore the need for clear annotation guidelines and robust modeling approaches in fine-grained emotion recognition.

In the context of app reviews, however, fine-grained emotion classification has been less explored. Existing opinion mining work in app review analytics largely focuses on identifying informative reviews and extracting general sentiment or feature requests \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572)\]\[[1505.04657](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04657)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af185f391d73e59c5e062de920d456e87c004d87](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/af185f391d73e59c5e062de920d456e87c004d87)\], with limited attention to the detailed emotional states expressed by users. Systems like AR-Miner filter and prioritize user reviews for developers but do not specifically address the complexity of emotion classification \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572)\]. Similarly, keyword-based frameworks like MARK facilitate semi-automated opinion mining but focus on sentiment polarity or feature-level opinions rather than discrete emotions \[[1505.04657](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04657)\]. This gap motivates the development of structured annotation frameworks tailored to app reviews, which must contend with informal language, domain-specific jargon, and subtle emotional cues.

The use of large language models (LLMs) and deep learning architectures has shown promise in automating emotion detection. Models leveraging BERT and gated recurrent neural networks have set new state-of-the-art results in fine-grained emotion classification benchmarks, achieving high accuracy across multiple emotion types and dimensions such as Plutchik’s primary emotions \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]\[[1910.11769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11769)\]. Yet, challenges remain in fully automating annotation due to the inherent subjectivity and complexity of emotional interpretation, particularly in domains requiring domain adaptation like app reviews \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]\[[2005.11882](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\]. Studies evaluating LLMs on emotion annotation highlight their ability to reduce manual effort and achieve substantial agreement with human annotators, although full automation without human oversight is still problematic \[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]\[[2102.12858](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12858)\].

Annotation methodology and reliability are critical to advancing fine-grained emotion classification. Crowdsourcing approaches have been successfully employed to construct large word-emotion lexicons and datasets, with careful question design improving annotation quality and inter-annotator agreement \[[1308.6297](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. Bi-perspectival annotation frameworks distinguishing writer and reader emotions, as well as appraisal-based annotation that considers cognitive evaluations of events, have been proposed to capture richer emotion representations \[[2205.01996](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01996)\]\[[2203.10909](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10909)\]\[[2102.12858](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12858)\]. These approaches provide valuable insights into designing annotation guidelines that balance complexity and consistency, a necessity for domain-specific datasets like those for app reviews.

Lastly, integrating fine-grained emotion classification with user feedback analysis offers promising avenues for enhancing app review mining. Combining emotion detection with stance, sentiment, and feature extraction layers enables more comprehensive understanding of user opinions \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. Studies focusing on emotion classification in social media and narrative texts provide transferable methodological foundations and highlight the importance of domain adaptation and dataset creation tailored to specific textual genres and applications \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66c8ab0b3b7698cf5333c3f7350a6eaa5302294b](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66c8ab0b3b7698cf5333c3f7350a6eaa5302294b)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6ec802c9a6032391c68c75bf5c7b3d0057c17e31](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6ec802c9a6032391c68c75bf5c7b3d0057c17e31)\].

In summary, while fine-grained emotion classification has seen substantial progress through dataset creation, annotation methodologies, and advanced modeling, its application to app reviews remains underdeveloped. Existing work in sentiment analysis and opinion mining for app reviews primarily addresses polarity and feature extraction, with limited focus on detailed emotional states. Leveraging insights from large-scale emotion datasets, crowdsourced lexicons, and appraisal-based annotation frameworks can inform the development of structured annotation processes and automated pipelines suitable for the challenges of emotion detection in app review texts \[[1309.6352](http://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6352)\]\[[2005.00547](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]\[[1910.11769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11769)\]\[[1308.6297](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]\[[2003.07723](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]\[[2205.01996](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01996)\]\[[2203.10909](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10909)\]\[[2005.11882](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572)\]\[[1505.04657](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04657)\]\[[2102.12858](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12858)\].
