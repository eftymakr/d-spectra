## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start issues by leveraging users' historical interactions across multiple domains \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Traditional CDSR approaches typically rely on overlapping users who have interactions in all considered domains to learn cross-domain item relationships, which limits their applicability in practical scenarios where such overlap is scarce or non-existent \[[2106.02768](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. This reliance is often referred to as the overlap dilemma. Moreover, modeling complex transition patterns within mixed user behavior sequences spanning multiple domains—the transition complexity problem—remains a fundamental challenge \[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\].

Early CDSR methods mainly focused on learning single-domain user preferences and then transferring knowledge across domains via latent representation mapping or knowledge transfer modules \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. For instance, CoNet employs dual neural networks with cross connections to enable knowledge transfer between domains, showing improvements over matrix factorization methods \[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Similarly, Mixed Information Flow networks integrate both behavioral and knowledge transfer flows to decide when and what cross-domain information to use, improving sequence representation by capturing cross-domain item relationships \[[2012.00485](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. However, these methods typically assume overlapping users and often ignore inter-sequence item relationships or the full complexity of user behavior transitions \[[2304.03891](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\].

To explicitly model complex transition patterns, graph-based approaches have been proposed. DA-GCN and DDGHM utilize graph convolutional networks to capture intra- and inter-domain sequential transitions, incorporating attention mechanisms to selectively propagate relevant information \[[2105.03300](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]\[[2209.10163](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. MGCL further enhances modeling by applying multi-view graph contrastive learning to jointly learn intra-domain and inter-domain user preferences, addressing sparsity and capturing cross-domain item transition patterns effectively \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206)\]. DREAM introduces decoupled representations and supervised contrastive learning to jointly model single- and cross-domain user preferences while tackling data imbalance and sparsity, demonstrating substantial performance gains \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\].

The overlap dilemma has also motivated research on scenarios with non-overlapping users or items between domains. The Prompt Learning-based Cross-domain Recommender (PLCR) addresses Non-overlapping Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (NCSR) by learning domain-invariant and domain-specific representations without relying on explicit domain alignment or overlapped entities \[[2103.01696](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Other approaches, such as federated learning frameworks and privacy-preserving models (e.g., FMoE and PriCDSR), aim to enable cross-domain knowledge transfer while maintaining user privacy and handling non-overlapping data \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]\[[1804.06769](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These methods extend CDSR’s practicality to industry-scale, privacy-sensitive applications.

Shared-account cross-domain sequential recommendations pose additional challenges, where multiple users share a single account across domains. Models like π-Net and RL-ISN address this by designing user identification and domain filtering mechanisms using attention and reinforcement learning to disentangle mixed behaviors and reduce irrelevant domain information \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[2206.08088](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08088)\]. Parallel Split-Join Networks similarly split and join role-specific representations to improve recommendations under shared-account and cross-domain settings \[[1910.02448](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\].

In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as promising tools for enhancing recommendation systems due to their powerful semantic representation and reasoning capabilities \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2307.02046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. LLM-based methods leverage rich textual item features and external knowledge to capture complex user-item relationships beyond ID-based embeddings. Surveys provide taxonomies categorizing LLM applications in recommendation into discriminative and generative paradigms, highlighting fine-tuning and prompt-tuning as effective adaptation techniques \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2307.02046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. Universal sequence representation learning frameworks, such as UniSRec, employ LLMs to generate transferable item and sequence embeddings across domains and platforms, improving generalization and cross-platform performance \[[2206.05941](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\].

Integrating LLMs into CDSR can potentially address both the overlap dilemma and transition complexity by representing items semantically and capturing nuanced user preferences across domains. Preliminary works explore contrastive learning and adapter modules to align LLM representations with CDSR tasks, facilitating the modeling of complex cross-domain interactions \[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]\[[https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206)\]. The ability of LLMs to generate hierarchical user profiles and encode multi-domain behaviors offers a promising direction to overcome limitations of traditional CDSR models that rely heavily on overlapping user data and struggle with complex sequential transitions \[[2304.04218](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\].

Overall, while conventional CDSR models have advanced the field through graph-based learning, attention mechanisms, and transfer learning, challenges remain in dealing with non-overlapping domains and intricate cross-domain behavior patterns. The fusion of LLMs with CDSR frameworks represents a significant step forward, leveraging semantic understanding to enhance recommendation accuracy and robustness in open-world and large-scale settings \[[2305.19860](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]\[[2311.04590](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]\[[2206.05941](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]\[[2307.02046](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\].
