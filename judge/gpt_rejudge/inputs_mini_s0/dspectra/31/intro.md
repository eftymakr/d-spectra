## Related Works

Recent advances in representation learning have shown that unsupervised and self-supervised pre-training techniques can effectively leverage large unlabeled datasets to learn expressive features across various domains, including images and text. In computer vision, spatial context prediction and solving pretext tasks such as image rotations or jigsaw puzzles have been demonstrated to produce rich visual representations without manual annotations, enabling competitive performance on downstream tasks like object detection and classification \[[1505.05192](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]\[[1803.07728](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\]\[[1603.09246](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\]. Similarly, contrastive learning approaches such as Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) have proven successful in learning useful latent representations by predicting future observations or contrasting positive and negative samples, achieving strong results across modalities including images, speech, and text \[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]\[[1905.09272](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]\[[2005.04966](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\].

In natural language processing, pre-training large transformer models with masked language modeling or autoregressive objectives has been pivotal for state-of-the-art performance on diverse language understanding benchmarks \[[1907.11692](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]\[[2101.03961](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]\[[1910.10683](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. These approaches highlight the effectiveness of leveraging large-scale unlabeled data with suitable pretext tasks and architectures for downstream generalization.

However, when applied to genomic sequence data, pure unsupervised pre-training methods face inherent limitations due to the unique nature of DNA sequences. Unlike natural images or language, DNA function is heavily regulated by complex genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility and epigenetic modifications, which cannot be directly inferred from sequence alone. Prior work focusing solely on sequence-based self-supervised learning or contrastive instance discrimination has shown some promise but lacks incorporation of these regulatory signals, limiting the biological relevance and predictive power of learned representations \[[1406.6909](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.6909)\]\[[1805.01978](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\].

This insight motivates the shift toward supervised pre-training paradigms that leverage experimentally derived genomic profiles to guide representation learning. Supervised training on multi-profile genomic data enables models to capture functional regulatory patterns beyond raw sequence information, improving both interpretability and downstream task performance \[[1206.5538](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]\[[2203.06041](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. Such approaches align with domain adaptation techniques that promote learning invariant features across related but distinct data distributions, addressing challenges posed by multi-species and multi-profile variability in genomic datasets \[[1505.07818](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\].

To better model this multi-faceted nature of genomic data, Mixture of Experts (MoE) architectures have emerged as a powerful paradigm. MoE allows a model to dynamically select specialized expert subnetworks tailored to different input types or domains, enhancing capacity and flexibility without proportional increase in computational cost \[[2101.03961](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]. While MoE has been extensively explored in vision and NLP tasks to handle multimodal or multilingual data, its application to genomic representation learning remains nascent \[[1609.02907](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]\[[1803.07728](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\]. Integrating MoE with supervised genomic profile prediction enables capturing complex inter-species and inter-profile relationships, facilitating more nuanced and effective DNA representations.

Our proposed SPACE framework builds upon this intersection by combining supervised genomic profile pre-training with a multi-task MoE design. This approach leverages the strengths of both supervised learning for functional genomic signals and MoE for handling heterogeneous data, thus addressing limitations of prior sequence-only or single-task models. Prior studies have shown that multi-task and multi-domain learning, when combined with appropriate architectural inductive biases, improve generalization and data efficiency \[[1606.04586](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\]\[[1703.03400](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.03400)\]. SPACE extends these principles specifically to DNA representation learning, achieving state-of-the-art results on various benchmarks.

In summary, while unsupervised and self-supervised learning methods have laid foundational work in representation learning across domains \[[1505.05192](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]\[[1807.03748](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]\[[1803.07728](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\]\[[2005.04966](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\], the complex regulatory biology of DNA necessitates supervised approaches informed by genomic profiles \[[1206.5538](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]\[[2203.06041](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. MoE architectures offer a scalable solution to model multi-species and multi-profile heterogeneity \[[2101.03961](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]\[[1505.07818](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\], and our work is the first to integrate these components for DNA representation learning, enabling more accurate and biologically meaningful models.
