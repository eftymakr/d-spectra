## Related Works

Generative retrieval is a novel information retrieval paradigm that replaces conventional search index structures with a single large-scale neural network, aiming to unify retrieval and generation within an end-to-end differentiable framework. This approach models retrieval as autoregressively generating document identifiers conditioned on a query, thereby eliminating explicit indexing and enabling seamless integration with generative large language models \[[2311.09134](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. Early models demonstrated promising results on small and synthetic datasets, but scaling to large, real-world corpora remains a critical challenge \[[2311.09134](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]\[[2305.11841](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].

Recent advancements have focused on improving both the effectiveness and scalability of generative retrieval models. RIPOR introduced a prefix-oriented ranking optimization algorithm that accurately estimates relevance scores during sequential ID generation and constructs document identifiers based on query-document relevance associations, yielding substantial improvements on large benchmarks like MSMARCO and TREC Deep Learning Track \[[2311.09134](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. Subsequent frameworks such as GBS extended generative retrieval to complex document types like books by employing data augmentation and outline-oriented encoding to preserve hierarchical structure, which significantly enhanced retrieval performance beyond RIPOR \[[2501.11034](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11034)\]. Another line of work, PAG, proposed simultaneous decoding guided by set-based and sequential identifiers to accelerate generation while improving accuracy, achieving both speedups and performance gains \[[2404.146](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\].

Despite these advances, scaling generative retrieval to millions of documents remains an open problem. Empirical studies reveal that naive scaling of model size or architecture modifications does not straightforwardly translate to better retrieval quality at large scale. Synthetic queries as document representations during indexing have been found crucial, but existing generative retrieval models still lag behind strong dual-encoder baselines on very large corpora \[[2305.11841](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This highlights the inherent difficulty of balancing model capacity, training data, and decoding constraints in large-scale generative retrieval.

The decoding strategy, particularly beam search, plays a pivotal role in generative retrieval performance. Standard beam search, while widely used, focuses on maximizing model likelihood and may not align well with retrieval relevance metrics. Research in neural decoding has explored alternatives such as Diverse Beam Search (DBS), which optimizes for output diversity to avoid near-identical sequences, and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), which incorporates metric-driven search heuristics to improve decoding outcomes \[[2104.05336](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\]\[[1610.02424](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02424)\]. These approaches suggest that the choice and design of decoding algorithms can critically affect the retrieval quality and generalization of autoregressive models.

Theoretical analyses of generative retrieval have started to elucidate the effects of constraints and decoding methods on model performance. One study derived a lower bound on the error introduced by imposing corpus-specific constraints during autoregressive generation, quantified via the KL divergence between ground-truth and model-predicted step-wise marginal distributions \[[2311.09134](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. This formalization highlights intrinsic limitations in constrained generation that impact generalization to out-of-distribution corpora. Complementary work on learning-to-rank frameworks for generative retrieval proposed optimizing autoregressive models directly towards ranking objectives, closing the gap between token-level generation and passage-level relevance ranking, and achieving state-of-the-art results \[[2306.15222](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\].

Moreover, pre-trained generative retrieval models like CorpusBrain integrate corpus knowledge directly into model parameters, enabling zero- and low-resource retrieval without explicit indexing, which demonstrates strong applicability across knowledge-intensive language tasks \[[2208.07652](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. Approaches combining semantic search techniques with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems have also shown improvements by blending dense and sparse retrieval strategies, enhancing retrieval accuracy and downstream generation \[[2404.0722](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. Autoregressive search engines that generate substrings as document identifiers further refine retrieval by leveraging n-gram based identifiers to achieve better passage-level performance with reduced memory footprint \[[2204.10628](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\].

In summary, the generative retrieval paradigm is an active research area marked by advances in model design, decoding strategies, and theoretical understanding. The interplay between constrained autoregressive generation, beam search limitations, and error bounds defined by divergence measures forms the core of ongoing efforts to improve generalization and scalability. These works provide a foundation for addressing the inherent challenges of generative retrieval and inspire future innovations towards more robust and generalizable systems \[[2311.09134](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]\[[2501.11034](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11034)\]\[[2305.11841](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]\[[2104.05336](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.05336)\]\[[1610.02424](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02424)\]\[[2208.07652](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]\[[2404.0722](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]\[[2204.10628](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]\[[2306.15222](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]\[[2404.146](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\].
