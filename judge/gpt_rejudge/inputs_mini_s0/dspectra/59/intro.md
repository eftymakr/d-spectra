## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking and recommendation systems has attracted significant attention, particularly regarding the inherent trade-offs between fairness and accuracy. Early approaches focused on balancing provider fairness with ranking quality. For instance, the Fairness-Aware Re-ranking algorithm (FAR) introduced personalized user tolerance to provider diversity, allowing iterative adjustments to the ranking list that trade off accuracy and provider coverage \[[2005.12974](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. Similarly, methods that treat exposure slots as scarce resources have been proposed to enforce provider fairness, using max-min fairness (MMF) to prioritize the utility of the worst-off providers over proportional fairness (PF) \[[1704.06840](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]\[[2404.17826](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. These approaches highlight the multi-stakeholder nature of fairness in ranking, emphasizing fair exposure distribution among item providers.

From a methodological perspective, fairness-aware re-ranking has been explored through various frameworks. A notable contribution is the conceptualization of fair re-ranking as an item-level tax policy, where fairness constraints impose a "tax" on items, affecting the overall ranking utility. The Tax-rank method operationalizes this idea by imposing taxes based on utility differences between items and optimizing the resulting objective using the Sinkhorn algorithm from optimal transport theory. This approach addresses key limitations of prior item-level tax policies by ensuring continuity—small changes in tax rates cause only minor accuracy and fairness shifts—and controllability over accuracy loss \[[1703.09207](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. This economic analogy aligns with the observation that fairness constraints on items translate into costs borne by users in terms of accuracy, analogous to commodity tax elasticity in economics.

Fairness has also been incorporated using demographic representations to ensure group-level fairness in re-ranking tasks. Using labeled demographic data to compute fairness-aware representations, a variation of Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) re-ranking was proposed, demonstrating improved precision and fairness even with limited labeled data \[[1507.05259](http://arxiv.org/abs/1507.05259)\]. This line of work emphasizes demographic fairness as a critical dimension, complementing provider fairness.

Recent developments extend fairness considerations to two-sided marketplaces, where both consumer and producer fairness must be balanced. CPFair introduces an optimization-based re-ranking approach that jointly enforces fairness constraints for consumers and producers without degrading overall recommendation quality, illustrating the complexity of multi-stakeholder fairness \[[1505.05723](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05723)\]. In a similar vein, dynamic learning-to-rank algorithms have been developed to guarantee merit-based fairness for groups while learning from implicit feedback, employing controllers that adapt fairness and utility estimators over time \[[2005.14713](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\].

The fundamental trade-off between accuracy and fairness has been rigorously studied in multiple contexts. Theoretical works have demonstrated the impossibility of simultaneously satisfying all fairness criteria and maximizing accuracy, particularly when base rates differ across protected groups \[[1703.09207](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]\[[1609.05807](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\]\[[1609.07236](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.07236)\]. These results highlight the necessity of carefully choosing and balancing fairness definitions depending on application contexts. Moreover, the notion of fairness elasticity, inspired by economic tax elasticity, provides a quantitative framework for understanding how imposing fairness on item providers impacts user-side ranking accuracy. This elasticity-based view underpins the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve), an evaluation framework that captures performance across varying elasticity levels, enabling comparative assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms \[[1703.09207](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]\[[1809.02921](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\].

Other relevant frameworks include attention-based fairness, which addresses position bias in rankings by ensuring that the attention each individual item receives over multiple rankings is proportional to its relevance, formulated as an online optimization problem solvable via integer linear programming \[[1805.01788](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]. Such approaches expand fairness beyond group-level metrics to individual-level attention fairness.

Fairness in ranking has also been addressed through pairwise comparison metrics that allow tractable fairness measurement and regularization during model training, improving fairness without sacrificing ranking quality \[[1903.00780](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. Additionally, convex fairness regularizers have been proposed for regression problems, enabling fine-grained control over the fairness-accuracy trade-off and quantifying the Price of Fairness (PoF) \[[1706.02409](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02409)\]. These methods emphasize the importance of flexible fairness constraints that can be adapted to different scenarios.

Finally, broader studies on algorithmic fairness have underscored the challenges of defining and auditing fairness, especially when fairness must hold across exponentially many subgroups to prevent fairness gerrymandering \[[1711.05144](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. Learning fair classifiers under such conditions involves computational complexity but can be approached via game-theoretic algorithms that converge to optimal fair classifiers given appropriate oracles \[[1711.05144](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. These advances in fairness auditing and learning complement fair re-ranking techniques by providing rigorous foundations for fairness guarantees.

In summary, the literature on fairness in re-ranking encompasses diverse approaches ranging from personalized fairness-aware re-ranking, economic tax analogies, demographic fairness representations, two-sided fairness optimization, to elasticity-based evaluation frameworks. These works collectively illustrate the nuanced and multifaceted nature of fairness, the unavoidable trade-offs with accuracy, and the necessity for flexible, theoretically grounded, and practically effective methods.
