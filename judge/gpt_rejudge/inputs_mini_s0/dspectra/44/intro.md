## Related Works

The development of collaborative filtering (CF) methods has significantly benefited from advances in graph-based representation learning, particularly through the use of graph neural networks (GNNs). Early approaches, such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), emphasized embedding propagation over the user-item bipartite graph to capture high-order connectivity and explicitly inject collaborative signals into the user and item embeddings \[[1906.01829](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. NGCF demonstrated performance improvements over traditional matrix factorization and shallow methods by leveraging graph convolutional layers to propagate embeddings across the interaction graph. However, NGCF’s inclusion of nonlinear transformations and feature mappings introduced complexity and training difficulties.

Building on this, LightGCN simplified the GCN architecture by removing feature transformation and nonlinear activation, retaining only the essential neighborhood aggregation mechanism. This linear propagation model achieved better efficiency and superior recommendation accuracy over NGCF, highlighting the importance of a streamlined graph convolution design tailored specifically for CF tasks \[[2002.02126](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Additionally, works like Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) extend the basic user-item bipartite graph by incorporating user-user and item-item graphs to enrich embeddings with proximal and similarity information, further improving recommendation quality \[[2001.00267](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\].

While GNNs excel in capturing complex relationships, they suffer from scalability issues, over-smoothing, and sensitivity to noisy interactions, especially in sparse datasets. To mitigate these problems, self-supervised learning techniques have been introduced, such as Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL), which generates multiple corrupted views of nodes and maximizes their agreement to improve robustness and accuracy, particularly for long-tail items \[[2010.10783](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. Complementary to this, Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning (NCL) enhances contrastive objectives by explicitly incorporating structural and semantic neighbors into positive pairs, leading to substantial performance gains in sparse scenarios \[[2202.062](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]. Furthermore, recent work explores knowledge distillation combined with contrastive learning to alleviate over-smoothing and improve scalability, as seen in SimRec, which transfers knowledge from a teacher GNN to a lightweight student model \[[2303.08537](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\].

Representation binarization, which compresses latent embeddings into compact binary codes, has emerged as a promising direction to reduce memory footprint and computational cost during online inference. Binarized Collaborative Filtering (BCF) frameworks leverage GCNs to distill ranking information into binary embeddings, enabling efficient top-K recommendation with implicit feedback. However, binarization inherently risks information loss, which can degrade recommendation performance \[[1906.01829](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Addressing this, some methods reformulate the binary optimization problem into continuous relaxations with stochastic penalties to facilitate effective training and reduce accuracy loss \[[1906.01829](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Despite these advances, most existing binarization approaches focus primarily on numerical quantization without explicitly mitigating the multi-stage information loss in embedding learning and binarization.

Beyond binarization, related efforts in graph representation learning include DeepWalk, which applies random walks and language modeling techniques to learn latent vertex embeddings that capture social relations in graphs, demonstrating effectiveness in sparse label settings \[[1403.6652](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]. GraphSAGE extends this by introducing an inductive framework that aggregates neighborhood features to generate embeddings for unseen nodes, addressing the transductive limitations of traditional graph embedding methods \[[1706.02216](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Simplification of GCNs through removal of nonlinearities and weight collapsing has also been studied to improve scalability and interpretability without compromising accuracy \[[1902.07153](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\].

Other notable directions include representation quality analysis in CF from the perspective of alignment and uniformity of embeddings on the hypersphere, where better alignment and uniformity correlate with improved recommendation performance \[[2206.12811](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.12811)\]. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) adapted for CF provide nonlinear probabilistic generative models capable of surpassing linear factorization baselines, using multinomial likelihood and Bayesian inference to learn expressive user and item representations \[[1802.05814](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\].

In summary, while graph-based CF and binarization methods have individually advanced recommender efficiency and accuracy, there remains a gap in explicitly addressing information loss during the binarization process. The proposed BiGeaR++ framework builds on these foundations by integrating supervisory signals from pseudo-positive samples and enhancing inference distillation and embedding sample synthesis, thereby mitigating information loss across embedding binarization stages and improving recommendation performance.
