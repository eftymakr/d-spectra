## Related Works

Generative retrieval (GR) has gained significant attention as a promising paradigm in information retrieval (IR), offering end-to-end optimization of retrieval tasks by generating document identifiers (docids) directly from queries \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. However, most existing GR models are primarily evaluated on static document collections, with limited studies on their performance in dynamic corpora where document sets evolve continuously \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\]. This section reviews related works that address the challenges of GR in dynamic environments, the design of docids, and the efficiency of retrieval systems.

Several studies have explored the adaptability of GR models to dynamic corpora. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\] highlights that GR models, which apply autoregressive language models to IR, demonstrate better adaptability to evolving knowledge compared to Dual Encoders (DE), which are commonly used in retrieval systems. Additionally, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\] introduces a continual learning framework for GR, addressing the challenge of incrementally indexing new documents while preserving the ability to answer queries with both previously and newly indexed documents. The proposed Continual-LEarner for generative Retrieval (CLEVER) model employs Incremental Product Quantization for efficient encoding of new documents and a memory-augmented learning mechanism to retain knowledge from previous documents.

The design of docids plays a crucial role in the effectiveness of GR models. While numeric-based docids are often favored for their efficiency, they suffer from poor generalization to unseen documents, likely due to overfitting on the training set \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\]. In contrast, text-based docids, which align better with the semantic knowledge of pre-trained language models, show superior performance in dynamic settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\]. This is further supported by \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13859)\], which emphasizes the importance of semantic alignment between docids and language models' pre-trained knowledge for effective retrieval.

Efforts have also been made to improve the efficiency of GR models. For example, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\] proposes a few-shot indexing-based GR framework (Few-Shot GR), which eliminates the need for extensive training by prompting a large language model (LLM) to generate docids for all documents in a corpus. This approach significantly reduces training costs while maintaining performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\].

In addition, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\] introduces GenRet, a document tokenization learning method that learns to tokenize documents into short discrete representations (docids) via a discrete auto-encoding approach. GenRet enables end-to-end modeling of document retrieval tasks and achieves state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\].

### <a id='category-few-shot-learning-approaches'></a>Few-Shot Learning Approaches: 1 papers found.
Few-shot learning approaches have gained attention for their ability to reduce training costs and improve adaptability in dynamic environments. Traditional generative retrieval (GR) methods rely on training-based indexing, which fine-tunes models to memorize associations between queries and document identifiers (docids), leading to high computational costs and limited adaptability to evolving document corpora \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed few-shot indexing-based GR frameworks that eliminate the need for training by prompting large language models (LLMs) to generate docids for all documents in a corpus, creating a docid bank for efficient retrieval \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\]. This approach enhances adaptability by allowing the model to generate relevant docids on-the-fly without retraining, making it suitable for dynamic corpora. Furthermore, few-shot indexing with one-to-many mapping has been introduced to improve performance by enabling the model to generate multiple docids per query, thereby increasing retrieval diversity and effectiveness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\]. These innovations highlight the potential of few-shot learning in overcoming the limitations of traditional training-based indexing, particularly in scenarios where rapid adaptation to new or changing data is essential.

[Snippet]: «Recently, generative retrieval has emerged as a promising alternative to the traditional retrieval paradigms. It assigns each document a unique identifier, known as the DocID, and employs a generative model to directly generate the relevant DocID for the input query. A common choice for the DocID is one or several natural language sequences, e.g. the title, synthetic queries, or n-grams, so that the pre-trained knowledge of the generative model can be effectively utilized. However, a sequence is generated token by token, where only the most likely candidates are kept and the rest are pruned at each decoding step, thus, retrieval fails if any token within the relevant DocID is falsely pruned. What's worse, during decoding, the model can only perceive preceding tokens in the DocID while being blind to subsequent ones, hence is prone to make such errors. To address this problem, we present a novel framework for generative retrieval, dubbed Term-Set Generation (TSGen). Instead of sequences, we use a set of terms as the DocID. The terms are selected based on learned weights from relevance signals, so that they concisely summarize the document's semantics and distinguish it from others. On top of the term-set DocID, we propose a permutation-invariant decoding algorithm, with which the term set can be generated in any permutation yet will always lead to the corresponding document. Remarkably, TSGen perceives all valid terms rather than only the preceding ones at each decoding step. Given the constant decoding space, it can make more reliable decisions due to the broader perspective. TSGen is also resilient to errors: the relevant DocID will not be falsely pruned as long as the decoded term belongs to it. Moreover, TSGen can explore the optimal decoding permutation of the term set on its own, which further improves the likelihood of generating the relevant DocID. Lastly, we design an iterative optimization procedure to incentivize the model to generate the relevant term set in its favorable permutation. We conduct extensive experiments on popular benchmarks of generative retrieval, which validate the effectiveness, the generalizability, the scalability, and the efficiency of TSGen.»

### <a id='category-overfitting-and-generalization-challenges'></a>Overfitting and Generalization Challenges: 4 papers found.
Generative retrieval (GR) has been increasingly explored for its potential to replace traditional search index structures with neural networks, offering improved efficiency and integration with large language models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18952)\]. However, generalization challenges persist, particularly in dynamic corpora where document collections evolve continuously \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Recent studies highlight that GR models with text-based docids demonstrate superior generalization to unseen documents, especially when fine-grained docid designs are employed, surpassing BM25 and even matching dense retrieval methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\]. In contrast, numeric-based docids, while efficient, suffer from significant performance drops in dynamic settings due to overfitting on the training set, leading to an excessive reliance on the initial document set \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. This underperformance is attributed to the constrained auto-regressive decoding process, where the unawareness of future constraints during generation introduces errors measured by KL divergence and Simpson diversity index \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\]. Additionally, beam search algorithms used in generation may not optimally utilize marginal distributions, resulting in poor top-k recall for sparse relevance distributions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13859)\]. These findings underscore the need for more robust and generalizable GR models, particularly in real-world search engines where dynamic corpora are the norm \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09935)\].
