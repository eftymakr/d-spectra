## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction from 2D images, leveraging implicit neural representations to model continuous 3D scenes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. These methods typically take multiple views as input and optimize the reconstructed scene by minimizing geometric and appearance uncertainty across views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often constrained by the number of input views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. In complex and dynamic scenes, where certain angles of objects are never captured, this limitation becomes more pronounced \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. To address this, several works have explored the use of video frame interpolation as a data augmentation strategy for neural rendering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\].

Video frame interpolation has been extensively studied, with various approaches focusing on improving the quality and efficiency of intermediate frame synthesis. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation followed by pixel synthesis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. More recent approaches have integrated these steps into a single convolutional process, using spatially adaptive kernels to account for motion and re-sampling simultaneously \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These methods, however, often require large kernels to handle large motion, which increases memory demands and limits scalability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. To address this, adaptive separable convolutions have been proposed, reducing the number of parameters and enabling efficient frame interpolation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\].

Another line of research has focused on leveraging neural fields for video interpolation, enabling continuous scene representation and spatial-temporal consistency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. The CURE method, for instance, represents videos as neural fields parameterized by spatiotemporal coordinates, allowing for high-quality interpolation across multiple videos \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. Additionally, methods such as the Video Frame Interpolation Transformer have introduced attention mechanisms to better capture long-range dependencies and content-aware aggregation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. These approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in both quantitative and qualitative metrics \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\].

Furthermore, the use of depth-aware techniques has been explored to improve the quality of frame interpolation, particularly in scenarios involving large object motion or occlusion \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. By explicitly detecting occlusion and leveraging depth information, these methods can synthesize more accurate intermediate frames \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Similarly, motion-aware event-based video frame interpolation has been proposed to enhance visual quality by emphasizing moving regions using appropriate loss functions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\].

In addition to these methods, several works have focused on improving the efficiency and robustness of frame interpolation models. For example, the FILM method introduces a single unified network that can handle large motion by optimizing with a Gram matrix loss \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. This approach outperforms state-of-the-art methods on benchmark datasets, demonstrating its effectiveness in synthesizing high-quality, temporally coherent videos \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\].

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-techniques'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Techniques: 16 papers found.
Video frame interpolation techniques have seen significant advancements, particularly with the integration of deep learning approaches. Traditional methods often rely on optical flow estimation followed by pixel synthesis, which can be computationally intensive and limited by motion estimation accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent works have sought to unify these steps into a single process, leveraging deep convolutional neural networks to estimate spatially adaptive kernels for pixel synthesis \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. These models are trained end-to-end using large video datasets without requiring explicit ground-truth annotations, enabling the incorporation of perceptual losses for visually pleasing results \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Additionally, some methods focus on reducing computational complexity, such as using splatting-based synthesis that is faster and achieves high-resolution results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. Others explore unsupervised learning strategies, utilizing cycle consistency to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring additional data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\]. Transformer-based frameworks have also emerged, offering content-aware aggregation and long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms, with local attention and space-time separation strategies to improve efficiency and performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. Furthermore, research has addressed the challenges of real-world scenarios, such as varying exposure times and motion blur, by developing general curvilinear motion trajectory formulas and optical flow refinement strategies \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06684)\]. These advancements collectively highlight the evolving landscape of video frame interpolation, emphasizing efficiency, quality, and adaptability to diverse video content.
