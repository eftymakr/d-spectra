## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area in the context of privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in response to the "right to be forgotten" regulation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. This concept is especially relevant in federated learning (FL), where models are trained across decentralized data sources without sharing raw data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. In federated online learning to rank (FOLTR), the challenge of unlearning becomes even more complex due to the continuous nature of model updates and the need to preserve privacy while optimizing search results based on implicit feedback \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\].

Recent studies have highlighted the limitations of existing unlearning methods, particularly in terms of evaluation practices. Most prior work has relied on single evaluation metrics, which may not comprehensively capture the success of unlearning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19218)\]. This has led to calls for more rigorous evaluation frameworks that can assess both under-unlearning and over-unlearning scenarios \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\]. Our work builds on these insights by introducing a set of adapted and newly proposed evaluation metrics to rigorously assess the effectiveness of unlearning strategies in FOLTR \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to enable efficient and secure federated unlearning. For example, FedEraser \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12502)\] introduces a method that reconstructs an unlearned model by leveraging historical parameter updates stored at the central server, significantly reducing the time required for unlearning compared to retraining from scratch. Similarly, the VeriFi framework \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02216)\] introduces a systematic way to verify the unlearning effect, allowing users to independently confirm that their data has been removed from the global model. This is particularly important in FL, where unlearning effects can be canceled out by contributions from other participants \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\].

In addition to verification, there has been growing interest in the efficiency and scalability of unlearning methods. The FedAU framework \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\] incorporates a lightweight auxiliary unlearning module into the learning process, enabling efficient and effective unlearning without requiring additional time-consuming steps. This approach supports unlearning at various levels of granularity, including individual data samples and entire clients \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\]. Another notable contribution is the FATS framework \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\], which introduces a TV-stable FL algorithm that enables exact federated unlearning with communication efficiency and provable guarantees. This framework ensures that the unlearned model is statistically indistinguishable from the one trained without the deleted data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15474)\].

### <a id='category-unlearning-techniques-and-algorithms'></a>Unlearning Techniques and Algorithms: 14 papers found.
The category "Unlearning Techniques and Algorithms" encompasses a range of methodologies aimed at enabling the removal of specific data contributions from federated learning (FL) models, aligning with the "right to be forgotten" and addressing privacy and security concerns. Federated unlearning (FU) has emerged as a critical area of research, focusing on the efficient and verifiable removal of data from FL models without requiring full retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. This is particularly important given the challenges posed by malicious clients who may inject backdoors into the global model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Existing approaches, such as FedEraser, leverage historical parameter updates to reconstruct unlearned models, significantly reducing the time required compared to retraining from scratch \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19218)\]. However, the verification of unlearning effects remains a challenge, as the unlearning impact on one participant's data can be canceled by contributions from other participants \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\]. To address this, frameworks like VeriFi have been proposed, which allow for systematic analysis and quantification of unlearning effects through marking and checking mechanisms \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\]. Additionally, methods like Ferrari focus on minimizing feature sensitivity to achieve effective feature unlearning in FL \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\]. The need for efficient, accurate, and privacy-preserving unlearning techniques is further emphasized by the limitations of existing methods, which often lack comprehensive evaluation metrics \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09947)\]. These challenges highlight the importance of developing robust and verifiable unlearning strategies that balance privacy, accuracy, and efficiency in FL settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\].

### <a id='category-verification-methods-for-unlearning'></a>Verification Methods for Unlearning: 2 papers found.
The category "Verification Methods for Unlearning" explores the challenges and innovations in verifying data deletion in machine learning systems, particularly in federated learning environments. Existing approaches often struggle with ensuring accurate and domain-specific unlearning, as traditional methods may inadvertently affect non-targeted domains or fail to properly erase data from deeper model layers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. This issue is exacerbated by the complexity of federated domain unlearning, where models are trained across multiple, heterogeneous data sources, making it difficult to isolate and remove specific user data without compromising overall model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. To address these limitations, recent work has introduced formal frameworks for verifying unlearning, leveraging backdoor-based mechanisms to assess whether data deletion has been successfully executed \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. These methods allow users to poison their training data with unique triggers, enabling them to verify if their data has been removed from the model by analyzing the model's predictions on test samples \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. This approach provides a statistically rigorous way to certify unlearning with high confidence, even in the presence of adaptive adversaries attempting to thwart the process \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Such verification mechanisms are crucial for compliance with regulations like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which enshrines the "right to be forgotten" \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. The development of these verification techniques is particularly relevant in the context of Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR), where privacy-preserving collaborative ranking models must support user-initiated data deletion while maintaining search quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. The integration of verification methods into federated unlearning strategies is essential for ensuring both data privacy and system integrity in modern machine learning services.

### <a id='category-challenges-in-federated-unlearning'></a>Challenges in Federated Unlearning: 3 papers found.
The category "Challenges in Federated Unlearning" highlights the unique difficulties faced in applying unlearning techniques within federated learning (FL) environments. Unlike centralized settings, FL introduces complexities such as interactivity, stochasticity, heterogeneity, and limited accessibility, which hinder the direct application of existing unlearning methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. These challenges are further compounded by the need to respect privacy regulations like GDPR and CPRA, which require model owners to support data removal requests \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\]. Research has shown that federated unlearning must address not only the technical feasibility of removing data influence but also the strategic considerations of users requesting data revocation, including the interplay between model performance and unlearning costs \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01235)\]. Additionally, the distributed nature of FL makes it vulnerable to poisoning attacks, where malicious clients can compromise the global model during the unlearning process \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17396)\]. Addressing these security concerns is crucial for developing robust federated unlearning frameworks that ensure both data privacy and model integrity.
