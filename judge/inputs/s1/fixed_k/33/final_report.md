## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have been widely studied in continual learning (CL) to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. These methods store a subset of past data in a memory buffer and reuse it during training on new tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14544)\]. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is commonly used, recent work has questioned its optimality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\]. Inspired by human learning, where revisiting tasks sequentially helps mitigate forgetting, this paper explores whether sequential rehearsal can offer greater benefits compared to standard concurrent rehearsal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\].

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of rehearsal in CL. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14544)\] highlights that models trained sequentially with rehearsal may stay in a low-loss region but risk overfitting on the sample memory, harming generalization. This suggests that rehearsal strategies must balance between maintaining past knowledge and avoiding overfitting. Similarly, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04998)\] examines the role of consistency regularization in experience replay, showing that soft-targets can improve the preservation of previous task information. This aligns with the idea that sequential rehearsal may better maintain past knowledge by leveraging consistency in predictions.

Theoretical analyses of rehearsal-based CL have also been explored. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\] provides a general theoretical analysis of forgetting in linear regression models using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), revealing that task sequence and algorithmic parameters significantly affect forgetting. Specifically, tasks with larger eigenvalues in their population data covariance matrices trained later tend to result in increased forgetting. This insight supports the hypothesis that sequential rehearsal may be more effective when tasks are less similar. \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16939)\] further extends this analysis to overparameterized models, showing that buffer size and model capacity influence the forgetting rate in replay-based CL.

In practice, several methods have been proposed to improve rehearsal-based CL. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13646)\] introduces GRASP, a sample selection policy that prioritizes prototypical samples first, leading to better performance with fewer updates. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21942)\] proposes SPARC, a scalable approach that eliminates the need for experience rehearsal and full-model surrogates, achieving superior performance with a lightweight design. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09330)\] presents a memoryless method combining self-organizing maps with standard neural networks to adaptively cluster inputs into task contexts, preserving past knowledge without explicit memory storage.

Moreover, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\] explores second-order loss approximation to avoid catastrophic forgetting without requiring data memorization, offering better privacy protection. \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.04381)\] introduces sparse routing networks with co-training, which activate different paths for different tasks, minimizing interference between dissimilar tasks. These approaches highlight the diversity of strategies to improve CL

### <a id='category-experience-replay-techniques'></a>Experience Replay Techniques: 5 papers found.
Experience Replay Techniques have been extensively explored to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL). These techniques aim to store and reuse past data to maintain performance on previously learned tasks while learning new ones. For instance, a dual memory system combined with a pseudo-rehearsal mechanism using a deep generative network has been proposed to sequentially learn Atari 2600 games without revisiting past tasks or storing raw data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\]. Similarly, the use of episodic memory replay buffers alongside sparse routing networks has shown effectiveness in reducing interference between dissimilar tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07492)\]. The concept of repetition in data streams, where previously seen objects or tasks reappear naturally, has also been leveraged to improve continual learning, with ensemble-based solutions demonstrating strong performance in exploiting such repetition \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14544)\]. Furthermore, the importance of sequence length and clear specification of CL scenarios has been emphasized in benchmarking and evaluating continual learning strategies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of experience replay in enabling models to learn continuously while retaining prior knowledge, especially in non-stationary environments.

### <a id='category-memoryless-continual-learning-methods'></a>Memoryless Continual Learning Methods: 8 papers found.
The category "Memoryless Continual Learning Methods" explores approaches that aim to mitigate catastrophic forgetting without relying on memory replay or full model surrogates, which are often computationally expensive. These methods focus on developing lightweight, efficient strategies that maintain performance on previously learned tasks while adapting to new ones. For instance, Automated Continual Learning (ACL) trains self-referential neural networks to metalearn their own in-context continual learning algorithms, encoding CL desiderata into metalearning objectives \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00276)\]. Similarly, SPARC proposes a scalable CL approach that eliminates the need for experience rehearsal and full-model surrogates by combining task-specific working memories with task-agnostic semantic memory for cross-task knowledge consolidation, achieving remarkable parameter efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07492)\]. Another method, leveraging self-organizing maps, adaptively clusters inputs into task contexts without explicit labels, enabling memoryless continual learning that performs on par with state-of-the-art approaches \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00276)\]. Additionally, methods like OML directly minimize catastrophic interference by learning representations that accelerate future learning and are robust to forgetting under online updates, offering a complementary approach to existing strategies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17269)\]. Theoretical analyses also contribute to this field, such as the study of sequential rehearsal strategies in overparameterized linear models, which shows that sequential rehearsal can outperform concurrent rehearsal when tasks are less similar, leading to the development of a hybrid rehearsal method \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the shift toward memoryless methods that prioritize efficiency and scalability, addressing the limitations of traditional rehearsal-based techniques \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00276)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07492)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17269)\]\[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\].
