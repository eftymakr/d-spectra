## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have been widely studied in continual learning (CL) to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. These methods store a subset of past data in a memory buffer and reuse it during training on new tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14544)\]. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is commonly used, recent work has questioned its optimality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\]. Inspired by human learning, where revisiting tasks sequentially helps mitigate forgetting, this paper explores whether sequential rehearsal can offer greater benefits compared to standard concurrent rehearsal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\].

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of rehearsal in CL. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14544)\] highlights that models trained sequentially with rehearsal may stay in a low-loss region but risk overfitting on the sample memory, harming generalization. This suggests that rehearsal strategies must balance between maintaining past knowledge and avoiding overfitting. Similarly, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04998)\] examines the role of consistency regularization in experience replay, showing that soft-targets can improve the preservation of previous task information. This aligns with the idea that sequential rehearsal may better maintain past knowledge by leveraging consistency in predictions.

Theoretical analyses of rehearsal-based CL have also been explored. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\] provides a general theoretical analysis of forgetting in linear regression models using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), revealing that task sequence and algorithmic parameters significantly affect forgetting. Specifically, tasks with larger eigenvalues in their population data covariance matrices trained later tend to result in increased forgetting. This insight supports the hypothesis that sequential rehearsal may be more effective when tasks are less similar. \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16939)\] further extends this analysis to overparameterized models, showing that buffer size and model capacity influence the forgetting rate in replay-based CL.

In practice, several methods have been proposed to improve rehearsal-based CL. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13646)\] introduces GRASP, a sample selection policy that prioritizes prototypical samples first, leading to better performance with fewer updates. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21942)\] proposes SPARC, a scalable approach that eliminates the need for experience rehearsal and full-model surrogates, achieving superior performance with a lightweight design. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09330)\] presents a memoryless method combining self-organizing maps with standard neural networks to adaptively cluster inputs into task contexts, preserving past knowledge without explicit memory storage.

Moreover, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\] explores second-order loss approximation to avoid catastrophic forgetting without requiring data memorization, offering better privacy protection. \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.04381)\] introduces sparse routing networks with co-training, which activate different paths for different tasks, minimizing interference between dissimilar tasks. These approaches highlight the diversity of strategies to improve CL
