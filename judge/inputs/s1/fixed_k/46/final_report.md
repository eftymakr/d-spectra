## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start issues by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\]. Traditional approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms within the Transformer architecture to model sequential dependencies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. However, these methods typically incorporate domain-specific modules as additional components without thoroughly examining the core self-attention mechanism, which is inherently capable of capturing complex correlations among user behaviors \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].

Several works have attempted to enhance CDSR by integrating domain-specific knowledge. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588)\] proposes a dual dynamic graph with hybrid metric training to address the challenges of retaining single-domain preferences while integrating cross-domain influence. Similarly, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\] introduces a framework that combines domain-aware graph convolutional networks with attention mechanisms to improve recommendation performance. These methods often rely on overlapping users and assume uniform data distributions across domains, which may not hold in real-world scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18383)\].

Recent studies have also explored the use of large language models (LLMs) to bridge domain gaps and capture semantic relationships \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15369)\]. The LLM4CDSR model, for example, employs a unified representation module and a trainable adapter with contrastive regularization to enhance cross-domain understanding \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\]. This approach demonstrates the potential of LLMs in capturing nuanced user preferences across domains. However, these models often require extensive training and fine-tuning, which can be computationally expensive \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21048)\].

In addition to domain-specific modules, some works focus on improving the self-attention mechanism itself. The attention weight refinement (AWRSR) model, for example, enhances self-attention by paying attention to attention weights, allowing for more refined attention distributions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10325)\]. Similarly, the STOchastic Self-Attention (STOSA) model introduces stochastic Gaussian distributions to encode uncertainty in item representations, improving cold-start performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]. These methods highlight the importance of refining the self-attention mechanism to better capture sequential dependencies.

Another line of research addresses the issue of non-overlapping users in cross-domain scenarios. The Prompt Learning-based Cross-domain Recommender (PLCR) model introduces a pre-trained sequence encoder and a dual-learning target to handle non-overlapping domains \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15694)\]. This approach demonstrates the effectiveness of automated prompting in capturing sequential patterns without requiring explicit domain alignment \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.06035)\].

Furthermore, the use of external attention mechanisms has been explored to enhance graph-based models. The EA-GCL framework, for instance, integrates external attention with graph contrastive learning to robustly capture user behavior patterns

### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation-(cdsr)-overview'></a>Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) Overview: 12 papers found.
Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical area in sequential recommendation, aiming to predict future user interactions by leveraging historical data across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\]. A key challenge lies in effectively capturing cross-domain user preferences, which requires considering both intra-sequence and inter-sequence item interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing approaches often rely on single-domain user preferences and transfer modules to infer cross-domain preferences, but these methods are limited by the bottleneck of the transfer module and neglect inter-sequence relationships \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. To address these limitations, several works have proposed novel frameworks. For instance, C2DSR simultaneously leverages intra- and inter-sequence item relationships and jointly learns single- and cross-domain user preferences through graph neural networks and sequential attentive encoders \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Similarly, the Adaptive Multi-Interest Debiasing framework (AMID) addresses open-world assumptions by improving model adaptability and reducing biases across domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Additionally, the DDGHM framework introduces dual dynamic graph modeling and hybrid metric training to alleviate data sparsity and enhance prediction accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Furthermore, the time interval-enhanced domain-aware graph convolutional network (TiDA-GCN) incorporates time interval information and domain-specific attention mechanisms to improve recommendation performance in shared-account scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18383)\]. These approaches highlight the evolving strategies in CDSR, emphasizing the integration of cross-domain information with sequential modeling techniques to enhance recommendation accuracy and adaptability \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15369)\].

### <a id='category-self-attention-mechanism-in-transformers'></a>Self-Attention Mechanism in Transformers: 7 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models have increasingly relied on self-attention mechanisms to capture complex item-item dependencies within user sequences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\]. However, traditional self-attention models face challenges in accurately modeling higher-order dependencies and mitigating issues like oversmoothing, where hidden representations become too similar to tokens, leading to loss of fine-grained sequential patterns \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. To address these limitations, several studies have proposed novel approaches. For instance, Attention Weight Refinement (AWRSR) enhances self-attention by refining attention weights to better capture item correlations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Similarly, Beyond Self-Attention for Sequential Recommendation (BSARec) leverages Fourier transforms to integrate low and high-frequency information, mitigating oversmoothing and improving recommendation performance \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588)\]. Additionally, STOchastic Self-Attention (STOSA) introduces a probabilistic framework where items are embedded as stochastic Gaussian distributions, allowing for modeling of uncertainty in user behavior \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Another approach, Relation-Aware Kernelized Self-Attention (RKSA), incorporates a latent space to model recommendation contexts using a multivariate skew-normal distribution, enhancing relation-awareness in the self-attention mechanism \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18383)\]. Furthermore, TemProxRec addresses temporal proximity by combining contrastive learning with self-attention to capture both vertical and horizontal temporal patterns in user-item interactions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15369)\]. Lastly, Rec-denoiser improves self-attention robustness by pruning noisy items through trainable binary masks, leading to cleaner attention distributions and better model interpretability \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\]. These studies collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to refine self-attention mechanisms for more accurate and robust sequential recommendation systems.
