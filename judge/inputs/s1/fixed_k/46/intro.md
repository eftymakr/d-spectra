## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start issues by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\]. Traditional approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms within the Transformer architecture to model sequential dependencies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. However, these methods typically incorporate domain-specific modules as additional components without thoroughly examining the core self-attention mechanism, which is inherently capable of capturing complex correlations among user behaviors \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].

Several works have attempted to enhance CDSR by integrating domain-specific knowledge. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588)\] proposes a dual dynamic graph with hybrid metric training to address the challenges of retaining single-domain preferences while integrating cross-domain influence. Similarly, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\] introduces a framework that combines domain-aware graph convolutional networks with attention mechanisms to improve recommendation performance. These methods often rely on overlapping users and assume uniform data distributions across domains, which may not hold in real-world scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18383)\].

Recent studies have also explored the use of large language models (LLMs) to bridge domain gaps and capture semantic relationships \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15369)\]. The LLM4CDSR model, for example, employs a unified representation module and a trainable adapter with contrastive regularization to enhance cross-domain understanding \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\]. This approach demonstrates the potential of LLMs in capturing nuanced user preferences across domains. However, these models often require extensive training and fine-tuning, which can be computationally expensive \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21048)\].

In addition to domain-specific modules, some works focus on improving the self-attention mechanism itself. The attention weight refinement (AWRSR) model, for example, enhances self-attention by paying attention to attention weights, allowing for more refined attention distributions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10325)\]. Similarly, the STOchastic Self-Attention (STOSA) model introduces stochastic Gaussian distributions to encode uncertainty in item representations, improving cold-start performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]. These methods highlight the importance of refining the self-attention mechanism to better capture sequential dependencies.

Another line of research addresses the issue of non-overlapping users in cross-domain scenarios. The Prompt Learning-based Cross-domain Recommender (PLCR) model introduces a pre-trained sequence encoder and a dual-learning target to handle non-overlapping domains \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15694)\]. This approach demonstrates the effectiveness of automated prompting in capturing sequential patterns without requiring explicit domain alignment \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.06035)\].

Furthermore, the use of external attention mechanisms has been explored to enhance graph-based models. The EA-GCL framework, for instance, integrates external attention with graph contrastive learning to robustly capture user behavior patterns
