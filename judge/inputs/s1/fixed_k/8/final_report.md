## Related Works

Fairness evaluation in machine learning has garnered significant attention, particularly in the context of large language models (LLMs). Traditional fairness metrics often focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, such as Equalized Odds \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\], which assess whether predictions are accurate across different groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\]. However, these metrics fail to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty, such as higher confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\]. This limitation has been highlighted in several studies, including those examining bias in LLMs across various domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\]. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\] emphasizes that fairness risks vary across deployment contexts and that existing approaches lack systematic guidance for selecting appropriate evaluation metrics. Similarly, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\] discusses the challenges of translating inclusive language, such as the singular 'they' pronoun, and highlights persistent gender biases in machine translation systems. These findings underscore the need for more nuanced fairness metrics that account for model confidence and uncertainty \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\].

Recent work has also addressed the limitations of fairness datasets. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\] identifies the insufficiency of existing fairness metrics in collaborative filtering systems and proposes four new metrics to better measure fairness. These metrics are designed to address different forms of unfairness and can be optimized by incorporating fairness terms into the learning objective. Similarly, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13555)\] introduces a comprehensive framework for benchmarking the fairness of image upsampling methods, emphasizing the importance of using an unbiased training set and revealing variations in how algorithms respond to dataset imbalances. These studies highlight the broader need for diverse and representative datasets to evaluate fairness in various applications \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13555)\].

In the context of LLMs, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\] evaluates gender, racial, and age biases in occupational and crime scenarios, revealing significant deviations from demographic data. The study highlights that efforts to reduce bias can sometimes lead to over-indexing of subgroups, potentially exacerbating the issue. This finding aligns with the broader challenges of fairness in deep learning, as discussed in \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\], which argues that traditional notions of fairness based solely on model outputs are insufficient when models are vulnerable to adversarial attacks. The paper proposes methods to measure robustness bias, which is a critical factor in auditing real-world systems \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\].

Furthermore, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06922)\] explores how ML practitioners use fairness toolkits in practice, identifying opportunities for these tools to better address practitioner needs. This study underscores the importance of designing fairness toolkits that support contextualization, communication, and collaboration around fairness efforts \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06922)\].
