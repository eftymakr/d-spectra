## Related Works

De novo genome assembly has been a focal point of computational biology for decades, with significant advancements in both algorithmic approaches and hardware acceleration. Early methods relied on de Bruijn graphs to model overlapping k-mers, but these approaches faced limitations in terms of memory usage and computational efficiency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. The exponential growth of genomic data has necessitated scalable, high-performance solutions, particularly for large and complex genomes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\]. 

Several studies have explored distributed-memory parallelism to address the computational demands of de novo assembly. For instance, the HipMer assembler was designed for extreme-scale analysis, leveraging efficient parallelization of the Meraculous code to handle large supercomputers with tens of thousands of cores \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\]. Similarly, the PPA-assembler toolkit adopted a de Bruijn graph-based approach and ran on Google's Pregel framework, demonstrating superior performance compared to existing assemblers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04453)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of parallelism in managing the computational complexity of genome assembly.

Recent work has also focused on reducing memory footprints and improving scalability. SparseAssembler introduced a sparse de Bruijn graph structure, significantly lowering memory requirements while maintaining comparable assembly quality \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. This was further refined in SparseAssembler2, which used a sparse k-mer graph to achieve similar memory efficiency and improved robustness \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556)\]. These innovations are crucial for handling the large-scale genomic data now being generated.

In addition to algorithmic improvements, hardware-software co-design has emerged as a promising avenue for accelerating genome assembly. The diBELLA 2D pipeline demonstrated near-linear scaling with over 80% parallel efficiency for the human genome, reducing runtime for overlap detection by 1.2–1.3× compared to state-of-the-art methods \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055)\]. Similarly, the ntLink tool leveraged long-read sequencing data to improve scaffolding and mapping, offering a resource-efficient approach for genome assembly \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08785)\]. These tools reflect the ongoing efforts to enhance both the accuracy and efficiency of de novo assembly.

Quantum computing has also been explored as a potential solution for genome assembly, with quantum and quantum-inspired optimization techniques showing promise in solving complex computational problems \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06719)\]. However, these approaches are still in early stages and have not yet been widely adopted in mainstream bioinformatics pipelines.
