## Related Works

De novo genome assembly has been a focal point of computational biology for decades, with significant advancements in both algorithmic approaches and hardware acceleration. Early methods relied on de Bruijn graphs to model overlapping k-mers, but these approaches faced limitations in terms of memory usage and computational efficiency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. The exponential growth of genomic data has necessitated scalable, high-performance solutions, particularly for large and complex genomes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\]. 

Several studies have explored distributed-memory parallelism to address the computational demands of de novo assembly. For instance, the HipMer assembler was designed for extreme-scale analysis, leveraging efficient parallelization of the Meraculous code to handle large supercomputers with tens of thousands of cores \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\]. Similarly, the PPA-assembler toolkit adopted a de Bruijn graph-based approach and ran on Google's Pregel framework, demonstrating superior performance compared to existing assemblers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04453)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of parallelism in managing the computational complexity of genome assembly.

Recent work has also focused on reducing memory footprints and improving scalability. SparseAssembler introduced a sparse de Bruijn graph structure, significantly lowering memory requirements while maintaining comparable assembly quality \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. This was further refined in SparseAssembler2, which used a sparse k-mer graph to achieve similar memory efficiency and improved robustness \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556)\]. These innovations are crucial for handling the large-scale genomic data now being generated.

In addition to algorithmic improvements, hardware-software co-design has emerged as a promising avenue for accelerating genome assembly. The diBELLA 2D pipeline demonstrated near-linear scaling with over 80% parallel efficiency for the human genome, reducing runtime for overlap detection by 1.2–1.3× compared to state-of-the-art methods \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055)\]. Similarly, the ntLink tool leveraged long-read sequencing data to improve scaffolding and mapping, offering a resource-efficient approach for genome assembly \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08785)\]. These tools reflect the ongoing efforts to enhance both the accuracy and efficiency of de novo assembly.

Quantum computing has also been explored as a potential solution for genome assembly, with quantum and quantum-inspired optimization techniques showing promise in solving complex computational problems \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06719)\]. However, these approaches are still in early stages and have not yet been widely adopted in mainstream bioinformatics pipelines.

### <a id='category-sparse-k-mer-graph-approaches'></a>Sparse k-mer graph approaches: 4 papers found.
Sparse k-mer graph approaches have emerged as a promising solution to address the computational and memory challenges of de novo genome assembly. These methods aim to reduce the memory footprint by constructing sparse representations of k-mer data, which significantly lowers the storage requirements compared to traditional de Bruijn graph-based algorithms \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. For instance, SparseAssembler introduces a sparse de Bruijn graph structure that skips intermediate k-mers, reducing the theoretical memory requirement to approximately (2k/g + 8)N, where g is a parameter that controls the sparsity \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. A practical value of g = 16 reduces memory consumption by about 90% while maintaining comparable assembly quality \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556)\]. Additionally, these approaches incorporate strategies to handle sequencing errors and polymorphisms, such as a Dijkstra-like breadth-first search algorithm tailored for sparse k-mer graphs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. Furthermore, the use of sparse k-mer graphs allows for more efficient traversal and assembly by focusing on a subset of k-mers that are more likely to contribute to accurate contig construction \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556)\]. This shift from dense to sparse representations not only improves memory efficiency but also enhances the robustness of genome assembly, especially in the presence of high error rates or repetitive genomic regions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\]. The development of such methods highlights a growing trend toward more scalable and adaptive solutions for handling the increasing complexity of genomic data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556)\].

### <a id='category-performance-benchmarking-of-assemblers'></a>Performance benchmarking of assemblers: 3 papers found.
Performance benchmarking of assemblers has been a critical area of research, particularly in the context of de novo genome and transcriptome assembly. Recent studies have highlighted the challenges of handling large data volumes and algorithmic complexity, especially with the advent of long-read sequencing technologies such as SMRT and Oxford Nanopore \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\]. These technologies produce reads over 10 kb in length, enabling high-quality genome assembly at a lower cost, but they also introduce significant computational challenges due to high error rates and the need for complex pipelines \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\]. In transcriptome assembly, de novo methods face additional difficulties, such as alternative splicing and variable expression levels, which have been addressed by specialized assemblers like SOAPdenovo-Trans, which outperforms existing tools in terms of contiguity, redundancy, and speed \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04453)\]. Similarly, in molecule generation for drug design, de novo methods often suffer from data inefficiency, requiring large training datasets or extensive sampling to optimize results, especially when combined with computationally expensive scoring functions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147)\]. To improve sample efficiency and chemical desirability, researchers have proposed methods like Augmented Hill-Climb, which has shown promising results in balancing efficiency and quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147)\]. These benchmarking efforts underscore the importance of evaluating both computational performance and the quality of generated sequences, ensuring that assemblers and generative models meet the demands of real-world applications.
