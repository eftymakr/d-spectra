## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has garnered significant attention in recent years, driven by the increasing deployment of recommendation systems in socially impactful domains such as employment and housing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. Early work primarily focused on optimizing recommendation accuracy while ensuring fairness across sensitive attributes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. However, these approaches often overlooked the role of individual preferences in limiting the ability of algorithms to produce fair recommendations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. This gap has been addressed by more recent methods that incorporate personalized preferences and multiple fairness dimensions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\].

The trade-off between accuracy and fairness in recommendation systems has been extensively studied. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\] highlights that fairness-aware re-ranking methods often face challenges in maintaining continuity and controllability over accuracy loss. This issue is further explored in \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120)\], where the authors propose a bankruptcy-inspired approach to balance fairness and accuracy under fluctuating user traffic. Their method, named BankFair, leverages the Talmud rule to ensure consistent user service while upholding long-term fairness \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120)\].

In addition to fairness, diversity and coverage have emerged as critical aspects of recommendation quality. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04250)\] introduces a post-processing re-ranking model that prioritizes provider fairness while maintaining user relevance and recommendation quality. Their work underscores the importance of considering beyond-accuracy metrics such as diversity and novelty when evaluating fairness-aware recommendation systems \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04250)\].

The concept of elasticity has also been introduced to model the trade-off between fairness and accuracy. \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\] draws an analogy between re-ranking and economic transactions, suggesting that the accuracy-fairness trade-off parallels the coupling of the commodity tax transfer process. This insight has led to the development of the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) as an evaluation framework \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. The EF-Curve enables a comparative analysis of algorithm performance across different elasticity levels, facilitating the selection of the most suitable approach \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of fairness-aware re-ranking methods. \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] proposes a fairness-aware variation of the Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) re-ranking method, which incorporates fairness with respect to demographic groups. Their experiments show that this method can incorporate fairness in the ranked results while obtaining higher precision than the baseline \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Similarly, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\] presents an optimization-based re-ranking approach that integrates fairness constraints from both the consumer and producer-side in a joint objective framework. Their method demonstrates the ability to improve both consumer and producer fairness without reducing overall recommendation quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].

In summary, the field of fairness-aware re-ranking has evolved significantly, with a growing emphasis on balancing accuracy, fairness, and other quality metrics.
