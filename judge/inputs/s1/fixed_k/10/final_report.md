Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike traditional next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP extends the prediction horizon by forecasting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been explored in various works, each proposing different strategies to enhance the effectiveness of MTP. For instance, L-MTP introduces a leap-based mechanism that allows the model to predict non-sequential tokens in a single forward pass, enhancing long-range dependency capture and inference efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Similarly, Future Token Prediction (FTP) generates embedding vectors for each token position that better capture the overall meaning of longer sequences, leading to improved topic coherence in generated text \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18160)\]. 

However, the application of MTP has not been without challenges. Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) in language model pretraining, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. Unlike NTP, which predicts one token at a time, MTP involves predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple output heads, enabling parallel processing and reducing inference latency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. Several studies have explored the benefits of MTP, such as enhanced sample efficiency and better handling of long-range dependencies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. However, challenges remain, particularly with smaller language models (SLMs), which struggle to fully leverage the MTP objective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed curriculum learning strategies, including forward and reverse curricula, to gradually adapt models to MTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. The forward curriculum starts with NTP and gradually increases the number of tokens predicted, while the reverse curriculum begins with MTP and reduces complexity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. These approaches aim to improve the effectiveness of MTP for SLMs while retaining the advantages of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Additionally, methods like leap multi-token prediction (L-MTP) have been introduced to enhance inference efficiency by skipping intermediate tokens during prediction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Other techniques, such as joint multi-token prediction (JTP), focus on enriching hidden state representations by jointly predicting multiple future tokens with minimal computational overhead \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\]. Meanwhile, approaches like Future Token Prediction (FTP) aim to generate embedding vectors that better capture the overall meaning of longer sequences by projecting top layer embeddings to pseudo-sequences for cross-attention \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10518)\]. These diverse methods highlight the ongoing research into optimizing MTP for various model sizes and tasks, with a focus on balancing performance and efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10518)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18160)\].
