## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification in app reviews has received increasing attention in recent years due to its potential to uncover nuanced user sentiments and improve app development strategies. While sentiment polarity analysis (e.g., positive, negative, neutral) has been extensively studied \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09739)\], the task of identifying more detailed emotional states remains underexplored. This section reviews relevant works that have contributed to the understanding of emotion classification in text, particularly in the context of app reviews and user feedback.

Several studies have focused on the annotation of emotions in textual data, highlighting the challenges of subjective interpretation and the need for structured frameworks. For instance, GoEmotions is a large-scale dataset of 58,000 Reddit comments, annotated with 27 emotion categories, which has been used to evaluate and improve emotion classification models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Similarly, the PO-EMO dataset explores aesthetic emotions in poetry, emphasizing the complexity of mixed and subtle emotional responses \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]. These datasets underscore the importance of fine-grained emotion labeling and the need for robust annotation guidelines.

In the context of app reviews, the emotional sentiment of users has been analyzed to gain insights into their satisfaction and behavior. A study by \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\] analyzed over 7 million Apple AppStore reviews, demonstrating the potential of sentiment analysis to inform software engineering practices. Another work \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10816)\] explored the characteristics of app reviews and their human-written responses, identifying patterns that could aid in the development of automated responding systems. These studies highlight the growing interest in leveraging sentiment analysis for app development and user engagement.

The use of large language models (LLMs) for emotion annotation has also gained traction. A recent study \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\] evaluated GPT-4’s performance on emotion annotation tasks, finding that it achieves high ratings in human evaluations, although differences between human and LLM annotations persist. This work suggests that LLMs can be integrated into emotion annotation pipelines to reduce manual effort and improve efficiency, while still preserving human input for quality control.

Furthermore, the application of encoder-only large language models for mobile app review feature extraction has shown promising results \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01063)\]. These models, when fine-tuned on annotated datasets, can improve the precision and recall of extracted features, enhancing the overall performance of sentiment and emotion analysis systems.

In summary, while sentiment polarity analysis has been widely studied, the field of fine-grained emotion classification in app reviews is still evolving. Additionally, research has explored the use of multi-task learning frameworks to enhance emotion prediction by modeling emotion definitions as an auxiliary task \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\]. These datasets and methods collectively highlight the importance of fine-grained emotion classification in various applications, from chatbots to sentiment analysis in social media and dialogue systems \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04367)\].

### <a id='category-annotation-frameworks-for-emotion-labeling'></a>Annotation Frameworks for Emotion Labeling: 1 papers found.
Document 1 discusses the formulation of automatic emotion categorization as text classification, typically using predefined emotion inventories such as Ekman's basic emotions (fear, joy, anger, disgust, sadness, surprise) and Plutchik's expanded set including trust and anticipation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.14155)\]. This approach often overlooks psychological theories that explain how events are perceived and interpreted, such as the cognitive appraisal theory, which suggests that emotions arise from the evaluation of situations \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.14155)\]. For example, the discovery of a snake is associated with fear due to the appraisal of the situation as unpleasant and uncontrollable \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.14155)\]. The paper emphasizes the importance of capturing these appraisal dimensions in event descriptions to improve emotion classification accuracy \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.14155)\].
