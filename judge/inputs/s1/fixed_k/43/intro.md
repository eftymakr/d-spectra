## Related Works

Performance modeling for large-scale machine learning systems has been an active research area, with several studies focusing on the challenges of accurately predicting execution times and optimizing resource utilization. Early work on performance modeling for dense linear algebra algorithms emphasized the importance of ranking equivalent algorithms based on their performance characteristics, rather than creating highly accurate models for individual algorithms \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2364)\]. This approach laid the groundwork for more sophisticated models that could generalize across different architectures and workloads.

Recent advances in performance modeling have leveraged machine learning techniques to improve prediction accuracy. For instance, the LLMPerf study explored the use of large language models (LLMs) to estimate performance for OpenCL device source programs, achieving a mean absolute percentage error of 24.25% on a generated validation set \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11244)\]. However, this work also highlighted the difficulties in applying LLMs to performance modeling tasks, particularly in the context of complex and heterogeneous hardware environments.

The LLM-Pilot system further advanced the field by introducing a framework for characterizing and predicting the performance of LLM inference services across different hardware configurations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02425)\]. This system not only benchmarks LLM inference services under realistic workloads but also learns predictive models to recommend cost-effective hardware for new models. LLM-Pilot demonstrated significant improvements over existing methods, achieving 33% higher performance satisfaction and 60% lower costs.

In the domain of distributed training, the Proteus simulator was introduced to model the performance of complex parallelization strategies by simulating execution time and runtime behaviors \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02267)\]. Proteus uses a hierarchical top-aware executor to simulate comp-comm overlap and bandwidth sharing, achieving a 3.0% average prediction error. This work highlights the importance of simulating complex runtime behaviors to accurately model distributed training performance.

Additionally, the work on distributed training of deep learning models has emphasized the need for scalable performance modeling techniques that can handle the increasing complexity of modern training systems \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03970)\]. The CM-DARE framework, for example, enables the analysis of distributed training performance under diverse cluster configurations, demonstrating the feasibility of using regression-based models to predict training speed and overhead \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03072)\].

The AIWC tool has also contributed to the field by providing architecture-independent features for characterizing OpenCL kernels, enabling accurate performance prediction across different compute devices \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00156)\]. This methodology has shown high accuracy, with an average error of just 1.2% in predicting execution times for a wide range of kernels.
