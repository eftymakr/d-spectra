## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a critical research area in recommender systems, aiming to improve recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Traditional approaches often assume a single structure for transferring domain-shared knowledge, which may not be optimal due to the varying relevance and quality of domain-specific information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. This limitation leads to the **Negative Transfer Problem (NTP)**, where unselective knowledge transfer can degrade performance, especially when domains conflict or contain poor-quality data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. 

Several studies have addressed the NTP by focusing on domain adaptation and knowledge transfer. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\] investigates Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR), where the effectiveness of systems depends on the interplay among multiple domains. The paper proposes a framework that assesses the extent of negative transfer and adaptively assigns low weights to prediction losses from problematic domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\] introduces a hierarchical hypergraph network for MDR, which captures correlative preferences and eliminates domain discrepancy in item representations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. This approach uses two hyperedge-based modules to improve user and item representations by exploiting high-order connections \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\].

Another line of research focuses on **domain-specific adaptation** and **knowledge disentanglement**. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05877)\] proposes a framework called MDAP, which disentangles user preferences across domains and adaptively combines embeddings to generate a comprehensive user representation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05877)\]. This model enhances adaptability by allowing feature selection and representation disentanglement. In contrast, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\] introduces EDDA, a method that disentangles embeddings and aligns domains by leveraging random walks to identify similar user-item pairs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\]. This improves knowledge transfer, especially in domains with small overlaps \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\].

Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\] presents a scalable MDR platform, MAMDR, which addresses domain conflicts and overfitting by using a model-agnostic learning framework. It incorporates Domain Negotiation (DN) and Domain Regularization (DR) strategies to enhance generalizability and scalability \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17374)\] introduces HAMUR, a hyper-network-based model that dynamically generates domain-specific parameters, reducing parameter interference and improving adaptability \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17374)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of **domain-specific adaptation** and **parameter management** in MDR systems.

Multi-domain recommendation (MDR) systems aim to improve recommendation performance by leveraging transferable knowledge across different domains, yet existing methods often suffer from the negative transfer problem (NTP) due to unselective knowledge transfer \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. This issue is exacerbated when domains exhibit conflicting knowledge or poor quality, leading to suboptimal recommendations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. To address this, recent works have focused on dynamic domain selection mechanisms to mitigate NTP. For instance, the Multi-Sparse-Domain Collaborative Recommendation (MSDCR) model treats all domains as sparse and employs a Multi-Domain Separation Network (MDSN) and Gated Aspect Preference Enhancement (GAPE) module to preserve domain-specific preferences while transferring complementary aspects from other domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Similarly, the Domain-to-Domain Translation Model (D2D-TM) leverages generative adversarial networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and Cycle-Consistency (CC) to extract both homogeneous and divergent features across domains, enabling more effective knowledge transfer \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\]. The Multi-Domain Recommendation to Attract Users (MDRAU) task further highlights the need for modeling user preferences at both domain and item levels, using masked domain modeling to learn seen-unseen domain mappings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Additionally, the MAGRec model employs graph neural networks to capture temporal intra- and inter-domain interactions, learning domain-guided and general sequential representations to mitigate negative knowledge transfer \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05877)\]. These approaches collectively emphasize the importance of dynamic domain selection, domain-specific modeling, and the integration of both domain-invariant and domain-specific features to enhance multi-domain recommendation performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05877)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11191)\].

[Snippet]: «This paper investigates Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR), a promising method that uses information from multiple domains (more than three) to generate accurate and diverse recommendations, and takes into account the sequential nature of user interactions. The effectiveness of these systems often depends on the complex interplay among the multiple domains. In this dynamic landscape, the problem of negative transfer arises, where heterogeneous knowledge between dissimilar domains leads to performance degradation due to differences in user preferences across these domains. As a remedy, we propose a new CDSR framework that addresses the problem of negative transfer by assessing the extent of negative transfer from one domain to another and adaptively assigning low weight values to the corresponding prediction losses. To this end, the amount of negative transfer is estimated by measuring the marginal contribution of each domain to model performance based on a cooperative game theory. In addition, a hierarchical contrastive learning approach that incorporates information from the sequence of coarse-level categories into that of fine-level categories (e.g., item level) when implementing contrastive learning was developed to mitigate negative transfer. Despite the potentially low relevance between domains at the fine-level, there may be higher relevance at the category level due to its generalised and broader preferences. We show that our model is superior to prior works in terms of model performance on two real-world datasets across ten different domains.»

We compare EDDA with 12 state-of-the-art baselines on 3 real datasets. The results show that EDDA consistently outperforms the baselines on all datasets and domains.
