## Related Works

Personalized outfit generation has been a focal point in recent fashion technology research, with a growing emphasis on generating compatible and personalized fashion items. Early approaches to outfit recommendation were constrained by pre-defined outfit sets or simple item compatibility rules, limiting their ability to address diverse user preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. More recent work has introduced generative models to create new outfits based on user preferences or partial inputs, enabling greater flexibility and personalization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17279)\]. However, these models often suffer from limitations in diversity and reliance on supervised learning paradigms, which can hinder their adaptability to real-world scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16353)\].

Several studies have explored the use of deep learning and generative models for outfit generation. For instance, the POG model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\] leverages Transformer architecture to connect user preferences with outfit generation, demonstrating improved compatibility and personalization metrics. Similarly, the COutfitGAN framework \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08674)\] introduces a comprehensive outfit generation pipeline that includes a pyramid style extractor and a UNet-based discriminator, achieving high-quality and compatible outfit synthesis. These models primarily focus on compatibility and visual realism but often lack mechanisms for incorporating user preferences or diversity in generated outfits.

To address the limitations of supervised learning, some researchers have proposed unsupervised or semi-supervised approaches. The work in \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08052)\] introduces a semi-supervised learning approach for fashion compatibility prediction, leveraging large unlabeled fashion corpora to create pseudo-outfits during training. This method significantly reduces the need for extensive labeled data while maintaining performance comparable to fully supervised methods. Similarly, the Hierarchical Fashion Graph Network (HFGN) \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12566)\] unifies fashion compatibility modeling and personalized outfit recommendation by integrating user-outfit and outfit-item relationships within a hierarchical graph structure, leading to more accurate and personalized recommendations.

Recent advances in diffusion models have also contributed to the field. The DiFashion model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17279)\] employs diffusion-based generation to produce high-fidelity and visually compatible outfits, guided by user-specific conditions. This approach enhances the alignment between generated images and user preferences through Classifier-Free-Guidance.
