## Related Works

Deploying large language models (LLMs) on edge devices has garnered significant attention due to its potential to enhance privacy and reduce latency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03745)\]. However, the computational and memory demands of LLMs pose significant challenges for resource-constrained edge hardware \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00462)\]. Several recent works have explored techniques to optimize LLM inference on edge devices, focusing on both algorithmic and hardware-level improvements.

Early efforts in this domain focused on reducing the computational footprint of LLMs through model compression techniques such as pruning and quantization \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00531)\]. Pruning methods aim to remove redundant parameters, while quantization reduces the precision of weights and activations to lower memory and bandwidth usage \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15654)\]. These techniques have been applied to various LLM architectures, including transformer-based models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01698)\]. However, these methods often require careful tuning to maintain model accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04466)\].

Hardware acceleration has also been a key area of research for improving LLM inference on edge devices. FPGA-based accelerators have been proposed as a viable solution due to their reconfigurability and ability to support model-specific optimizations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07840)\]. For instance, AccLLM leverages an FPGA-based accelerator with a reconfigurable computing engine (RCE) to support algorithmic innovations such as $\Lambda$-shaped attention and W2A8KV4 quantization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03745)\]. Similarly, SECDA-LLM introduces a new design platform to streamline the development of FPGA-based LLM accelerators, demonstrating significant latency improvements over CPU-based inference \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00462)\].

Another approach to edge inference involves distributed computing and collaborative inference across multiple devices \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00531)\]. TPI-LLM, for example, employs tensor parallelism to enable the inference of 70B-scale models on memory-limited devices, reducing peak memory usage by 90% \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15654)\]. Similarly, Jupiter introduces a pipelined architecture to optimize both the prefill and decoding phases of generative LLMs, achieving up to 26.1× latency reduction \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01698)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of system-level optimizations to balance latency, energy consumption, and model accuracy.

### <a id='category-algorithm-hardware-co-design'></a>Algorithm-Hardware Co-Design: 3 papers found.
The category "Algorithm-Hardware Co-Design" focuses on the integration of algorithmic optimizations with hardware-specific implementations to enhance the performance and efficiency of large language models (LLMs) on edge devices. This is exemplified by AccLLM, which combines pruning, $\Lambda$-shaped attention, and W2A8KV4 quantization to reduce memory and bandwidth usage, enabling efficient long-context inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03745)\]. These algorithmic improvements are complemented by hardware-level innovations, such as FPGA-based accelerators with reconfigurable computing engines, which adapt to the compressed model structures and deliver significant gains in energy efficiency and throughput \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03745)\]. Similarly, SECDA introduces a hardware/software co-design methodology that reduces development overhead by leveraging SystemC simulation and hardware execution, enabling faster design exploration and optimization of DNN accelerators on edge FPGAs \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00478)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of co-design in addressing the unique constraints of edge computing, such as limited resources and power, while maintaining model accuracy and low latency. Additionally, MIST provides a simulation framework that models multi-stage LLM inference pipelines across diverse hardware platforms, supporting heterogeneous clients and advanced batching strategies to optimize performance in hybrid CPU-accelerator deployments \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09775)\]. Together, these works underscore the critical role of algorithm-hardware co-design in advancing the deployment of LLMs on edge devices.

### <a id='category-hardware-acceleration-for-llms'></a>Hardware Acceleration for LLMs: 6 papers found.
Hardware acceleration for large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a critical area of research, particularly for enabling efficient deployment on edge devices with limited resources. Several approaches have been proposed to address the computational and memory demands of LLMs on such platforms. For instance, FPGA-based accelerators are highlighted for their reconfigurability and performance per watt, allowing model-specific optimizations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03745)\]. A notable effort in this direction is the SECDA-LLM platform, which streamlines the design and deployment of FPGA-based accelerators for LLM inference, demonstrating significant latency reductions compared to CPU-based execution \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00462)\]. Similarly, Cambricon-LLM introduces a hybrid architecture combining NPU and NAND flash chips to minimize data movement overhead and enable efficient on-device inference of large models, achieving speeds up to 36.34 tokens/s for 7B LLMs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00531)\]. Additionally, the GenZ tool provides an analytical framework to evaluate the trade-offs between LLM model architectures, serving optimizations, and hardware design parameters, aiding in the development of next-generation AI accelerators \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15654)\]. Research also highlights the importance of hardware-level optimizations such as speculative decoding, as demonstrated by the HADES approach, which enhances LLM performance and energy efficiency through novel hardware designs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01698)\]. These works collectively emphasize the need for tailored hardware solutions that balance performance, energy efficiency, and adaptability to the evolving landscape of LLMs.

### <a id='category-edge-computing-frameworks'></a>Edge Computing Frameworks: 8 papers found.
Edge computing frameworks have been increasingly explored to address the challenges of deploying large language models (LLMs) on resource-constrained edge devices, with a focus on improving inference efficiency, reducing latency, and ensuring data privacy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00531)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to optimize LLM inference on edge devices, such as TPI-LLM, which introduces a sliding window memory scheduler and overlaps disk I/O latency with computation and communication to enable efficient execution of 70B-scale models on memory-limited devices \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00531)\]. Similarly, Edgent focuses on collaborative and on-demand DNN co-inference, leveraging device-edge synergy through adaptive DNN partitioning and early-exit techniques to reduce computation latency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07840)\]. Additionally, Xenos has been developed to automatically optimize computation graphs through dataflow-centric techniques, achieving significant improvements in inference time by restructuring inter-operator dataflow and enabling parallelism across multiple DSP units \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00282)\]. These frameworks highlight the growing interest in optimizing model inference on edge devices, addressing the limitations of traditional cloud-based approaches and enabling real-time, privacy-preserving AI applications \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00531)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07840)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00282)\].

[Snippet]: «Quantized neural networks are well known for reducing the latency, power consumption, and model size without significant harm to the performance. This makes them highly appropriate for systems with limited resources and low power capacity. Mixed-precision quantization offers better utilization of customized hardware that supports arithmetic operations at different bitwidths. Quantization methods either aim to minimize the compression loss given a desired reduction or optimize a dependent variable for a specified property of the model (such as FLOPs or model size); both make the performance inefficient when deployed on specific hardware, but more importantly, quantization methods assume that the loss manifold holds a global minimum for a quantized model that copes with the global minimum of the full precision counterpart. Challenging this assumption, we argue that the optimal minimum changes as the precision changes, and thus, it is better to look at quantization as a random process, placing the foundation for a different approach to quantize neural networks, which, during the training procedure, quantizes the model to a different precision, looks at the bit allocation as a Markov Decision Process, and then, finds an optimal bitwidth allocation for measuring specified behaviors on a specific device via direct signals from the particular hardware architecture. By doing so, we avoid the basic assumption that the loss behaves the same way for a quantized model. Automatic Mixed-Precision Quantization for Edge Devices (dubbed AMED) demonstrates its superiority over current state-of-the-art schemes in terms of the trade-off between neural network accuracy and hardware efficiency, backed by a comprehensive evaluation.»
