## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has emerged as a critical research direction in computer vision, enabling models to detect objects of novel categories without requiring extensive labeled training data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. Traditional object detection methods are limited to a fixed vocabulary of known classes, which restricts their applicability to new or unseen objects \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. Recent advances have leveraged vision-language models (VLMs) to bridge this gap, allowing models to detect objects based on natural language descriptions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. However, these methods often face challenges in accurately aligning visual features with linguistic descriptions, particularly when dealing with fine-grained or ambiguous object attributes \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to address these limitations. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16833)\] introduces a framework that generates pseudo bounding-box labels from large-scale image-caption pairs, enabling the training of open-vocabulary detectors without requiring expensive annotations. This method significantly improves performance on benchmarks like COCO and LVIS, achieving state-of-the-art results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13040)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18207)\] proposes a method that utilizes pseudo caption labels to enhance knowledge distillation from VLMs, leading to better generalization to novel object categories \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11134)\]. These methods highlight the importance of leveraging weakly supervised signals to expand the vocabulary of object detectors \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving the alignment between image regions and textual descriptions. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.12130)\] introduces a method that aligns region embeddings with fine-grained textual descriptors, enabling more precise object detection. This approach is particularly effective in scenarios where object attributes play a crucial role in detection accuracy \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12437)\]. Furthermore, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13682)\] proposes a framework that generates region-text pairs from image-caption data, which can be used to train open-vocabulary detectors with improved performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09456)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of using generated data to overcome the scarcity of annotated training examples \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09073)\].

Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling models to detect objects of novel categories without requiring extensive retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. Traditional methods are constrained by closed-set assumptions, limiting their ability to generalize to unseen classes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. Recent approaches leverage vision-language models to expand vocabulary size, but they often struggle with proposal-level alignment and confidence bias toward base categories \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various frameworks, such as VLDet, which directly learns from image-text pairs by formulating object-language alignment as a set matching problem \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. Similarly, MEDet introduces proposal mining and prediction equalization to improve performance on novel categories \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. Other works, like CondHead, conditionally parameterize network heads on semantic embeddings to enhance detection accuracy for unseen classes \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13040)\]. Additionally, methods such as 3F-OVD extend fine-grained object detection to open-vocabulary settings, emphasizing the need for precise caption understanding and detailed image analysis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. The scarcity of fine-grained datasets has led to the creation of specialized benchmarks, such as NEU-171K, to support evaluation in open-vocabulary scenarios \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. Furthermore, approaches like Object2Scene and CastDet address 3D and aerial object detection, respectively, by leveraging large-scale datasets and pseudo-labeling strategies to enrich vocabulary and improve generalization \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11134)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\]. ### <a id='category-zero-shot-and-open-set-detection-capabilities'></a>Zero-shot and open-set detection capabilities: 4 papers found.
The category "Zero-shot and open-set detection capabilities" encompasses research efforts aimed at enhancing the flexibility and robustness of object detection models by enabling them to recognize objects based on natural language descriptions without prior training on specific categories. This includes the development of open-vocabulary attribute detection benchmarks, such as the OVAD benchmark, which allows for the evaluation of models on 117 attribute classes across 80 object classes in the MS COCO dataset \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. Additionally, studies have explored open-vocabulary monocular 3D detection, addressing the limitations of existing methods that rely on costly sensors or closed vocabularies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. These approaches often integrate pretrained vision-language models (VLMs) to leverage their ability to understand and generate text-based descriptions, thereby improving detection accuracy in novel or unseen categories \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. Furthermore, research has extended these capabilities to video understanding, where models are adapted to perform open-vocabulary multilabel video classification by integrating large language models (LLMs) for semantic guidance \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. The importance of such capabilities is further highlighted by studies evaluating open-vocabulary models in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, such as detecting unusual objects in street scenes, where models are tested on benchmarks like OoDIS, demonstrating both their potential and current limitations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16833)\]. These works collectively emphasize the growing interest in developing more adaptable and context-aware detection systems that can operate effectively in real-world, open-world environments.
