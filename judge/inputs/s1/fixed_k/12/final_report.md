## Related Works

Event cameras, which capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis, offer significant advantages in temporal resolution over traditional frame-based cameras \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13627)\]. However, these benefits come with challenges such as high bandwidth, memory, and computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. Prior work has explored methods to reduce these burdens, primarily through event downsampling. Most existing approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit adaptability to varying scene dynamics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. For instance, some methods use static thresholds to discard less informative events, but this can lead to loss of critical visual information \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00847)\]. Others attempt to adaptively adjust sampling rates based on scene activity, but often lack principled probabilistic modeling \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01040)\].

To address these limitations, recent research has introduced more sophisticated methods. For example, the EventFly framework introduces a probabilistic approach to cross-platform adaptation, using event activation prior (EAP) and eventBlend to align features across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. While not focused on downsampling, this work highlights the importance of domain-aware adaptation in event-based perception. Similarly, the CED dataset provides a rich source of color event data, enabling more accurate modeling of dynamic scenes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10772)\]. The development of color event cameras, such as the Color-DAVIS346, has also expanded the scope of event-based vision research \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10772)\].

Another line of work focuses on improving the usability of event data for downstream tasks. The Monte Carlo Language Model Pipeline for zero-shot sociopolitical event extraction demonstrates how generative models can be fine-tuned for specific tasks, even in the absence of labeled training data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15051)\]. This approach, while not directly related to downsampling, underscores the importance of task-specific adaptation and model robustness in event-based systems. Additionally, the work on probabilistic event calculus for event recognition introduces a formal framework for handling uncertainty in event-based systems \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1207.3270)\], which is relevant to the design of robust sampling strategies.

In the context of event downsampling, the paper "Learning to Deblur and Generate High Frame Rate Video with an Event Camera" proposes a residual learning approach to deblurring and video generation, leveraging event data to restore sharp images and videos \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00847)\]. This work, while focused on restoration, demonstrates the potential of event data to enhance temporal fidelity in visual processing. Similarly, the "Event Masked Autoencoder" introduces a framework for preserving spatiotemporal structure in event data, which is crucial for tasks like action recognition \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01040)\].

### <a id='category-event-based-vision-applications'></a>Event-Based Vision Applications: 8 papers found.
Event-based vision applications leverage the unique properties of event cameras, which capture changes in intensity asynchronously, offering high temporal resolution and low latency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10395)\]. These cameras are particularly advantageous for dynamic scenes, as they avoid motion blur and provide detailed motion cues \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00847)\]. However, the sparse and asynchronous nature of event data poses challenges for traditional computer vision methods, necessitating specialized processing techniques \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. Recent work has focused on improving the usability of event data by developing novel encoding strategies, such as scalar-wise modulation for embedding optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\], and event-to-intensity conversion methods that quantify inter-event intervals for precise intensity reconstruction \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15051)\]. Additionally, researchers have explored the integration of event data with conventional models, such as using GR-YOLO for face and eye tracking \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21616)\], and employing attention mechanisms to enhance visual recognition tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02586)\]. The development of synthetic datasets and event simulators has also been crucial for training and evaluating event-based models, enabling robust performance across various conditions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10395)\]. Furthermore, approaches like zero-shot event downsampling have been introduced to maintain model performance when reducing event data without task-specific adaptation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in event-based vision and the diverse techniques being developed to harness its potential.

However, they introduce challenges in terms of bandwidth, memory, and computational demands due to the high volume of event data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02586)\]. Prior work has attempted to address these challenges through event downsampling, but most approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, limiting their adaptability to different scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02586)\]. This aligns with broader research on zero-shot event detection, where models are trained to follow diverse event definitions to improve performance on unseen event types \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. Event datasets such as BiasBench and the Color Event Camera Dataset (CED) have also been developed to support research in this area, providing diverse scenes and enabling evaluation of event-based vision techniques \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21616)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10772)\]. [Snippet]: «The accuracy of an integral approximation via Monte Carlo sampling depends on the distribution of the integrand and the existence of its moments. In importance sampling, the choice of the proposal distribution markedly affects the existence of these moments and thus the accuracy of the obtained integral approximation. In this work, we present a method for improving the proposal distribution that applies to complicated distributions which are not available in closed form. The method iteratively matches the moments of a sample from the proposal distribution to their importance weighted moments, and is applicable to both standard importance sampling and self-normalized importance sampling. We apply the method to Bayesian leave-one-out cross-validation and show that it can significantly improve the accuracy of model assessment compared to regular Monte Carlo sampling or importance sampling when there are influential observations. We also propose a diagnostic method that can estimate the convergence rate of any Monte Carlo estimator from a finite random sample.»
