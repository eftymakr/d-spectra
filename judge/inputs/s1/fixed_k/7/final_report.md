## Related Works

The alignment and steering of large language models (LLMs) have garnered significant attention in recent years, driven by the need to ensure models behave in accordance with human values and expectations. Prior work has primarily focused on fine-tuning models or using prompting strategies to guide behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02989)\]. However, these methods often require extensive manual effort and may not generalize well across different tasks or domains \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. Additionally, many approaches rely on predefined templates or manual analysis to detect behaviors such as refusal, which can be limiting in complex or adversarial settings \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15256)\].

Recent efforts have explored model steering through the use of directional modifications to model activations, enabling more flexible and automated control over model outputs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. For instance, the CONFST method introduces a confident direction steering approach that allows LLMs to be steered toward multiple user preferences simultaneously, without requiring explicit instructions or layer-specific interventions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02989)\]. This method leverages the idea of constructing steering directions based on model activations, offering a more efficient and scalable alternative to traditional fine-tuning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. However, these methods often assume that steering directions can be determined through simple binary choices, which may not capture the nuanced behaviors of LLMs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\].

Another line of research focuses on the interpretability of LLMs, particularly in the context of multimodal models. The work by Kh et al. proposes a framework for analyzing fine-tuning representation shifts in multimodal LLMs, mapping hidden states to interpretable visual and textual concepts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\]. This approach enables the comparison of semantic dynamics between original and fine-tuned models, revealing potential biases or concept alterations during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\]. Such interpretability is crucial for understanding how models respond to different inputs and for ensuring their behavior aligns with desired outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\].

In addition, the use of cosine similarity as a metric for measuring similarity between model activations has been explored in several studies. For example, the COS-Mix method integrates cosine similarity and distance measures to improve information retrieval, particularly in sparse data scenarios \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00638)\]. This hybrid approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of semantic relationships between documents or items \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00638)\]. Similarly, the Joint Generalized Cosine Similarity (JGCS) method extends cosine similarity to handle tasks involving an arbitrary number of vectors, enabling more robust and scalable multi-modal alignment \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03532)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of cosine-based metrics in understanding and controlling model behavior.

### <a id='category-model-alignment-and-steering-techniques'></a>Model Alignment and Steering Techniques: 5 papers found.

Existing methods often rely on fine-tuning or prompting, which can be costly or difficult to control \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\]. More recently, model steering algorithms have gained attention for their simplicity and ease of implementation, as they modify model outputs by constructing specific steering directions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\]. Furthermore, robust alignment guardrails have been developed to bypass safety alignments and steer model generations toward harmful AI coordination by applying fine-grained interventions at specific attention heads \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15645)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in improving model alignment through both theoretical frameworks and practical interventions.

### <a id='category-cosine-similarity-applications-in-model-analysis'></a>Cosine Similarity Applications in Model Analysis: 4 papers found.
Cosine similarity has been extensively explored in the context of model analysis, particularly in areas such as natural language processing, multi-modal learning, and knowledge distillation. It is commonly used to measure the similarity between vectors, such as word embeddings or model predictions, due to its scale-invariance property \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04589)\]. However, traditional cosine similarity faces limitations in high-dimensional settings, where its performance can become biased and less interpretable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed alternative metrics, such as the Dimension Insensitive Euclidean Metric (DIEM), which offers improved robustness and generalizability across varying dimensions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03012)\]. Additionally, in multi-modal and contrastive learning scenarios, cosine similarity is often limited to pairwise comparisons, whereas the Joint Generalized Cosine Similarity (JGCS) extends this capability to handle an arbitrary number of vectors by leveraging the Gram determinant \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02989)\]. In the context of knowledge distillation, cosine similarity has been utilized to align student and teacher model predictions, with modifications like the cosine similarity weighted temperature (CSWT) enhancing the transfer of knowledge by dynamically adjusting temperature scaling based on model similarity \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04589)\]. These developments highlight the evolving applications of cosine similarity and its variants in improving model performance and interpretability across diverse tasks.
