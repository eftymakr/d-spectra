## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling detectors to identify objects from novel categories described by arbitrary text inputs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. Early approaches relied on zero-shot learning, where models were trained on a fixed set of categories and then extended to new ones without additional annotations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. However, these methods often suffered from poor generalization due to the lack of fine-grained alignment between visual features and textual descriptions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. More recent work has focused on leveraging pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP and ALIGN to bridge the visual and linguistic modalities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00227)\]. These models are trained on large-scale image-text pairs, enabling zero-shot recognition of novel objects \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\].

Several methods have explored the use of language descriptions to guide object detection. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\] proposed a multi-modal classifier that combines language-based and vision-based classifiers, demonstrating that such fusion improves performance over either modality alone. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\] introduced a framework that directly learns from image-text pairs, formulating object-language alignment as a set matching problem between image region features and word embeddings. This approach achieved superior performance on novel categories, with 32.0% mAP on COCO and 21.7% mask mAP on LVIS \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\]. Another work, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\], focused on open-vocabulary attribute detection, introducing a benchmark that evaluates the ability of vision-language models to detect object-level attributes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\].

### <a id='category-open-vocabulary-object-detection-frameworks'></a>Open-vocabulary object detection frameworks: 13 papers found.
Open-vocabulary object detection (OVOD) has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling detectors to identify objects from arbitrary categories specified by users at inference time without requiring retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. Existing approaches typically rely on pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) such as CLIP or ALIGN to bridge the gap between visual and textual modalities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. These models are often fine-tuned or adapted to detect novel objects by leveraging image-text pairs or pseudo-labels generated from large-scale datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. For instance, methods like ViLD and Pseudo-Labeling for Arbitrary Concepts (PLAC) demonstrate how vision-language knowledge distillation can be used to transfer classification capabilities to detection tasks, improving performance on unseen categories \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00227)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the distribution mismatch between global image embeddings and region-level features, as well as the difficulty of localizing objects of unseen classes \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. To address these issues, frameworks like CORA and PromptDet have been proposed, which incorporate region prompting and self-training strategies to enhance generalization and reduce reliance on manual annotations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. Additionally, some works focus on improving localization accuracy by aligning visual and textual embeddings more effectively, such as through de-biased triplet contrastive learning or regional prompt learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16833)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16513)\]. These advancements collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to make open-vocabulary detection more robust, scalable, and practical for real-world applications.

### <a id='category-benchmarking-open-vocabulary-detection'></a>Benchmarking open-vocabulary detection: 2 papers found.
The category "Benchmarking open-vocabulary detection" encompasses research aimed at improving the evaluation and performance of models that can detect objects in novel or open-vocabulary settings. Existing work highlights the limitations of current benchmarks, which often focus on object classes rather than attributes, leading to a lack of reliable evaluation for attribute-based tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. To address this, the OVAD benchmark was introduced, providing a clean, densely annotated dataset with 1.4 million annotations across 117 attribute classes, enabling open-vocabulary attribute detection \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. Meanwhile, other studies, such as 3F-OVD, extend the scope to fine-grained object detection, emphasizing the need for detailed captions and precise annotations to ensure accurate detection \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. These benchmarks also highlight the challenges of evaluating open-vocabulary models, where variations in training data can lead to unreliable results \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. Additionally, the development of new datasets like NEU-171K underscores the ongoing efforts to create more robust and versatile benchmarks for both supervised and open-vocabulary settings \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. Overall, these works emphasize the importance of benchmarking in advancing open-vocabulary detection, while also pointing to the need for more comprehensive and reliable evaluation methods.

[Snippet]: «Open-vocabulary object detection (OVOD) aims to detect the objects beyond the set of classes observed during training. This work introduces a straightforward and efficient strategy that utilizes pre-trained vision-language models (VLM), like CLIP, to identify potential novel classes through zero-shot classification. Previous methods use a class-agnostic region proposal network to detect object proposals and consider the proposals that do not match the ground truth as background. Unlike these methods, our method will select a subset of proposals that will be considered as background during the training. Then, we treat them as novel classes during training. We refer to this approach as the self-training strategy, which enhances recall and accuracy for novel classes without requiring extra annotations, datasets, and re-training. Compared to previous pseudo methods, our approach does not require re-training and offline labeling processing, which is more efficient and effective in one-shot training. Empirical evaluations on three datasets, including LVIS, V3Det, and COCO, demonstrate significant improvements over the baseline performance without incurring additional parameters or computational costs during inference. In addition, we also apply our method to various baselines. In particular, compared with the previous method, F-VLM, our method achieves a 1.7% improvement on the LVIS dataset. Combined with the recent method CLIPSelf, our method also achieves 46.7 novel class AP on COCO without introducing extra data for pertaining.
