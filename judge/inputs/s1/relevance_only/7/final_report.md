## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and express complex behaviors within their activation spaces. Prior work has explored how LLMs represent and generate outputs based on internal activations, with a particular focus on behaviors such as refusal, alignment, and steering. Several studies have investigated how these behaviors can be identified or modified through various techniques, including fine-tuning, prompting, and activation modification.

Refusal behavior in LLMs, particularly in conversational models, has been shown to be mediated by a single direction in the model's activation space \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. This finding suggests that refusal is not a complex, multi-faceted behavior but rather a result of a specific directional bias in the model's representations. This insight has led to the development of methods to surgically disable refusal behavior with minimal impact on other model capabilities \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. However, these methods often require detailed knowledge of the model's internal structure and may not generalize well across different models or tasks.

Model steering, which involves modifying model outputs by adjusting activation directions, has also been explored in several works. One approach, called CONFST, leverages confident directions to steer LLMs toward user preferences without requiring explicit instructions or layer-specific adjustments \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02989)\]. This method is more powerful than bidirectional steering techniques, as it can align multiple user preferences simultaneously. However, it still relies on the assumption that steering directions can be reliably identified and applied.

Another line of work focuses on improving the truthfulness of LLMs by modifying their activations during inference. The Adaptive Activation Steering (ACT) method, for instance, introduces steering vectors that shift LLMs' activations toward a "truthful" direction, improving the accuracy of generated content across various models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00034)\]. This approach is tuning-free and can be applied to a wide range of models, demonstrating its versatility and effectiveness.

The use of cosine similarity for analyzing and steering LLMs has also been explored. While cosine similarity is widely used for comparing representations in various domains, recent studies have highlighted its limitations in high-dimensional spaces, particularly when dimensions increase \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08623)\]. This has led to the development of alternative metrics, such as the Dimension Insensitive Euclidean Metric (DIEM), which offers better robustness and generalizability across dimensions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08623)\]. These findings suggest that while cosine similarity remains a useful tool, it may not always be the best choice for certain tasks.

The majority of current alignment methods rely on fine-tuning or prompting, which can be either costly or difficult to control. Model steering algorithms, which modify the model output by constructing specific steering directions, are typically easy to implement and optimization-free. However, their capabilities are typically limited to steering the model into one of the two directions (i.e., bidirectional steering), and there has been no theoretical understanding to guarantee their performance. In this work, we propose a theoretical framework to understand and quantify the model steering methods. Inspired by the framework, we propose a confident direction steering method (CONFST) that steers LLMs via modifying their activations at inference time. More specifically, CONFST builds a confident direction that is closely aligned with users' preferences, and this direction is then added to the activations of the LLMs to effectively steer the model output. Our approach offers three key advantages over popular bidirectional model steering methods: 1) It is more powerful, since multiple (i.e. more than two) users' preferences can be aligned simultaneously; 2) It is simple to implement, since there is no need to determine which layer to add the steering vector to; 3) No explicit user instruction is required. We validate our method on GPT-2 XL (1.5B), Mistral (7B) and Gemma-it (9B) models for tasks that require shifting the output of LLMs across various topics and styles, achieving superior performance over competing methods.»

In this work, we show that refusal is mediated by a one-dimensional subspace, across 13 popular open-source chat models up to 72B parameters in size. ### <a id='category-cosine-similarity-applications'></a>Cosine Similarity Applications: 2 papers found.
Cosine similarity is widely utilized in various domains such as information retrieval, natural language processing, computer vision, and multimodal learning due to its ability to compare learned representations effectively \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16318)\]. It is favored for its computational efficiency and its alignment with objectives that train normalized embeddings, which removes positive radial scale and focuses on angular relationships \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16318)\]. However, its limitations are also well-documented, including the discarding of radial variation and the potential mismatch between the symmetric, uncalibrated score and the intended relation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16318)\]. In the context of document similarity, cosine similarity remains a standard method, though more complex semantic analysis techniques exist, which are computationally expensive and less suitable for real-time applications \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.03934)\]. To address these constraints, new measures like Textual Spatial Cosine Similarity have been developed, leveraging word placement information to detect semantic similarity while maintaining the efficiency of cosine-based approaches \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.03934)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of cosine similarity in handling both traditional and advanced similarity tasks.

### <a id='category-dimensionality-and-metric-limitations'></a>Dimensionality and Metric Limitations: 6 papers found.
The category "Dimensionality and Metric Limitations" explores challenges in measuring and interpreting high-dimensional data, particularly in the context of machine learning and language models. Several studies highlight the limitations of traditional metrics such as cosine similarity, which can become biased or poorly interpretable as dimensionality increases \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08623)\]. For instance, in the analysis of high-dimensional data from physiological signals or natural language processing, cosine similarity's dependency on vector dimensions leads to unreliable comparisons \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08623)\]. To address this, researchers have introduced the Dimension Insensitive Euclidean Metric (DIEM), which offers improved robustness and generalizability across varying dimensions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08623)\]. Similarly, in the context of contextualized language models like BERT and GPT-2, standard similarity measures such as cosine similarity and Euclidean distance are found to be dominated by a small number of "rogue dimensions," which do not align with the model's functional behavior \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04404)\]. This highlights the need for postprocessing techniques to correct for these biases and improve the informativeness of similarity-based analyses \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04404)\]. Additionally, the limitations of discrete tokenizers in handling high-dimensional data and their impact on model performance are also discussed, emphasizing the importance of systematic reviews and design improvements in tokenizer development \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12448)\]. These findings collectively underscore the critical need for more robust and dimensionally invariant metrics to enhance the interpretability and reliability of high-dimensional data analysis in AI systems.
