## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have become a cornerstone of modern information retrieval systems, particularly in Web search, due to their ability to learn complex representations from raw data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11903)\]. However, these models typically require substantial amounts of labeled query-document pairs for training, which are both costly and labor-intensive to obtain \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. To address this challenge, researchers have explored synthetic query generation as a scalable alternative, leveraging large language models (LLMs) to generate queries from Web document collections like ClueWeb22 \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. While this approach offers promise, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal downstream retrieval performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\].

Existing methods typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. However, this approach introduces additional complexity and may not fully address the root cause of suboptimal performance. In contrast, our work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach aims to optimize the model towards generating high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

DPO has been widely adopted for preference alignment of LLMs due to its simplicity and effectiveness \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\]. It directly optimizes LLMs using human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This method has been shown to be stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or significant hyperparameter tuning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been extended to address various challenges, such as token-level importance sampling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\], the inclusion of rationales in preference data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14477)\], and the optimization of dense retrieval models with hard negatives \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\].

In addition to DPO, other approaches have explored the use of synthetic queries for retrieval tasks. For example, some studies have investigated the use of synthetically generated queries for evaluating retrievability bias in PLM-based rankers \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]. These studies have shown that synthetically generated queries can lead to lower bias compared to user-generated queries, suggesting that they may be more suitable for training retrieval models \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]. Similarly, other works have explored the use of weak supervision for training neural ranking models, using pseudo query-document pairs that already exhibit relevance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\]. These approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in improving retrieval performance without requiring extensive manual labeling \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\].

In summary, while synthetic query generation offers a scalable alternative to labeled data in neural retrieval, it remains a challenging task due to the variability in query quality

### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 4 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\]. Unlike RLHF, which requires complex reward modeling and reinforcement learning stages, DPO directly optimizes the model using human preferences, simplifying the training process and improving computational efficiency \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach eliminates the need for explicit reward modeling and reduces the instability often associated with RLHF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\]. DPO has been applied in various domains, including generative information retrieval (GenIR), where it has been used to improve document relevance estimation by directly optimizing pairwise ranking without the need for reinforcement learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. Additionally, DPO has been shown to outperform existing methods in terms of stability, performance, and simplicity, particularly in controlling the sentiment of generated text and improving response quality in tasks such as summarization and dialogue \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Theoretical work has also explored the assumptions underlying DPO, highlighting its reliance on pairwise preference approximations and proposing new objectives that bypass these limitations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\]. Overall, DPO represents a significant advancement in aligning LLMs with human preferences, offering a streamlined and effective approach to model fine-tuning.

### <a id='category-dpo-applications-and-extensions'></a>DPO Applications and Extensions: 5 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a powerful technique for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences, particularly in scenarios where high-quality, contextually relevant responses are critical \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. Unlike traditional methods that rely on reinforcement learning from human feedback, DPO simplifies the alignment process by using preference data to directly optimize model outputs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to various tasks, including hate speech mitigation, where it enables the generation of constructive counter-speech by aligning model outputs with human preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. Additionally, DPO has shown effectiveness in improving reasoning, summarization, and alignment tasks by modeling the relative probability of preferred responses over dispreferred ones \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the potential for DPO to reduce the likelihood of preferred examples if the relative probability between preferred and dispreferred responses is not carefully managed \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed extensions like DPO-Positive (DPOP), which introduces a new loss function and training procedure to avoid such failures and improve performance across diverse datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been adapted for generative retrieval tasks, where it helps align models with human preferences by integrating ranking signals into the query generation process, leading to improved downstream retrieval performance \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19829)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility and effectiveness of DPO in enhancing model alignment and performance across a wide range of applications.

The category "Weak Supervision in Neural Ranking Models" explores the use of weakly labeled data to train neural ranking models without relying on expensive human-labeled relevance judgments. This approach leverages automatically generated signals, such as those from unsupervised ranking models like BM25, to provide pseudo-relevance labels for training \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\]. These signals are used to train feed-forward neural networks that learn to rank query-document pairs effectively \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\]. The effectiveness of such models is evaluated using various learning scenarios, including point-wise and pair-wise ranking, and different input representations, such as dense/sparse vectors and word embeddings \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\]. Experiments on standard collections like Robust and ClueWeb show significant improvements, with over 13% and 35% gains in MAP scores compared to BM25 \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\]. Additionally, weak supervision sources, such as newswire headline-content pairs and encyclopedic heading-paragraph pairs, are explored as effective training data, and filtering techniques are proposed to enhance performance by removing out-of-domain samples \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\]. These findings highlight the potential of weak supervision in improving neural ranking models, especially when combined with large-scale training data and appropriate objective functions \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\].
