## Related Works

The problem of learning from noisy annotations has been extensively studied in recent years, with a focus on improving the robustness of deep learning models. Early approaches primarily relied on identifying and removing noisy samples through statistical methods, such as importance re-weighting and bootstrapping \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02391)\]. However, these methods often fail to account for the complex nature of label noise, particularly when the noise is class-dependent or when the dataset contains a high proportion of incorrect labels \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03683)\].

More recently, researchers have explored semi-supervised learning techniques to mitigate the impact of noisy labels. For instance, DivideMix \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\] introduces a framework that dynamically divides the training data into clean and noisy subsets using a mixture model, allowing the model to be trained in a semi-supervised manner. This approach helps to reduce the influence of noisy labels by leveraging both labeled and unlabeled data. Similarly, the work by \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11364)\] proposes a joint optimization framework that simultaneously learns the model parameters and estimates the true labels, correcting labels during training through alternating updates. This method has shown promising results on noisy datasets such as CIFAR-10 and Clothing1M.

Another line of research focuses on using synthetic samples to improve the robustness of models trained on noisy data. The paper \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16966)\] introduces a framework that generates synthetic samples with soft labels to mitigate the impact of noisy labels. By creating synthetic samples through a mixing strategy that aggregates original samples with their top-K nearest neighbors, the model is trained on a more diverse set of data, reducing overfitting to noisy labels. This approach has been shown to produce more accurate approximations of ground truth labels and improve the quality of learned representations.

In addition to these methods, some studies have investigated the use of trusted data to train models on datasets with severe label noise. For example, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05300)\] demonstrates that a small set of clean labels can be leveraged to correct the loss function during training, improving the robustness of deep neural networks to label noise. This approach is particularly effective in scenarios where label noise is arbitrarily bad, such as in adversarial settings.

Furthermore, the work by \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01189)\] introduces a denoising after entropy-based debiasing approach, which first debiases the dataset by underestimating easy-to-learn samples and highlighting difficult-to-learn ones. This method is then followed by denoising algorithms that reduce the impact of noisy labels while preserving the informative samples. This two-step process has been shown to improve the generalization performance of models trained on noisy datasets.

The category "Debiasing and Denoising Frameworks" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the robustness of machine learning models in the presence of noisy or biased data. These frameworks address the challenges posed by inaccurate annotations, such as those arising from crowdsourcing or web-based data collection, by developing methods to detect, correct, or mitigate the impact of such noise \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10456)\]. For instance, some studies focus on decoupling entangled noises in object detection tasks, where both bounding box and class label inaccuracies are present, and propose techniques to correct these annotations before training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10456)\]. Others explore the early learning phase of deep neural networks, where models initially fit clean samples before memorizing noisy ones, and leverage this behavior to design regularization strategies that prevent overfitting to false labels \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00151)\]. Additionally, there is significant research on source bias in retrieval models, where models may favor content generated by large language models over human-written text, and efforts to correct this bias using causal inference and perplexity-based analysis have been proposed \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08684)\]. The problem of noisy labels is also tackled through synthetic data generation, where models are trained on artificially created samples to reduce the impact of label noise \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16966)\]. Furthermore, there are approaches that focus on the structural properties of noisy annotations, such as human reporting bias in image tagging, and propose algorithms to separate this bias from the true visual content \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05300)\]. Overall, the field emphasizes the development of robust and generalizable methods that can handle various forms of noise and bias, often leveraging theoretical insights and semi-supervised learning techniques to improve model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10456)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00151)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.08684)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16966)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05300)\].

Two prominent directions include learning with noisy labels and semi-supervised learning by exploiting unlabeled data. In this work, we propose DivideMix, a novel framework for learning with noisy labels by leveraging semi-supervised learning techniques. In particular, DivideMix models the per-sample loss distribution with a mixture model to dynamically divide the training data into a labeled set with clean samples and an unlabeled set with noisy samples, and trains the model on both the labeled and unlabeled data in a semi-supervised manner. During the semi-supervised training phase, we improve the MixMatch strategy by performing label co-refinement and label co-guessing on labeled and unlabeled samples, respectively. Sources of label noise include automatic labeling for large datasets, non-expert labeling, and label corruption by data poisoning adversaries. In the latter case, corruptions may be arbitrarily bad, even so bad that a classifier predicts the wrong labels with high confidence. To protect against such sources of noise, we leverage the fact that a small set of clean labels is often easy to procure. We demonstrate that robustness to label noise up to severe strengths can be achieved by using a set of trusted data with clean labels, and propose a loss correction that utilizes trusted examples in a data-efficient manner to mitigate the effects of label noise on deep neural network classifiers.
