## Related Works

Long-tailed recognition remains a challenging problem in computer vision due to the significant imbalance in class distribution, where a few dominant (head) classes occupy most of the data while the majority of minority (tail) classes are severely underrepresented \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. This imbalance leads to biased feature spaces, poor generalization, and suboptimal performance on rare classes \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Various approaches have been proposed to address this issue, primarily focusing on class-balancing strategies, data augmentation, and contrastive learning \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04596)\].

Contrastive learning has shown promising results in representation learning, but its performance on long-tailed data is often suboptimal. Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) has been widely used to optimize feature distributions by pulling positive samples together and pushing negative samples apart \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11938)\]. However, SCL tends to bias towards high-frequency classes, exacerbating the imbalance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. To mitigate this, several works have introduced class-balancing mechanisms. For instance, Rebalanced Contrastive Learning (RCL) introduces a class frequency-based SoftMax loss balancing to address class imbalance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. Similarly, Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) introduces class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. These methods aim to improve the representation of tail classes by adjusting the loss function or introducing additional constraints.

Another line of research focuses on decoupling representation learning from classifier training. Decoupled Contrastive Learning (DCL) separates the learning process into representation and classifier learning, allowing for more flexible and effective optimization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. This approach has shown that it is possible to achieve strong long-tailed recognition ability by adjusting only the classifier, even when representations are learned with simple sampling strategies \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Similarly, the Equalization Loss \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05566)\] introduces a gradient-driven training mechanism to balance the positive and negative gradients, improving the performance of tail classes.

In addition, some works have explored the use of self-distillation and knowledge transfer to improve the performance of tail classes. For example, Decoupled Contrastive Learning for Long-Tailed Recognition introduces a patch-based self-distillation to transfer knowledge from head to tail classes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. Similarly, the Balanced Contrastive Learning (BCL) framework introduces class-averaging and class-complement to balance the gradient contributions of negative classes and ensure all classes appear in every mini-batch \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. These methods aim to improve the generalization ability of models by addressing the imbalanced data distribution during training.
