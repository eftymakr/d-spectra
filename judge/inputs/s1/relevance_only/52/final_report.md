## Related Works

Dense retrieval systems typically rely on pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT and LLaMA, which are fine-tuned to encode text into dense representations that can be compared via simple similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Recent research has questioned the relative contributions of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may not significantly alter this knowledge \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This idea was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with DPR as a representative dense retriever \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. However, the scope of this analysis was limited, and the current study extends the investigation by considering alternative representation approaches, such as CLS token versus mean pooling, different backbone architectures (encoder-only BERT vs. decoder-only LLaMA), and additional datasets (MSMARCO in addition to Natural Questions) \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\].

The findings confirm that in DPR tuning, pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance, with fine-tuning primarily adjusting neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\]. This pattern, however, does not hold universally. For example, in mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based models like LLaMA, fine-tuning appears to have a more significant impact on retrieval performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. These results highlight the importance of model architecture and representation strategies in determining how effectively fine-tuning can enhance or modify retrieval capabilities \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of contrastive learning and pre-training strategies in dense retrieval. For instance, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00635)\] proposed a contrastive pre-training approach to learn a discriminative autoencoder with a lightweight MLP decoder, showing that this method can significantly outperform state-of-the-art autoencoder-based language models and other pre-trained models for dense retrieval. Similarly, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)\] introduced coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, demonstrating that it removes the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\].

In addition, \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c7f8acb0bdae40eeb0505bf87744fa29270073ac)\] investigated the use of contrastive learning for unsupervised dense retrieval, showing that it leads to strong performance in various retrieval settings and outperforms BM25 on 11 out of 15 datasets for the Recall@100 \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14876)\]. ### <a id='category-unsupervised-contrastive-learning-for-retrieval'></a>Unsupervised Contrastive Learning for Retrieval: 5 papers found.
Recent research has explored the effectiveness of fine-tuned language models for dense retrieval, highlighting the challenges associated with training dense retrievers, such as their fragility to training data noise and the need for large batches to robustly learn the embedding space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. To address these issues, the Condenser pre-training architecture has been proposed, which condenses information into dense vectors through LM pre-training, and coCondenser further enhances this by introducing an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Additionally, contrastive pre-training has been shown to construct pseudo-positive examples from unlabeled data, offering a potential solution to the demand for abundant training data in dense retrievers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, the relevance of these pseudo-positive examples can be questionable, leading to the proposal of relevance-aware contrastive learning, which estimates the relevance of positive pairs using an intermediate-trained model and adaptively weighs the contrastive loss accordingly \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Furthermore, unsupervised contrastive learning has been demonstrated to significantly improve retrieval performance, outperforming BM25 on multiple datasets and enabling strong cross-lingual transfer \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the importance of contrastive learning in improving dense retrievers, reducing the need for extensive data engineering, and enhancing the robustness of embedding spaces \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\].

### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-in-dense-retrieval'></a>Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval: 10 papers found.
The category "Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval" explores the challenges and advancements in utilizing pre-trained language models (PLMs) for dense retrieval tasks. Recent studies highlight that standard PLMs, such as BERT, are not structurally ready for dense retrieval due to the gap between pre-training and fine-tuning, which affects their ability to aggregate textual information into a single dense representation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed novel architectures like Condenser, which pre-trains a Transformer model specifically for dense encoding by conditioning LM predictions on dense representations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, the Aggretriever model improves dense retrieval by aggregating contextualized token embeddings into a dense vector, enhancing effectiveness without significant training overhead \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\]. Additionally, the use of parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, such as LoRA, has been explored to reduce training costs while maintaining performance, with LoRA-DPR outperforming full parameter fine-tuning on several benchmarks \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2f184d2e7182dd69dfb708aa088ada7abc47597d)\]. Autoencoder-based models have also been examined, where theoretical and empirical analyses show that low reconstruction loss may not always yield high-quality embeddings, prompting the development of self-learning methods to improve representation quality \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. Furthermore, the adaptation of large language models (LLMs) for dense retrieval has been investigated, with approaches like Llama2Vec introducing pretext tasks to align LLMs with dense retrieval requirements \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15503)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the importance of pre-training and architectural innovations in enhancing the performance and efficiency of dense retrieval systems.

### <a id='category-fine-tuning-vs.-pre-training-contributions'></a>Fine-tuning vs. Pre-training Contributions: 6 papers found.
The category "Fine-tuning vs. Pre-training Contributions" explores the interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning in large language models (LLMs), emphasizing how each phase influences model performance and knowledge acquisition. Research indicates that pre-training plays a foundational role in embedding broad linguistic knowledge, as seen in BERT, RoBERTa, and ALBERT, where fine-tuning can alter representations and probing accuracy, though the effects vary across models and tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Studies also highlight that while fine-tuning may adjust neuron activations, it does not necessarily reorganize knowledge, suggesting that pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance in models like DPR \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, this pattern is not universal; for instance, models like Contriever (mean-pooled) and LLaMA (decoder-based) exhibit different behaviors, where fine-tuning can significantly impact performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. Additionally, the effectiveness of fine-tuning is influenced by factors such as the type of fine-tuning (unsupervised vs. retrieval-augmented generation), the model architecture, and the diversity of training data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. These findings underscore the need for careful interpretation of fine-tuning effects and highlight the importance of balancing pre-training and fine-tuning to optimize model capabilities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\].
