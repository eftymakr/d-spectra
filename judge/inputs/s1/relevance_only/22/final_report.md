## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has been extensively studied to address the challenge of transferring knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19899)\]. Traditional UDA methods typically assume a shared label space between the source and target domains, which limits their applicability in real-world scenarios where novel classes may appear in the target domain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19899)\]. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes exist, as these classes are not accounted for during the adaptation process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19899)\].

To overcome this limitation, several works have explored open-set domain adaptation (OSDA) scenarios, where the target domain may contain unknown classes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19899)\]. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10639)\] introduces a two-step semi-supervised dual-domain adaptation (SSDDA) approach that addresses both cross- and intra-domain gaps in semantic segmentation. The method leverages image-level mixing strategies and student-teacher networks to improve adaptation performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10639)\]. Similarly, \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5f9ce93f82dcd9b0f9ffe4274b81682f40d86253)\] proposes a source data-free UDA framework that constructs virtual source domain data to mimic the source distribution, enabling adaptation without access to the original source domain data \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5f9ce93f82dcd9b0f9ffe4274b81682f40d86253)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving the alignment of domain-specific features while preserving the semantic consistency across domains. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12545)\] proposes a content transfer approach that separates image information into content and style components, aiming to reduce the domain gap by focusing on content for semantic segmentation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12545)\]. This idea is further extended in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.15467)\], where adversarial learning is used to bridge the style gap between domains, and a category-adaptive threshold mechanism is introduced to improve pseudo-label generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.15467)\].

Additionally, methods such as \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] employ a dual soft-paste (DSP) strategy to enhance domain-invariant feature learning by soft-pasting image patches from both source and target domains. This approach improves the model's ability to generalize across domains \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. In contrast, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09600)\] introduces a multi-source domain adaptation framework that leverages collaborative learning across multiple source domains, reducing the domain gap through image translation and pseudo-label refinement \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09600)\].

Recent works have also focused on improving the robustness of UDA methods by incorporating uncertainty-aware mechanisms. For instance, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04717)\] proposes an uncertainty-aware consistency regularization method that enhances the reliability of pseudo-labels during adaptation \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04717)\]. This is further extended in \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a0ee98646f8244028a1a85f23bc100ec5b3a3815)\], where a dual-level domain mixing approach is used to reduce domain gaps at both region and sample levels, leading to improved adaptation performance \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a0ee98646f8244028a1a85f23bc100ec5b3a3815)\].
