## Related Works

Personalized outfit generation has gained significant attention in recent years, driven by the increasing demand for tailored fashion recommendations. Early approaches focused on pre-defined outfit recommendation systems, which relied on static combinations of items \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03016)\]. However, these methods lacked personalization and flexibility, as they could not adapt to individual user preferences \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03016)\]. More recent work has shifted toward personalized outfit composition, where the goal is to generate outfits that are both compatible and aligned with user preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17279)\].

Several studies have explored the use of deep learning techniques for outfit generation. For instance, FashionNet \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02443)\] introduced a system that combines feature extraction and compatibility computation to recommend personalized outfits. The model uses a two-stage training strategy to transfer a general compatibility model to a personalized one, improving recommendation accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02443)\]. Similarly, OutfitGAN \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/61c15a1723d210ac75824bf185b547cee37c70af)\] proposed a generative adversarial network (GAN) that ensures compatibility among generated items, leading to more realistic and coherent outfits \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/61c15a1723d210ac75824bf185b547cee37c70af)\].

Another line of research has focused on improving the compatibility and personalization of generated outfits. POG \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\] introduced a model that connects user preferences with Transformer architecture, enabling the generation of personalized outfits. This model was evaluated on a large-scale dataset, demonstrating its effectiveness in both compatibility and personalization metrics \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. Similarly, OutfitTransformer \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4d431b88f7dfe1e75b0e23bb21fc9dd378b15cf2)\] leverages self-attention mechanisms to model outfit compatibility, supporting large-scale complementary item retrieval \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4d431b88f7dfe1e75b0e23bb21fc9dd378b15cf2)\].

The challenge of generating diverse and compatible outfits has also been addressed through graph-based models. The Outfit Graph Network \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c16dbfb1303c5dce657e29a84a12ee8a43caedcd)\] represents outfits as graphs, using attention mechanisms to capture visual relationships between items. This approach allows for more accurate compatibility prediction by modeling both item-item and user preferences \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c16dbfb1303c5dce657e29a84a12ee8a43caedcd)\]. Additionally, the Regional Attention Compatibility Scoring model \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c1edd7ae1b786fd77c5fb04bd287bcc158796d4c)\] incorporates user attention to specific regions of items, enhancing the accuracy of compatibility prediction \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c1edd7ae1b786fd77c5fb04bd287bcc158796d4c)\].

Recent advances in generative models have also focused on improving the quality and controllability of generated outfits. DiFashion \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17279)\] introduced a diffusion-based model that generates multiple fashion images in parallel, ensuring high fidelity and compatibility. The model uses classifier-free guidance to align generated images with conditions, improving the overall quality of the generated outfits \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17279)\]. Similarly, FCBoost-Net \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00992)\] proposed a framework that generates multiple collocated and diversified outfits, improving compatibility while maintaining diversity \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00992)\].
