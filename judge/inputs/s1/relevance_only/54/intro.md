## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommendation systems, leveraging user-item interactions to generate personalized recommendations \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/de2562febbdf3181834648d1fc679e2cb75aa973)\]. Recent advances in graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly enhanced CF methods by modeling high-order interactions through graph convolutional networks (GCNs) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. However, existing GCN-based CF models face challenges in capturing fine-grained user preferences and mitigating over-smoothing during the embedding process \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08827)\]. 

Several works have explored graph-based CF approaches, such as SpectralCF \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\], which directly learns from the spectral domain of user-item bipartite graphs to uncover deep connections between users and items \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. Similarly, LightGCN \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\] simplifies GCN by removing unnecessary nonlinear operations, leading to improved performance and easier training \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. Despite these advancements, most models still rely on linear aggregation and fail to effectively model complex nonlinear relationships in the graph \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. 

The limitations of traditional GCN-based methods have prompted the development of more sophisticated graph filters. For instance, PolyCF \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] reframes CF as a graph signal processing problem and employs polynomial graph filters to capture spectral features across multiple eigenspaces \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This approach allows for the recovery of missing interactions by approximating the optimal polynomial response function \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, BernNet \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04384)\] introduces a method for learning arbitrary graph spectral filters using Bernstein polynomial approximations, enabling the modeling of complex band-rejection and comb filters \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04384)\]. 

Another line of research focuses on improving the robustness and expressiveness of graph filters. For example, LCF \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/219fd0434ce709f39d37df5452c811e7e9c4c9f9)\] addresses the computational inefficiency of graph convolution by introducing a low-pass collaborative filter that reduces noise and complexity without sacrificing performance \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/219fd0434ce709f39d37df5452c811e7e9c4c9f9)\]. Additionally, MDGCF \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13996)\] enhances graph-based CF by mining neighborhood- and homogeneous-level dependencies, capturing richer interaction patterns between users and items \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13996)\]. 

The issue of over-smoothing in deep GCN models has also been addressed by several studies. For instance, LR-GCCF \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] introduces a linear residual graph convolutional network that mitigates over-smoothing by removing non-linear activations, thereby improving training efficiency and model performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, AutoGCN \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.15516)\] proposes an automatic graph convolutional network that adapts the bandwidth of graph filters to capture the full spectrum of graph signals, avoiding the limitations of fixed-bandwidth filters \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.15516)\].
