## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area in natural language processing, aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions. This task requires models to encode task-specific semantics while maintaining efficiency and scalability. Prior work has explored various approaches to achieve this goal, each with its own strengths and limitations.

Several studies have focused on instruction-tuned text embeddings, where models are trained to generate embeddings that are aligned with specific instructions. For example, INSTRUCTOR \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\] introduces a method that computes text embeddings given task instructions, embedding text together with instructions explaining the use case. This approach enables a single embedder to generate embeddings tailored to different downstream tasks without further training. However, INSTRUCTOR relies on a large number of annotated instructions, which may not always be available \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\].

In contrast, InBedder \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\] treats the instruction as a question about the input text and encodes the expected answers to obtain the representation. This approach improves instruction-following capabilities by leveraging abstractive question answering tasks during fine-tuning. InBedder demonstrates significant improvements in instruction awareness and robustness tests, particularly when applied to both large language models and smaller encoder-based models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\].

Another line of research focuses on multimodal instruction-following agents, which integrate visual and linguistic information to perform tasks in vision-based environments. For instance, the work in \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13431)\] proposes a multimodal transformer that encodes visual observations and language instructions, enabling robots to solve instruction-following tasks. This model is pre-trained on millions of image-text pairs and natural language text, producing generic cross-modal representations of observations and instructions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13431)\].

Instruction embedding has also been explored as a way to capture latent representations of instructions for task identification. The concept of instruction embedding, introduced in \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19680)\], aims to construct representations that focus on the task rather than the specific semantics. This approach is particularly useful for tasks like data selection and demonstrations retrieval, where the ability to identify task categories is crucial \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19680)\].

In addition to these methods, there are approaches that utilize pre-trained text representations to enhance instruction-following capabilities. For example, the work in \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6f6b1ff19c8ec043a278bdd76553a267aa4b101c)\] discusses the use of pre-trained language models for text mining tasks, highlighting their ability to transfer generic linguistic features to various applications. These models are fine-tuned on target corpora, reducing the need for extensive training from scratch \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6f6b1ff19c8ec043a278bdd76553a267aa4b101c)\].

Efficiency and scalability in instruction-following text embedding have been actively explored, with recent works addressing the computational overhead of re-encoding entire corpora for each instruction \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00200)\]. To improve performance, semi-supervised approaches such as Predictive Text Embedding (PTE) have been proposed, leveraging both labeled and unlabeled data to create embeddings that are more aligned with specific tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00200)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to balance efficiency and effectiveness in instruction-following text embedding, with methods such as GSTransform offering real-time adaptation of pre-computed embeddings to align with user instructions, significantly reducing computational costs \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00200)\].

[Snippet]: «Despite rapid advances in the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), they continue to struggle with following relatively simple and unambiguous instructions, particularly when compositional structure is involved. Recent work suggests that models may follow instructions more effectively when they are expressed in pseudo-code rather than natural language. However, writing pseudo-code programs can be tedious, and relying on few-shot demonstrations or inference-time code prompting is often unnatural for non-expert users of LLMs. To overcome these limitations, we propose a training time approach that fine-tunes LLMs using instruction-tuning data augmented with pseudo-code representations of natural language instructions paired with final responses. We evaluate our method on 12 publicly available benchmarks spanning instruction-following, mathematical reasoning, and commonsense reasoning, across six base models. Our results show that models trained with pseudo-code follow instructions more reliably, achieving relative gains of 8-21\% on instruction following benchmarks, while largely preserving and in some cases improving performance on mathematical and commonsense reasoning tasks, with an average gain of up to 30\% across all evaluated benchmarks.»
