## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts multiple tokens simultaneously, leveraging multiple prediction heads to capture richer contextual information \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly in large models, by enabling more efficient and parallelized token generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) often struggle with MTP due to limitations in their capacity to handle complex prediction tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several studies have explored the challenges and benefits of MTP. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\] demonstrates that training models to predict multiple future tokens at once results in higher sample efficiency and improved downstream performance, especially in coding benchmarks. The method, which uses independent output heads for each predicted token, achieves gains in both accuracy and inference speed without increasing training time \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This aligns with findings from \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17765)\], which introduces a model based on tensor decomposition to enable efficient multi-token prediction while maintaining accuracy. The model can be interpreted as a mixture of experts, allowing for robust and scalable training \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17765)\].

Curriculum learning has also been proposed as a strategy to address the challenges of MTP, particularly for smaller models. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\] explores two curriculum variants: a forward curriculum that gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, and a reverse curriculum that does the opposite. The forward curriculum enables SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training, improving downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. In contrast, the reverse curriculum achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The effectiveness of curriculum learning in improving model performance has been explored in other domains as well. \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\] introduces a curriculum learning approach for natural language understanding (NLU) tasks, where examples are arranged in an easy-to-difficult order to align with human learning procedures. This approach significantly improves performance across a wide range of NLU tasks without requiring manual model architecture design or external data \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\]. Similarly, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\] proposes a competence-based curriculum learning framework for neural machine translation (NMT), which reduces training time and improves performance by filtering training samples based on their difficulty and the model’s current competence \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\].
