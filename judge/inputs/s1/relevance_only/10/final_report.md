## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts multiple tokens simultaneously, leveraging multiple prediction heads to capture richer contextual information \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly in large models, by enabling more efficient and parallelized token generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) often struggle with MTP due to limitations in their capacity to handle complex prediction tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several studies have explored the challenges and benefits of MTP. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\] demonstrates that training models to predict multiple future tokens at once results in higher sample efficiency and improved downstream performance, especially in coding benchmarks. The method, which uses independent output heads for each predicted token, achieves gains in both accuracy and inference speed without increasing training time \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This aligns with findings from \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17765)\], which introduces a model based on tensor decomposition to enable efficient multi-token prediction while maintaining accuracy. The model can be interpreted as a mixture of experts, allowing for robust and scalable training \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17765)\].

Curriculum learning has also been proposed as a strategy to address the challenges of MTP, particularly for smaller models. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\] explores two curriculum variants: a forward curriculum that gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, and a reverse curriculum that does the opposite. The forward curriculum enables SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training, improving downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. In contrast, the reverse curriculum achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The effectiveness of curriculum learning in improving model performance has been explored in other domains as well. \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\] introduces a curriculum learning approach for natural language understanding (NLU) tasks, where examples are arranged in an easy-to-difficult order to align with human learning procedures. This approach significantly improves performance across a wide range of NLU tasks without requiring manual model architecture design or external data \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\]. Similarly, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\] proposes a competence-based curriculum learning framework for neural machine translation (NMT), which reduces training time and improves performance by filtering training samples based on their difficulty and the model’s current competence \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\].

### <a id='category-multi-token-prediction-(mtp)-methodology'></a>Multi-token Prediction (MTP) Methodology: 9 papers found.
Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to next-token prediction (NTP) in language model pretraining, offering potential benefits in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\]. Unlike NTP, which predicts one token at a time, MTP trains models to predict multiple future tokens simultaneously, often using multiple output heads \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\]. This approach has been shown to improve inference speed, with some models achieving up to 3 times faster inference even with large batch sizes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\]. Additionally, MTP can enhance the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies and improve generative capabilities, particularly in coding tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in effectively leveraging the MTP objective, prompting the development of curriculum learning strategies to gradually adapt models from NTP to MTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Various methods have been proposed to address these challenges, including leap-based multi-token prediction (L-MTP), which skips intermediate tokens to enable non-sequential prediction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\], and joint multi-token prediction (JTP), which uses a representation bottleneck to jointly predict multiple future tokens with minimal computational overhead \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21801)\]. Furthermore, speculative decoding has been introduced as a decoding paradigm that allows for the simultaneous prediction of multiple tokens, reducing inference latency \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07851)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in MTP as a way to enhance both training and inference processes in large language models.

### <a id='category-next-token-prediction-(ntp)-as-a-baseline'></a>Next-token Prediction (NTP) as a Baseline: 2 papers found.
Next-token prediction (NTP) has been the dominant training paradigm for large language models (LLMs), serving as a foundational method for both understanding and generation tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\]. While alternative approaches such as critical token prediction (CTP) have been proposed to focus on specific tokens like answers in Q&A datasets, empirical studies show that NTP outperforms CTP in reasoning ability, despite its exposure to noise during training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\]. This is attributed to the regularizing effect of noise, which enhances generalization and robustness across reasoning benchmarks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\]. Furthermore, NTP has been extended to multimodal learning, where it is used to encapsulate tasks from different modalities by transforming multimodal information into tokens and predicting the next one given the context \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. This versatility has led to the development of a comprehensive taxonomy that unifies understanding and generation within the NTP framework, covering aspects such as multimodal tokenization, model architectures, and evaluation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. These advancements highlight the enduring relevance of NTP as a baseline for training LLMs, even as new objectives like multi-token prediction (MTP) emerge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\].

### <a id='category-curriculum-learning-strategies'></a>Curriculum Learning Strategies: 6 papers found.
Curriculum learning strategies have been extensively explored to enhance the performance of language models across various tasks, including natural language understanding (NLU), code generation, and sequence prediction. These strategies aim to address the challenge of varying example difficulty by introducing a structured, progressive learning path, similar to human learning \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\]. For instance, in NLU tasks, curriculum learning has been shown to significantly improve model performance by arranging training data in an order that transitions from easy to difficult examples, without requiring manual architecture design or external data \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\]. This approach has also been applied to neural machine translation (NMT), where a curriculum learning framework reduces training time and improves performance by dynamically selecting training samples based on their estimated difficulty and the model’s current competence \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\]. In code language models, curriculum learning has demonstrated notable improvements in code execution accuracy, although its impact on code completion remains less pronounced \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\]. Additionally, curriculum learning has been adapted for reinforcement learning-based post-training of large language models, where it leverages distribution-level learnability to optimize training schedules \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09710)\]. These strategies often rely on automated methods to determine the optimal curriculum, such as using multi-armed bandit algorithms to select the most informative training samples based on learning progress metrics \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03003)\]. Overall, curriculum learning provides a flexible and effective approach to improving model performance across diverse tasks and model architectures.
