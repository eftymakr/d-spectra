## Related Works

Fairness in machine learning has garnered significant attention due to its implications for societal equity and algorithmic accountability. Early work on fairness in ML focused primarily on classification tasks, where researchers explored definitions and measures of fairness, such as demographic parity and equalized odds \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. These approaches often relied on accuracy-based metrics, which fail to capture nuanced aspects of bias, such as model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. More recent studies have highlighted the limitations of traditional fairness metrics, which may overlook implicit biases stemming from model confidence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. For instance, Equalized Odds, a widely used fairness criterion, does not account for variations in model confidence across different groups, leading to potential misinterpretations of fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

The importance of context-specific fairness evaluation has also been emphasized. Research has shown that fairness risks vary significantly across deployment contexts, and existing benchmarks may not generalize well to new or diverse settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. This has led to the development of use-case-specific frameworks that map LLM use cases to relevant fairness metrics based on task type, prompt characteristics, and stakeholder priorities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. Such frameworks underscore the need for tailored evaluation strategies that consider the unique challenges of different applications.

In the realm of translation systems, recent work has addressed gender bias in machine translation, particularly in handling non-binary gender expressions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]. The FairTranslate dataset, for example, was introduced to evaluate non-binary gender biases in English-French translation systems, revealing persistent challenges in achieving equitable outcomes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]. This aligns with broader concerns about how LLMs perpetuate harmful social biases, particularly in areas like gender representation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

Moreover, fairness in ML has been linked to robustness, as models may exhibit robustness disparities across subgroups, leading to biased decision-making under adversarial conditions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\]. This highlights the need for fairness metrics that account for both model outputs and model robustness, especially in real-world applications where adversarial attacks are a concern \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\].

Several studies have also emphasized the importance of stakeholder involvement in designing fairness metrics \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c1f52179b75bd55f662b64c3c2358bbd73f8243d)\]. For instance, in the context of recommender systems, researchers have collaborated with content creators and dating app users to co-design fairness goals and metrics that better reflect their experiences of algorithmic fairness \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c1f52179b75bd55f662b64c3c2358bbd73f8243d)\]. This participatory approach is critical for ensuring that fairness metrics are both meaningful and actionable.

[Snippet]: «Bias and fairness risks in Large Language Models (LLMs) vary substantially across deployment contexts, yet existing approaches lack systematic guidance for selecting appropriate evaluation metrics. We present a decision framework that maps LLM use cases, characterized by a model and population of prompts, to relevant bias and fairness metrics based on task type, whether prompts contain protected attribute mentions, and stakeholder priorities. Our framework addresses toxicity, stereotyping, counterfactual unfairness, and allocational harms, and introduces novel metrics based on stereotype classifiers and counterfactual adaptations of text similarity measures. We release an open-source Python library, \texttt(langfair), for practical adoption. In this paper, we argue that traditional notions of fairness that are only based on models' outputs are not sufficient when the model is vulnerable to adversarial attacks. We argue that this kind of bias arises due to both the data distribution and the highly complex nature of the learned decision boundary in the case of DNNs, thus making mitigation of such biases a non-trivial task. Our results show that robustness bias is an important criterion to consider while auditing real-world systems that rely on DNNs for decision making.
