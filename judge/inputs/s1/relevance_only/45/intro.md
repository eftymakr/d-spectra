## Related Works

Opinion mining has gained significant traction in software engineering and natural language processing (NLP) due to its ability to extract meaningful insights from textual data \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e0a7e1c24edc628b093daf4dca1dca86a583c377)\]. While most studies focus on sentiment polarity (positive, negative, neutral), fine-grained emotion classification in app reviews remains underexplored \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. This paper addresses this gap by developing a structured annotation framework and dataset based on Plutchik’s emotion taxonomy \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. Prior work has identified challenges in emotion annotation, including the subjective nature of emotions and the difficulty in creating reliable annotations \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/72c953db90d98c7377a942394161aa127ba1494d)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of crowdsourcing and best-worst scaling to generate word-sentiment associations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01741)\]. These techniques have been applied across different domains, including general English, Twitter, and Arabic, showing consistent results in ranking words by sentiment \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01741)\]. However, emotion annotation in text remains a complex task, with current annotation schemes based on psychological theories not always conducive to creating reliable annotations \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/72c953db90d98c7377a942394161aa127ba1494d)\]. 

The use of large language models (LLMs) for emotion annotation has emerged as a promising tool to reduce the workload of human annotators \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. GPT-4, in particular, has shown high ratings in human evaluation studies, outperforming previous work where human labels were the only ground truth \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. Despite this, differences between human and LLM emotion perception persist, highlighting the importance of human input in annotation studies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. 

The GoEmotions dataset, a large manually annotated dataset of 58k English Reddit comments, has been used to evaluate the performance of emotion detection models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. This dataset includes 27 emotion categories and has been used to demonstrate the generalization capability of models across different domains and emotion taxonomies \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. The BERT-based model achieves an average F1-score of 0.46, indicating room for improvement in fine-grained emotion detection \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. 

Fine-grained emotion prediction has also been addressed through multi-task learning frameworks that model emotion definitions as an auxiliary task \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12135)\]. This approach has shown superior performance on the GoEmotions dataset and has demonstrated the potential for transfer learning on other benchmark datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12135)\]. 

In the context of app reviews, emotion analysis has been explored using both manual and automated methods \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\]. A study on app reviews from the Apple App Store analyzed over seven million reviews to evaluate emotional sentiment, showing the potential of sentiment analysis as an informative feature for software engineers \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\].
