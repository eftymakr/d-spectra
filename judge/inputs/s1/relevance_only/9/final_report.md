## Related Works

The detection and segmentation of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) in multiple sclerosis (MS) have been the focus of numerous studies, driven by their clinical significance as biomarkers of disease progression and severity \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. PRLs, characterized by a paramagnetic rim surrounding the lesion, are associated with more aggressive disease courses and are detectable using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. However, their rarity and the inherent class imbalance between PRLs and non-rim lesions pose significant challenges for automated detection \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. 

Several studies have explored automated methods for PRL detection. A fully automated technique using radiomic features and machine learning algorithms has been proposed to classify PRLs with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80 \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. Another study introduced QSM-RimNet, a deep learning-based method for PRL detection, but it lacked rim segmentation capabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10492)\]. In response, QSM-RimDS was developed to provide both detection and segmentation, achieving a mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.57 with expert segmentation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10492)\]. These methods highlight the importance of accurate segmentation for quantifying lesion characteristics and understanding disease mechanisms.

The challenge of class imbalance has also been addressed in recent works. QSMRim-Net, a data imbalance-aware deep neural network, outperformed other state-of-the-art methods by achieving a partial AUC of 0.760 and a high correlation with expert annotations \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. Similarly, the RimSet method, which combines unsupervised segmentation with radiomic features, demonstrated improved performance over existing methods, achieving a partial ROC AUC of 0.808 \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16835)\]. These approaches underscore the need for robust feature extraction and handling of class imbalance in PRL detection.

Synthetic data generation has emerged as a promising strategy to address the scarcity of PRLs in training datasets. A study proposed a generative adversarial network (GAN) to synthesize synthetic QSM images of PRLs, which were used to improve classifier performance \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.09065)\]. This approach was further extended to generate synthetic lesions with accompanying contrasts and probabilistic segmentation maps, enhancing the robustness of detection algorithms \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. The use of synthetic data has also been explored in other medical imaging tasks, such as liver tumor segmentation, where it significantly improved segmentation accuracy \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.09065)\].

The category "Automated Detection Methods" highlights the development of machine learning techniques for identifying and classifying paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) in multiple sclerosis (MS) using MRI data. A study introduced an automated approach using radiomic features and a random forest classifier to distinguish PRLs from non-rim lesions, achieving an area under the curve of 0.80 \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. This approach leverages standard T1, T2-FLAIR, and T2*-phase sequences, demonstrating the feasibility of automated PRL detection \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. Other works emphasize the importance of advanced MRI techniques and expert knowledge for assessing emerging biomarkers such as cortical lesions and the central vein sign, which are also relevant to MS research \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.07463)\]. The challenge of classifying PRLs is influenced by lesion heterogeneity, including equivocal rim signatures and variable locations \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. Additionally, the literature underscores the difficulty of automated lesion segmentation due to the high variability in lesion appearance and MRI acquisition protocols, highlighting the need for robust and universally applicable methods \[[30](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Efforts to improve performance in the presence of class imbalance, such as generating synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps, have shown promise in enhancing classifier accuracy and enabling probabilistic segmentation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. These advancements reflect a growing trend toward integrating generative models and machine learning to overcome limitations in current automated detection techniques.

### <a id='category-segmentation-techniques-for-prls'></a>Segmentation Techniques for PRLs: 11 papers found.
The related works section focuses on segmentation techniques for paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) in multiple sclerosis, emphasizing the challenges of detection and segmentation using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) and deep learning approaches. Existing methods, such as QSM-RimNet, provide automated PRL detection but lack accurate rim segmentation for microglial density quantification \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10492)\]. To address this, QSM-RimDS was developed as a U-Net-based method that enables joint PRL detection and rim segmentation using T2-weighted FLAIR lesion masks, achieving a mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of 0.57 and improved detection performance with an AUC of 0.754 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10492)\]. Similarly, RimSet combines an unsupervised segmentation method with radiomic measurements to detect rim+ lesions, achieving a partial ROC AUC of 0.808 and PR AUC of 0.737 \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.16835)\]. Other studies have explored deep learning frameworks, such as nnU-Net, to improve segmentation performance through data augmentation and lesion-aware methods, achieving an average Dice score of 0.510 and low false positive rates \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b1d6aa6e4f34601f21fdc459d2aed99a464726fa)\]. Additionally, fully convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been proposed to segment white matter lesions from multi-contrast MR images, with significant improvements in segmentation quality over existing methods \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance lesion segmentation accuracy, particularly in addressing class imbalance and improving robustness across different MRI scanners and lesion types \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d21452a4c6439e9e90e7127252cab9cd4a7230f8)\]. The integration of synthetic data generation and denoising techniques further demonstrates the potential to improve classifier performance and segmentation consistency in rare lesion types like PRLs \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\].

### <a id='category-synthetic-data-generation'></a>Synthetic Data Generation: 5 papers found.
Synthetic data generation has emerged as a critical approach to address data scarcity and class imbalance in medical imaging, particularly in the context of lesion detection and segmentation. Recent studies have focused on generating realistic synthetic lesions to augment training datasets and improve model performance. For instance, a foreground-based generative method has been proposed to synthesize pseudo-healthy and pseudo-pathological brain images, demonstrating significant improvements in segmentation performance compared to traditional augmentation techniques \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02135)\]. Similarly, a two-input two-output fully convolutional neural network model has been introduced to generate synthetic multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions on MRI images, enabling data augmentation without manual annotation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. Additionally, a modified generative adversarial network (GAN) with a partial convolution strategy has been developed to generate synthetic liver lesions, achieving high similarity in texture parameters compared to real lesions \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.09065)\]. In the domain of skin lesion analysis, GANs have been utilized to synthesize high-resolution dermoscopic images, producing realistic images that even expert dermatologists find difficult to distinguish from real ones \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.01410)\]. Furthermore, GANs have been applied to synthesize quantitative susceptibility maps of paramagnetic rim lesions in multiple sclerosis, enhancing classifier performance and enabling probabilistic segmentation maps \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. These approaches highlight the growing importance of synthetic data generation in overcoming the challenges of limited labeled data and class imbalance in medical imaging tasks.

### <a id='category-deep-learning-architectures'></a>Deep Learning Architectures: 6 papers found.
Automatic segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data has been a focal point in deep learning research, with several studies proposing advanced architectures to improve accuracy and generalization. For instance, U-Net-based models have been widely adopted for their effectiveness in biomedical image segmentation, with modifications to enhance performance on multi-modal MRI data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02135)\]. These models often incorporate data augmentation techniques to address the challenge of limited training datasets \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c5b009008dba478edd3079793a7f878a6402835c)\]. Additionally, attention-guided architectures have been introduced to facilitate information exchange between different time points, improving lesion activity segmentation by suppressing irrelevant lesions \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02001)\]. Furthermore, the use of inception modules in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has been explored to handle varying lesion sizes by applying multiple filter sizes in parallel, leading to improved segmentation outcomes \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/88e2056078ebd7de11cb88be4ab8fea648e986df)\]. Recent approaches have also leveraged transformer-based architectures, such as ChangeFormer, which utilize Siamese networks for detecting new and enlarging lesions by analyzing changes in MRI scans \[[27](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/10f2005871bc7fe1b7822d3746cb292da38a4565)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to develop robust and generalizable deep learning models for MS lesion segmentation, addressing challenges such as class imbalance and the need for accurate, reproducible results \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\].

### <a id='category-medical-imaging-applications'></a>Medical Imaging Applications: 5 papers found.
In the context of medical imaging applications for multiple sclerosis, recent advancements have focused on improving lesion detection and segmentation, particularly for paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), which are critical biomarkers for disease progression \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a18be9d1fecb50c5537f2911f127bda61b71fa28)\]. Traditional MRI techniques, such as T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR sequences, provide complementary insights into lesion characteristics, but automated segmentation remains challenging due to the need for extensive annotations and the rarity of PRLs, leading to class imbalance issues \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b3c3d8404369a4fe76c70cd02f240901cff6f908)\]. To address these challenges, deep learning approaches have been increasingly utilized, enabling automated detection of various lesion subtypes, including PRLs, with improved accuracy and reproducibility \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a18be9d1fecb50c5537f2911f127bda61b71fa28)\]. Additionally, self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a promising alternative, allowing models to exploit unannotated data and reduce reliance on costly and error-prone annotations \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b3c3d8404369a4fe76c70cd02f240901cff6f908)\]. Furthermore, unsupervised lesion detection methods have gained attention, leveraging deep learning to estimate normative distributions of healthy anatomy and detect lesions as outliers without training examples \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00031)\]. These methods often involve latent-variable models to approximate normative distributions and identify lesions based on differences between reconstructed and original images, although they may suffer from false positives \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00031)\]. Recent studies have also explored generative adversarial networks (GANs) to synthesize quantitative susceptibility maps of PRLs, enhancing classifier performance and enabling probabilistic segmentation maps \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. Such approaches not only improve the detection of rare lesions but also support longitudinal monitoring and clinical decision-making in MS management \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a18be9d1fecb50c5537f2911f127bda61b71fa28)\].
