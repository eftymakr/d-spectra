## Related Works

Fund allocation in financial domains has attracted significant research interest, with a growing emphasis on integrating forecasting and optimization in a unified framework. Traditional approaches typically separate these tasks, first predicting asset returns and then optimizing the portfolio based on these forecasts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\]. However, this two-step process often leads to goal mismatch, as the optimization stage may not fully account for the uncertainty inherent in the forecasting step \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\]. This issue is further exacerbated by the introduction of state-of-the-art time series forecasting models, which, while powerful, introduce additional uncertainty into the prediction process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\]. To address these challenges, recent works have proposed end-to-end frameworks that jointly optimize the allocation process with the forecasting task \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\].

One such approach is the Portfolio Transformer (PT), which directly optimizes the Sharpe ratio without requiring explicit return forecasts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\]. The PT leverages attention mechanisms to model long-term dependencies among assets, enabling it to adapt quickly to changing market conditions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\]. This work is closely related to our proposed Risk-aware Time-Series Predict-and-Allocate (RTS-PnO) framework, which also aims to integrate forecasting and allocation in an end-to-end manner \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving the robustness of forecasting models in financial applications. The FinTSBridge suite evaluates various time series forecasting models on financial datasets, highlighting the importance of domain-specific metrics such as msIC and msIR \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06928)\]. Similarly, the TPA-LSTM model introduces a novel attention mechanism to capture temporal patterns in multivariate time series, achieving state-of-the-art performance on several real-world tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04206)\]. These models emphasize the need for specialized architectures that can effectively model complex temporal dependencies in financial data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04206)\].

The integration of large language models (LLMs) into financial forecasting has also gained traction. Recent studies demonstrate that LLMs can provide explainable forecasts by reasoning over textual and numerical inputs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. For instance, the LLM-based approach presented in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\] outperforms traditional models such as ARMA-GARCH and gradient-boosting trees, showcasing the potential of LLMs in financial forecasting tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. This aligns with our RTS-PnO framework, which is agnostic to the forecasting model used and can incorporate various models, including LLMs, into its optimization process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03246)\].

In addition to forecasting, transfer learning has been explored to address the challenge of limited historical data in financial applications \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.12648)\].
