## Related Works

Representation learning is a cornerstone of modern recommender systems, enabling efficient user-item matching through vectorized embeddings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. However, the computational and memory costs of dense embeddings have motivated research into binarization techniques, which convert embeddings into compact binary representations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. This approach reduces both storage and inference costs, making it suitable for resource-constrained environments. Despite these benefits, existing methods often suffer from significant performance degradation due to information loss during binarization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. 

Several studies have explored graph-based collaborative filtering, which leverages user-item interaction graphs to capture high-order relationships \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. For example, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates bipartite graph structures into the embedding process, improving the modeling of collaborative signals \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) explicitly incorporates multiple graphs, including user-user and item-item relationships, to enhance the modeling of structural information \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of graph structure in capturing latent user preferences.

Binarization techniques have also been applied to graph neural networks (GNNs) to improve efficiency. Binarized Collaborative Filtering with Distilling Graph Convolutional Networks (Binarized GCN) introduces a framework to convert binary constrained optimization into a continuous optimization problem, enabling efficient training with standard solvers like SGD and Adam \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. This approach addresses the challenge of optimizing binary codes while preserving information during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Additionally, Near-lossless Binarization of Word Embeddings demonstrates that binary representations can retain high semantic accuracy, with only a 2% loss in accuracy compared to real-valued embeddings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\].

In the context of recommendation systems, contrastive learning has been used to enhance embedding quality by leveraging unlabeled data \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bcf2b3bfd3f7df75c0590444ad531e3cf1f02bb1)\]. Community-Enhanced Contrastive Learning (CECL) introduces a novel sampling method that considers both global and local information, improving the effectiveness of contrastive learning in sparse data scenarios \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bcf2b3bfd3f7df75c0590444ad531e3cf1f02bb1)\]. Similarly, TwinCL proposes a twin encoder mechanism to generate diverse contrastive views, optimizing alignment and uniformity on a hypersphere \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19169)\]. These methods highlight the potential of contrastive learning to improve embedding quality, particularly in data-scarce settings.

### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering-models'></a>Graph-Based Collaborative Filtering Models: 12 papers found.
Graph-based collaborative filtering models have emerged as a powerful approach in recommendation systems, leveraging the structure of user-item interactions to enhance the learning of user and item embeddings \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03502)\]. These models typically incorporate bipartite graphs to capture high-order connectivity and collaborative signals, which are critical for accurate recommendations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) explicitly integrates user-item graph structures into the embedding process, enabling the propagation of embeddings and capturing complex relationships \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) extends this idea by incorporating additional user-user and item-item graphs, thereby enriching the model with proximal information \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Furthermore, the use of graph convolutional networks (GCNs) has been explored to improve feature propagation and enhance the model's ability to generalize from sparse data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11824)\]. Variational embeddings and graph variational embedding collaborative filtering (GVECF) have also been introduced to pre-train recommenders and improve the quality of feature propagation through graph convolutional networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11824)\]. In addition, recent studies have focused on addressing data sparsity and improving robustness through techniques such as contrastive learning and perturbation-based methods \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bcf2b3bfd3f7df75c0590444ad531e3cf1f02bb1)\]. For example, the Community-Enhanced Contrastive Learning (CECL) method uses community detection to sample examples with semantic and global information, leading to significant performance improvements \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bcf2b3bfd3f7df75c0590444ad531e3cf1f02bb1)\]. Moreover, the integration of spike signal embedding perturbation (SEP) into the graph collaborative filtering process has been shown to enhance model performance and training efficiency \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3c8fba26e95c922d2616ecfb891de2542d01aea8)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to refine graph-based collaborative filtering models by incorporating diverse graph structures, improving embedding quality, and addressing key challenges such as data sparsity and over-smoothing \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03502)\].

### <a id='category-representation-learning-in-recommender-systems'></a>Representation Learning in Recommender Systems: 3 papers found.
Representation learning has emerged as a critical approach in recommender systems, particularly in the context of collaborative filtering, where it enables the creation of more informative and robust problem characterizations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. Traditional metafeatures, while useful, are often based on practitioner intuition and may miss key aspects of the problem \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. By leveraging representation learning techniques such as graph2vec, researchers can generate distributed representations that are guided by the data itself, leading to more accurate algorithm selection \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. This approach not only enhances the performance of metalearning but also reduces the need for extensive human input \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. Additionally, inductive frameworks like GraphSAGE have been developed to generate node embeddings for previously unseen data, improving the generalization capabilities of recommendation models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. These methods are particularly valuable in large-scale systems where efficient and scalable representation learning is essential \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Furthermore, deep learning approaches that jointly model distributional representations for users and items have shown promising results in collaborative filtering, achieving better performance than existing state-of-the-art algorithms \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04163)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of representation learning in improving the efficiency and effectiveness of recommender systems.
