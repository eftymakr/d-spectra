## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have been widely studied in continual learning (CL) to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. These methods store a subset of past data in a memory buffer and reuse it during training on new tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17091)\]. This approach has shown superior performance compared to other strategies, particularly in scenarios where the model needs to retain knowledge from previous tasks while adapting to new ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\]. However, the optimal way to structure rehearsal—whether to train past and new data together or to revisit past data sequentially—remains an open question.

Concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is a common strategy. However, it may lead to overfitting on past samples, especially when tasks are dissimilar \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17091)\]. In contrast, sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first and then past data is revisited, has shown promise in reducing forgetting by maintaining a clearer separation between tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17091)\]. This idea is supported by empirical evidence from various studies, which suggest that sequential rehearsal can improve generalization and reduce the risk of overfitting \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\].

Several works have explored the theoretical underpinnings of rehearsal-based CL. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17091)\] provides a theoretical analysis of rehearsal-based CL under overparameterized linear models, showing that sequential rehearsal performs better when tasks are less similar. This finding aligns with the hypothesis that sequential rehearsal can better preserve the distinctiveness of past tasks, thereby reducing the risk of catastrophic forgetting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17091)\]. Furthermore, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\] highlights that models trained sequentially with rehearsal tend to stay in the same low-loss region after a task has finished, which may lead to overfitting on past samples and harm generalization.

The importance of robust features in mitigating catastrophic forgetting has also been emphasized in recent studies \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8eaed8eafb25d5c41cf0fce53d276765ddded613)\]. These features help the model retain knowledge from previous tasks by providing stable representations that are less prone to forgetting. This aligns with the idea that rehearsal should focus on preserving these robust features rather than raw data samples \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8eaed8eafb25d5c41cf0fce53d276765ddded613)\]. Additionally, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.12641)\] introduces a dynamic prototype-guided memory replay module that stores only a few samples to achieve good performance, suggesting that efficient memory management is crucial for effective rehearsal.

In the context of deep reinforcement learning, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02464)\] proposes a pseudo-rehearsal system that "recalls" items representative of previous tasks via a deep generative network. This approach allows the model to learn sequentially without catastrophic forgetting, demonstrating that rehearsal can be effective even in complex reinforcement learning settings \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02464)\].
