## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods such as matrix factorization laid the groundwork, but they lacked the ability to explicitly model high-order interactions between users and items \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This limitation was addressed by Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), which integrated user-item interactions into the embedding process through graph propagation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, NGCF and similar models often rely on nonlinear activations and feature transformations, which can complicate training and reduce performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

The field has evolved further with LightGCN, which simplifies GCN by removing non-linearities and focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation. This approach leads to improved performance and easier training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, Multi-GCCF extends this idea by incorporating user-user and item-item graphs, capturing more nuanced relationships \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These models highlight the importance of modeling not only direct interactions but also indirect ones through graph structures.

Another key development is the use of graph signal processing for collaborative filtering, as seen in SpectralCF. This method operates directly in the spectral domain, leveraging connectivity information to discover deeper user-item relationships and alleviate the cold-start problem \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. The success of these methods underscores the value of spectral analysis in capturing latent patterns in user-item graphs.

Despite these advances, challenges remain, particularly in handling large-scale graphs and ensuring robustness against noisy interactions. FastGCN addresses computational inefficiencies by using importance sampling, enabling efficient training on large graphs \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10247)\]. Similarly, RGCF introduces refined graph convolution and normalization techniques to enhance embedding quality, especially in sparse scenarios \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. These approaches demonstrate the ongoing efforts to improve both the scalability and expressiveness of graph-based recommendation models.

Recent work also explores the use of graph contrastive learning, as seen in QRec, which simplifies the training process by removing graph augmentations and instead adding uniform noise to embeddings \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08679)\]. This approach not only improves training efficiency but also enhances recommendation accuracy. Additionally, Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) focuses on modeling user intents more explicitly, leading to more interpretable and diverse representations \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\].

In summary, the field of graph-based collaborative filtering has progressed from basic matrix factorization to sophisticated GCN models that leverage spectral and structural information. While these models have achieved impressive results, ongoing research continues to refine their capabilities, addressing issues such as scalability, robustness, and interpretability.
