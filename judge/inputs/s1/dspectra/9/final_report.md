## Related Works

The detection and segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions, particularly paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), have been extensively studied in recent years. These lesions, characterized by a paramagnetic rim due to iron deposition, are considered important biomarkers for disease progression and treatment response \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. However, due to their rarity, they pose significant challenges for automated detection and segmentation methods \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Several studies have explored the use of deep learning techniques for MS lesion segmentation, with a focus on improving performance on imbalanced datasets \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]\[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a6c3b459dcaed58347ae0d270c0cfd93ee8aed6f)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07950)\].

Early approaches to MS lesion segmentation relied on intensity-based models, such as the MOPS model, which attempted to distinguish lesions from normal tissue by analyzing signal intensity distributions \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83d2364d1672806d66d75c0bc78a53970905c330)\]. However, these methods often struggled with the high overlap between lesion and normal tissue intensities, leading to suboptimal sensitivity and specificity \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83d2364d1672806d66d75c0bc78a53970905c330)\]. More recent work has focused on using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for lesion segmentation, leveraging multi-modal MRI data to improve accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]\[[26](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d21452a4c6439e9e90e7127252cab9cd4a7230f8)\]. For instance, the use of a multi-branch CNN architecture enabled the integration of information from different MRI modalities, leading to improved segmentation performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\].

The challenge of class imbalance, particularly in the case of rare PRLs, has also been addressed through synthetic data generation. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been employed to create synthetic lesion images, which can be used to augment training datasets and improve classifier performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01229)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07950)\]. In particular, the use of GANs to generate synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps (QSMs) has shown promise in addressing the imbalance between rim and non-rim lesions \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83d2364d1672806d66d75c0bc78a53970905c330)\]. This approach allows for the creation of synthetic PRLs that can be used to train models on a more balanced dataset, thereby improving detection accuracy \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/83d2364d1672806d66d75c0bc78a53970905c330)\].

Several studies have also explored the use of attention mechanisms and multi-scale feature fusion to enhance lesion segmentation accuracy \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02001)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12470)\]. For example, the RSANet architecture, which models 3D MRI images as sequences of slices and captures long-range dependencies through a recurrent manner, has demonstrated superior performance compared to state-of-the-art methods \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12470)\]. ### <a id='category-synthetic-data-generation-for-medical-imaging'></a>Synthetic Data Generation for Medical Imaging: 4 papers found.
Synthetic data generation for medical imaging has gained significant attention due to the challenges associated with obtaining large-scale annotated datasets in the medical domain \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01229)\]. Various approaches, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), have been explored to synthesize realistic medical images, such as MRI scans and skin lesion images \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.01410)\]. For instance, GANs have been successfully used to generate synthetic multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions on MRI images, enabling data augmentation and improving the performance of supervised machine learning algorithms \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. These synthetic lesions are created by encoding lesion information as binary masks and training a fully convolutional neural network end-to-end, eliminating the need for manual annotation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. Similarly, in the context of liver lesion classification, GANs have been employed to generate high-quality synthetic lesion ROIs, which, when combined with traditional data augmentation, significantly improved classification performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01229)\]. Moreover, methods have been proposed to generate subject-specific pseudo-healthy and pseudo-pathological brain images, enhancing the training of segmentation networks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02135)\]. In skin lesion analysis, GANs with progressive growing techniques have been utilized to synthesize high-resolution dermoscopic images, which are indistinguishable from real images even to expert dermatologists \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.01410)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of synthetic data augmentation to address class imbalance and improve diagnostic accuracy in medical imaging tasks.
