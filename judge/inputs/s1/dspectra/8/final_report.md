## Related Works

Fairness in machine learning has gained significant attention due to its implications in real-world decision-making systems. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10104)\], focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations and often overlook the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more nuanced fairness measures that account for model confidence and decision bias \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. Recent studies have explored various fairness-enhancing mechanisms, categorized into pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing approaches \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04053)\]. These mechanisms aim to mitigate bias in different stages of the machine learning pipeline, but they often fail to address the complexities introduced by model uncertainty \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05511)\].

The issue of fairness in algorithmic decision-making is not new; it has been studied in fields such as education and hiring for over 50 years \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10104)\]. These early definitions of fairness have influenced modern fairness research, though many insights have been overlooked \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10104)\]. Current fairness research has expanded to include not only classification tasks but also regression, recommender systems, and unsupervised learning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04053)\]. However, the challenge remains in developing metrics that capture both the accuracy and fairness of predictions, particularly when uncertainty is involved \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

Several studies have investigated fairness in recommendation systems, where biased historical data can lead to unfair predictions against minority groups \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\]. These studies have proposed new fairness metrics that address different forms of unfairness and have shown that incorporating fairness terms into the learning objective can effectively reduce bias \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\]. Similarly, research on fairness in deep learning has highlighted the importance of robustness and the potential for robustness bias, where certain subgroups may be more vulnerable to adversarial attacks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\]. This has led to the development of methods for measuring robustness bias and understanding its implications for fairness \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\].

The concept of counterfactual fairness has also emerged as a framework for modeling fairness using causal inference \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This approach defines fairness in terms of whether a decision would remain the same in a counterfactual world where the individual belonged to a different demographic group \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This idea aligns with the need for fairness metrics that go beyond simple accuracy and consider the broader implications of model decisions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

In addition to these efforts, there is growing recognition of the importance of measurement modeling in fairness research \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05511)\]. This approach emphasizes the need to align theoretical constructs with operationalizations, ensuring that fairness metrics accurately reflect the intended outcomes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05511)\]. This is particularly relevant when dealing with unobservable constructs such as socioeconomic status or risk of recidivism, which must be inferred from observable data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05511)\].

[Snippet]: «We propose measurement modeling from the quantitative social sciences as a framework for understanding fairness in computational systems. Computational systems often involve unobservable theoretical constructs, such as socioeconomic status, teacher effectiveness, and risk of recidivism. Such constructs cannot be measured directly and must instead be inferred from measurements of observable properties (and other unobservable theoretical constructs) thought to be related to them---i.e., operationalized via a measurement model. This process, which necessarily involves making assumptions, introduces the potential for mismatches between the theoretical understanding of the construct purported to be measured and its operationalization. We argue that many of the harms discussed in the literature on fairness in computational systems are direct results of such mismatches. We show how some of these harms could have been anticipated and, in some cases, mitigated if viewed through the lens of measurement modeling. To do this, we contribute fairness-oriented conceptualizations of construct reliability and construct validity that unite traditions from political science, education, and psychology and provide a set of tools for making explicit and testing assumptions about constructs and their operationalizations. We then turn to fairness itself, an essentially contested construct that has different theoretical understandings in different contexts. We argue that this contestedness underlies recent debates about fairness definitions: although these debates appear to be about different operationalizations, they are, in fact, debates about different theoretical understandings of fairness. We show how measurement modeling can provide a framework for getting to the core of these debates.»

To address these gaps, new fairness metrics have been proposed, incorporating fairness terms into the learning objective to better align model behavior with fairness principles \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\].
