## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has garnered significant attention in recent years, with a growing body of research addressing the trade-offs between ranking accuracy and item fairness. Early work in this area, such as \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\], highlights the challenges of incorporating fairness constraints into ranking algorithms, noting that fairness can reduce diversity and lead to polarized outcomes. This issue has been further explored in \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\], where the authors emphasize the importance of considering individual preferences when designing fair recommendation systems, as prior work often overlooked how personal preferences could limit the ability to achieve fairness. 

The concept of fairness in rankings has been formalized in various ways, including the use of demographic parity and disparate impact constraints \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. These constraints aim to ensure that items from different groups are represented fairly in the ranking. However, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01203)\] argues that the assumption of inherent opposition between accuracy and fairness is often unexamined, leading to inconsistent conclusions and even emergent unfairness. This highlights the need for more nuanced approaches to fairness that account for the complexities of real-world data and user interactions.

Several studies have proposed methods to incorporate fairness into re-ranking algorithms. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] introduces a fairness-aware re-ranking algorithm (FAR) that balances ranking quality with provider-side fairness, while \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01989)\] presents a framework for quantifying and mitigating algorithmic bias in ranking systems, achieving fairness criteria such as equality of opportunity and demographic parity. The work in \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\] further extends this by proposing a joint optimization approach that considers both consumer and producer fairness in recommender systems, demonstrating that fairness can be improved without sacrificing recommendation quality.

The economic analogy between re-ranking and commodity tax transfer has been explored in \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\], where the authors introduce a taxation perspective for fair re-ranking, reformulating previous methods as item-level tax policies. This approach allows for a more flexible and controllable way to adjust ranking resources. The concept of elasticity, which measures how changes in fairness affect accuracy, has also been applied to ranking systems. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\] introduces the idea of amortized group fairness, ensuring that fairness guarantees are maintained over time while learning the ranking function from implicit feedback data.

In addition to these methods, several studies have focused on the evaluation of fairness in ranked outputs. \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08559)\] proposes fairness measures for ranked outputs, demonstrating how fairness can be systematically controlled and evaluated. The work in \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00787)\] further advances this by using inequality indices from economics to measure both individual and group unfairness, providing a unified framework for comparing different fairness metrics.

### <a id='category-fairness-aware-re-ranking-methods'></a>Fairness-Aware Re-ranking Methods: 6 papers found.
Fairness-aware re-ranking methods have gained significant attention as recommender systems increasingly influence high-stakes domains like employment and housing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. Furthermore, systems have been designed to help users choose criterion weights that enhance fairness, enabling efficient identification of regions in a multi-dimensional space that satisfy fairness criteria \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. These advancements underscore the growing recognition of fairness as a critical factor in re-ranking, with ongoing efforts to address its complexities through novel metrics and algorithmic innovations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\].
