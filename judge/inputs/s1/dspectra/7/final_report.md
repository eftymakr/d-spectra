## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit various behaviors, including refusal, which is often a result of safety fine-tuning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Traditional methods for identifying such behaviors rely on predefined refusal templates detectable in output tokens or require manual analysis \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. These approaches are limited in their ability to generalize across different models and alignment conditions. Recent work has explored the use of steering vectors to control LLM outputs, with methods such as Adaptive Activation Steering (ACT) showing promising results in improving truthfulness by modifying model activations during inference \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00034)\]. However, these methods often require fine-tuning or specific steering vectors tailored to particular tasks or models \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00034)\].

The concept of steering in LLMs has also been explored in the context of multimodal reasoning, where models are trained to incorporate both textual and visual information to generate more accurate and contextually relevant responses \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00923)\]. This approach, known as Multimodal-CoT, separates rationale generation from answer inference, allowing for better utilization of multimodal information \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00923)\]. Similarly, the use of cosine similarity to measure the similarity between embeddings has been widely studied, with some research highlighting the limitations of cosine similarity for high-frequency words \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05092)\]. These studies emphasize the importance of understanding the geometric properties of embeddings and how they affect similarity metrics \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05092)\].

Another line of research has focused on the use of pre-trained models for various tasks, including object recognition and scene understanding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. The Microsoft COCO dataset, for example, has been instrumental in advancing object recognition by providing a large-scale, annotated dataset of complex everyday scenes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. Similarly, the BERT model has demonstrated significant improvements in natural language processing tasks through its bidirectional training approach \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. These models have been further enhanced through techniques such as residual learning, which allows for the creation of deeper networks without compromising performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\].

The use of pre-trained models for cross-modal tasks has also been explored, with models like LXMERT demonstrating the ability to learn vision-and-language connections through a combination of pre-training tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. These models have been fine-tuned to achieve state-of-the-art results on visual question answering datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. Additionally, the study of language models as unsupervised multitask learners has highlighted their ability to learn from large amounts of unlabeled data, leading to significant improvements in various NLP tasks \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9405cc0d6169988371b2755e573cc28650d14dfe)\].

### <a id='category-model-alignment-and-steering-techniques'></a>Model Alignment and Steering Techniques: 8 papers found.
The category "Model Alignment and Steering Techniques" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the controllability and ethical behavior of large language models (LLMs). Existing methods often rely on predefined templates or manual analysis to detect behaviors such as refusal, which limits their effectiveness in diverse and adversarial settings \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. Recent work has explored alternative strategies, such as steering LLMs through latent spaces by identifying and modifying specific activation directions that influence model outputs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. This approach, known as model steering, allows for fine-grained control over generated text without requiring explicit user instructions or extensive fine-tuning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. For instance, studies have shown that steering vectors can be extracted from pretrained models and used to generate target sentences with high accuracy, demonstrating the potential of latent space manipulation for controlled text generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. Additionally, researchers have investigated the use of cosine similarity metrics to identify viable steering directions and target layers, enabling automated and model-agnostic steering frameworks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.00501)\]. These methods offer advantages over traditional alignment techniques, such as prompting or fine-tuning, by providing greater flexibility and reducing dependency on specific model behaviors or output patterns \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. The development of robust steering algorithms is crucial for ensuring that LLMs can be reliably aligned with human values while maintaining performance across a wide range of tasks and alignment conditions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.00501)\].

The development of such models is supported by large, annotated datasets that provide rich visual and linguistic context, enabling precise object localization and scene understanding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. ### <a id='category-cosine-similarity-in-embedding-analysis'></a>Cosine Similarity in Embedding Analysis: 2 papers found.
Cosine similarity has been widely utilized in embedding analysis to quantify semantic similarity between high-dimensional objects, particularly in natural language processing tasks such as question answering, information retrieval, and machine translation, as well as in metrics like BERTScore \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05092)\]. This approach, however, is not without limitations. For instance, cosine similarity applied to contextual embeddings can systematically underestimate the similarity of frequent words with other instances of the same word or other words across contexts, even after accounting for polysemy \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05092)\]. Additionally, cosine similarity can yield arbitrary and non-unique 'similarities' in certain linear models, especially when the embeddings are derived from regularized linear models where the regularization implicitly controls the similarity \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440)\]. These issues highlight the importance of understanding the representational geometry of embeddings and the potential pitfalls of using cosine similarity without careful consideration of the underlying model architecture and training data characteristics.
