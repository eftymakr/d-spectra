## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a powerful alternative to traditional sparse retrieval methods, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to generate dense vector representations of text that can be efficiently compared using similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This approach has been extensively studied, with several works focusing on improving the quality of dense embeddings through various pre-training and fine-tuning strategies. For instance, Condenser introduces a novel Transformer architecture that pre-trains the model to condense information into dense vectors, improving retrieval performance over standard PLMs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, SimLM proposes a simple bottleneck architecture that compresses passage information into a dense vector through self-supervised pre-training, achieving substantial improvements on large-scale retrieval datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\].

The role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers has been a topic of debate. Some studies argue that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, suggesting that fine-tuning mainly adjusts neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This hypothesis was initially tested using BERT-based encoders with Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) as a representative model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, recent work has extended this analysis to other representation approaches, such as CLS token vs. mean pooling, and different backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These studies reveal that while pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance in many cases, this pattern does not universally apply—mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based models like LLaMA exhibit different behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\].

Contrastive learning has also been widely adopted to train dense retrievers, with methods such as ANCE and DANCE aiming to improve the quality of negative samples during training \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]\[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. ANCE constructs negative instances from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, resolving the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. DANCE, on the other hand, introduces a dual training objective inspired by query likelihood, leading to more uniform and discriminative embedding spaces \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of carefully designed training mechanisms in achieving high-quality dense retrievers.

Additionally, efforts have been made to adapt large language models (LLMs) for dense retrieval tasks without requiring extensive fine-tuning. Llama2Vec, for example, proposes an unsupervised adaptation method that leverages two pretext tasks—embedding-based auto-encoding and auto-regression—to initialize LLMs as effective dense retrievers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15503)\]. ### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-methods'></a>Contrastive Learning Methods: 8 papers found.
Contrastive learning methods have been widely explored to improve dense retrievers by enhancing the quality of text representations and enabling effective retrieval in embedding spaces \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These methods often focus on addressing challenges such as training data noise, the need for large batches, and the bypass effect of decoders \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. For instance, coCondenser introduces an unsupervised contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the reliance on heavy data engineering and large batch training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, some studies argue that decoding all input texts is not discriminative and that weak decoders can lead to the bypass effect, prompting the introduction of contrastive span prediction tasks to pre-train encoders more effectively \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Additionally, contrastive learning has been applied to train unsupervised dense retrievers, achieving strong performance across various retrieval settings and even outperforming traditional term-frequency methods like BM25 \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Furthermore, contrastive strategies have been used to suppress common words and highlight representative ones, leading to more discriminative representations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. Some works also emphasize the importance of diverse data augmentation practices, proposing new approaches that combine multiple sources of supervision to train generalizable dense retrievers \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. Finally, methods like Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE) and Contrastive Dual Learning for Approximate Nearest Neighbor (DANCE) have been introduced to address the mismatch between training and testing data distributions, improving the realism of negative samples and the quality of embedding spaces \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. These studies collectively highlight the potential of contrastive learning to enhance dense retrieval performance while reducing dependency on extensive fine-tuning or labeled data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]\[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\].
