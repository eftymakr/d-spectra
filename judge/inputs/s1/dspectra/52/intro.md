## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a powerful alternative to traditional sparse retrieval methods, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to generate dense vector representations of text that can be efficiently compared using similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This approach has been extensively studied, with several works focusing on improving the quality of dense embeddings through various pre-training and fine-tuning strategies. For instance, Condenser introduces a novel Transformer architecture that pre-trains the model to condense information into dense vectors, improving retrieval performance over standard PLMs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, SimLM proposes a simple bottleneck architecture that compresses passage information into a dense vector through self-supervised pre-training, achieving substantial improvements on large-scale retrieval datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\].

The role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers has been a topic of debate. Some studies argue that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, suggesting that fine-tuning mainly adjusts neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This hypothesis was initially tested using BERT-based encoders with Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) as a representative model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, recent work has extended this analysis to other representation approaches, such as CLS token vs. mean pooling, and different backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These studies reveal that while pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance in many cases, this pattern does not universally apply—mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based models like LLaMA exhibit different behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\].

Contrastive learning has also been widely adopted to train dense retrievers, with methods such as ANCE and DANCE aiming to improve the quality of negative samples during training \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]\[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. ANCE constructs negative instances from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, resolving the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. DANCE, on the other hand, introduces a dual training objective inspired by query likelihood, leading to more uniform and discriminative embedding spaces \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of carefully designed training mechanisms in achieving high-quality dense retrievers.

Additionally, efforts have been made to adapt large language models (LLMs) for dense retrieval tasks without requiring extensive fine-tuning. Llama2Vec, for example, proposes an unsupervised adaptation method that leverages two pretext tasks—embedding-based auto-encoding and auto-regression—to initialize LLMs as effective dense retrievers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15503)\].
