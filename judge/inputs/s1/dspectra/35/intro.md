## Related Works

Protein structure prediction and conformational sampling have seen significant advancements with the integration of machine learning and physical models. Traditional methods, such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, are computationally intensive and often limited in their ability to explore the conformational space of proteins \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Recent work has focused on developing data-driven generative models that can efficiently sample protein conformations while incorporating physical principles \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\].

Energy-based models (EBMs) have been proposed for predicting protein conformations at the atomic scale, trained solely on crystallized protein data \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These models aim to capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy and have been benchmarked on tasks such as rotamer recovery, achieving performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods like Rosetta \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. However, training EBMs remains challenging due to intractable optimization problems, especially when integrating physical supervision \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\].

Diffusion models have emerged as a promising approach for generating protein structures. EigenFold, for instance, uses a diffusion process to model protein structures as harmonic oscillators, enabling the generation of diverse, high-quality structures \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\]. Similarly, the folding diffusion model generates protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state toward a stable folded structure, mimicking the natural folding process \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600)\]. These models benefit from representations that alleviate the need for complex equivariant networks by leveraging inherent invariance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600)\].

The Rosetta all-atom energy function has been instrumental in protein modeling and design, incorporating physical and biological knowledge into its deep learning architecture \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/54e662430bd8445d75456846db4ca942bccad7bb)\]. While effective, Rosetta's energy function has limitations in accurately modeling certain conformational cases, such as those involving unusual loop structures or cis-Pro touch turns \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\]. This highlights the need for more robust energy functions that can be integrated with generative models \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\].

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) and score-based models have also been explored for protein structure prediction. However, training score-based models on high-resolution images remains challenging due to instability and limited resolution \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585)\]. Improvements in training procedures, such as maintaining an exponential moving average of model weights, have enabled the scaling of these models to higher resolutions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585)\].

Protein language models, such as ProGen and ProGen2, have demonstrated the ability to generate novel protein sequences with fine-grained control over structural properties \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11459)\]. These models leverage large-scale sequence data and are trained on diverse datasets, enabling the generation of sequences with favorable biophysical properties \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11459)\].
