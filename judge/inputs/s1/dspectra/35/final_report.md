## Related Works

Protein structure prediction and conformational sampling have seen significant advancements with the integration of machine learning and physical models. Traditional methods, such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, are computationally intensive and often limited in their ability to explore the conformational space of proteins \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Recent work has focused on developing data-driven generative models that can efficiently sample protein conformations while incorporating physical principles \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\].

Energy-based models (EBMs) have been proposed for predicting protein conformations at the atomic scale, trained solely on crystallized protein data \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These models aim to capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy and have been benchmarked on tasks such as rotamer recovery, achieving performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods like Rosetta \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. However, training EBMs remains challenging due to intractable optimization problems, especially when integrating physical supervision \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\].

Diffusion models have emerged as a promising approach for generating protein structures. EigenFold, for instance, uses a diffusion process to model protein structures as harmonic oscillators, enabling the generation of diverse, high-quality structures \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\]. Similarly, the folding diffusion model generates protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state toward a stable folded structure, mimicking the natural folding process \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600)\]. These models benefit from representations that alleviate the need for complex equivariant networks by leveraging inherent invariance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600)\].

The Rosetta all-atom energy function has been instrumental in protein modeling and design, incorporating physical and biological knowledge into its deep learning architecture \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/54e662430bd8445d75456846db4ca942bccad7bb)\]. While effective, Rosetta's energy function has limitations in accurately modeling certain conformational cases, such as those involving unusual loop structures or cis-Pro touch turns \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\]. This highlights the need for more robust energy functions that can be integrated with generative models \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\].

Generative adversarial networks (GANs) and score-based models have also been explored for protein structure prediction. However, training score-based models on high-resolution images remains challenging due to instability and limited resolution \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585)\]. Improvements in training procedures, such as maintaining an exponential moving average of model weights, have enabled the scaling of these models to higher resolutions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585)\].

Protein language models, such as ProGen and ProGen2, have demonstrated the ability to generate novel protein sequences with fine-grained control over structural properties \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11459)\]. These models leverage large-scale sequence data and are trained on diverse datasets, enabling the generation of sequences with favorable biophysical properties \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11459)\]. These models operate at the atomic scale and achieve performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods like the Rosetta energy function \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Additionally, high-resolution reduced models have been developed, incorporating lattice representations with detailed force fields that include statistical potentials for conformational biases, main-chain hydrogen bonds, and context-dependent side-group interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. These models, which utilize Replica Exchange Monte Carlo sampling, have shown improved accuracy over previous lattice models and are competitive with all-atom techniques in applications such as ab initio structure prediction and comparative modeling \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. Furthermore, recent advances in training EBMs have focused on improving contrastive divergence methods through techniques like gradient correction, data augmentation, and multi-scale processing, leading to better stability and performance across benchmarks \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of EBMs in bridging the gap between data-driven generative models and physically accurate protein conformation sampling.

### <a id='category-physical-energy-functions-in-protein-modeling'></a>Physical Energy Functions in Protein Modeling: 3 papers found.
Recent advances in protein modeling have focused on improving the accuracy and efficiency of structure prediction and conformation sampling, particularly through the integration of physical energy functions with data-driven approaches. Traditional methods, such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, are computationally intensive, while newer generative models, like denoising diffusion models, offer faster and more accurate predictions by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. However, these models often struggle to incorporate physical supervision effectively, as standard energy-based objectives can lead to intractable optimization problems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. To address this, Energy-based Alignment (EBA) has been introduced, which aligns generative models with feedback from physical models, allowing for more accurate and physically plausible structure generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. This approach has shown promising results in generating high-quality protein ensembles, as demonstrated on the MD ensemble benchmark \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. The integration of physical energy functions, such as those used in the Rosetta suite, has also been critical in improving the accuracy of protein modeling, particularly for complex structures and irregular loops \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. The Rosetta energy function, specifically the beta_nov15 version, has been refined to handle a broader range of biomolecules, including membrane proteins and nucleic acids, enhancing its applicability in structural biology and drug discovery \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Additionally, multiscale modeling strategies, such as the CABS coarse-grained model, have been developed to balance computational efficiency with accuracy by merging all-atom and coarse-grained representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to bridge the gap between data-driven generative models and physically accurate protein modeling, emphasizing the importance of energy functions in achieving reliable structural predictions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]\[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\].

### <a id='category-integration-of-physical-supervision-in-generative-models'></a>Integration of Physical Supervision in Generative Models: 3 papers found.
The integration of physical supervision in generative models for protein structure prediction and conformation sampling has seen significant advancements, driven by the need to overcome limitations in traditional molecular dynamics simulations and data-driven approaches. Recent work highlights the importance of incorporating physical knowledge into deep learning frameworks to improve the accuracy and efficiency of structure prediction, particularly in cases where no homologous structures are available \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. For instance, AlphaFold has demonstrated the ability to predict protein structures with atomic accuracy by leveraging multi-sequence alignments and a novel deep learning architecture that integrates biological and physical insights \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\]. Similarly, efforts to generate conformations based on backmapping from a fixed protein backbone have shown promise in maintaining conformational diversity and coupling side-chain fluctuations with global information about the protein's conformation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11459)\]. These approaches aim to address the computational bottlenecks of traditional methods by enabling rapid data acquisition and scalable physical simulation. Additionally, Boltzmann generators have been introduced to sample equilibrium states of many-body systems by learning invertible transformations between complex configurations and simpler latent space representations, allowing for one-shot generation of low-energy structures with high Boltzmann probability \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. These models combine statistical mechanics with deep learning to overcome rare event sampling challenges, offering a promising direction for future research in structural bioinformatics and drug discovery.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-protein-folding'></a>Diffusion Models in Protein Folding: 5 papers found.
Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful framework for protein folding and conformation generation, offering a flexible and tractable approach to modeling complex biological systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. These models are inspired by non-equilibrium statistical physics, where a forward diffusion process systematically destroys structure, and a reverse process restores it, enabling efficient sampling and evaluation of probabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Traditional methods like molecular dynamics (MD) simulations face challenges such as rare event sampling and long equilibration times, limiting their applicability to large-scale protein systems \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. To address these limitations, recent work has focused on integrating physical priors into diffusion models, such as force-guided networks that incorporate physical forces to guide the generation of protein conformations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. Additionally, models like EigenFold leverage harmonic oscillator systems to generate diverse and high-fidelity protein structures by modeling the conformational landscape as a cascading-resolution process \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Another approach, ConfDiff, combines data-based score models with force-guided networks to produce protein conformations that maintain equilibrium distribution accuracy while preserving structural diversity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1208.2680)\]. Furthermore, denoising diffusion probabilistic models have been applied to generate protein backbone structures by denoising from a random unfolded state toward a stable folded structure, mirroring the natural folding process \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\]. These advancements highlight the growing integration of physical principles with deep generative modeling to enhance the accuracy and biological plausibility of generated protein structures.
