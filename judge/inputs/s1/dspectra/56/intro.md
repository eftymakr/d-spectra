## Related Works

Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has attracted significant attention due to its ability to alleviate the cold-start and data sparsity problems by leveraging user behavior across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Early approaches primarily focused on single-domain sequential recommendation, which struggles with sparse user-item interactions \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08490)\]. To address this, several methods have been proposed to transfer knowledge between domains, often relying on overlapping users to propagate cross-domain information \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08490)\]. However, this approach is limited in real-world scenarios where overlapping users are scarce \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. 

To overcome the overlap dilemma, recent works have explored non-overlapping cross-domain settings. For instance, Automated Prompting for Non-Overlapping Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (NCSR) introduces a prompt learning-based framework to align domains without overlapping entities \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]. This approach leverages implicit feedback and learns domain-invariant representations to mitigate domain discrepancy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]. Similarly, the DREAM framework proposes decoupled representations to simultaneously learn single- and cross-domain user preferences, addressing the issue of inter-sequence relationships and data sparsity \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. These methods demonstrate that cross-domain alignment can be achieved even in the absence of overlapping users \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\].

Another challenge in CDSR is modeling complex transition patterns from mixed behavior sequences. Traditional methods often fail to capture the semantic relationships between items and users, leading to suboptimal recommendations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. To address this, the LLMCDSR framework integrates Large Language Models (LLMs) to enhance cross-domain sequential modeling by capturing semantic item relationships and user preferences \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e33f51c13728bedeeb54a76b398949a381d6152f)\]. LLMs are particularly effective in this context due to their strong representation and reasoning capabilities, allowing for more accurate modeling of user behavior across domains \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e33f51c13728bedeeb54a76b398949a381d6152f)\]. Additionally, the LLM4CDSR model further leverages LLMs to derive semantic item relationships and user preferences, integrating them into a tri-thread framework for improved recommendation performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\].

The use of LLMs in recommendation systems has also been explored in other contexts. For example, WizardLM demonstrates that AI-evolved instructions can be used to fine-tune LLMs for complex tasks, achieving performance comparable to human-created instructions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. Similarly, LLM-Rec proposes a domain-agnostic recommendation system by utilizing pre-trained LLMs to generate semantically rich representations of users and items \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14304)\]. These studies highlight the potential of LLMs in enhancing recommendation accuracy by capturing semantic and contextual information \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]\[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14304)\].
