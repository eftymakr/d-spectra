## Related Works

Prosody modeling in text-to-speech (TTS) has been a central research focus, with numerous approaches aiming to improve the naturalness and controllability of synthesized speech. Early methods often relied on handcrafted rules or statistical models to estimate pitch, duration, and energy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. However, these approaches were limited in their ability to capture the complex dependencies between linguistic features and prosody \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. More recently, deep learning has enabled end-to-end TTS systems that implicitly model prosody through acoustic features \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07654)\]. Despite these advancements, controlling prosody remains challenging, especially when no explicit prosody labels are available in the training data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\].

Several works have attempted to enhance prosody modeling by incorporating pre-trained language models or latent representations. For instance, BERT embeddings have been used to encode semantic and syntactic information, which helps TTS systems generate more natural prosody \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\]. Similarly, quantized latent vectors pre-trained on large-scale unpaired data have been proposed to improve prosody expressiveness \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08947)\]. These methods often rely on additional training or specialized modules, which can limit their flexibility for post-hoc adjustments \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\].

Non-autoregressive TTS models have also gained attention for their efficiency and parallelizability. For example, Parallel Tacotron uses a variational autoencoder to relax the one-to-many mapping problem and improve naturalness \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08969)\]. Similarly, FastSpeech and its variants have been developed to generate speech in parallel, reducing inference time while maintaining quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. However, these models often struggle with long-range dependencies and require careful design to ensure prosody consistency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03575)\].

Mispronunciation correction in TTS has traditionally relied on grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) dictionaries, which are not practical in low-resource settings \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05161)\]. Recent approaches have explored end-to-end mispronunciation detection and diagnosis using attention mechanisms and data augmentation techniques \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. These methods aim to improve the robustness of TTS systems by aligning audio with phoneme sequences and detecting mispronunciations during inference \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03788)\].

The challenge of post-hoc control of prosody and pronunciation has motivated the development of model-agnostic methods that manipulate internal representations of pre-trained TTS models. Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Many existing approaches rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. For instance, some methods use explicit prosody modeling, such as hierarchical architectures that condition phoneme-level features on word-level prosody \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\], or introduce word-level prosody encoders that quantize speech and compress attributes into latent vectors \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. Additionally, non-autoregressive models have been proposed to enable faster inference and better control over prosody dimensions like pitch, duration, and energy \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. These methods often require large-scale data or explicit prosody annotations, which may not always be available \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05161)\]. To address these limitations, some works explore fully automatic prosody control, such as introducing augmented attention mechanisms for better pace control \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07654)\] or using variational auto-encoders to train duration predictors in a semi-supervised manner \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08969)\]. Others focus on improving the expressiveness of synthesized speech by incorporating emotional and stylistic variations through prosody embeddings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Despite these advancements, there remains a gap in enabling post-hoc adjustments without retraining, which is where methods like Counterfactual Activation Editing aim to bridge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\].

### <a id='category-latent-representation-methods-for-prosody-control'></a>Latent Representation Methods for Prosody Control: 2 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) synthesis have enabled highly natural speech generation, but precise prosody control remains a challenge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Existing methods often rely on specialized modules or additional training, limiting their flexibility for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. In contrast, latent representation methods for prosody control, such as those using variational autoencoders (VAEs) with fine-grained latent features, allow for more nuanced manipulation of speech characteristics \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03788)\]. These models typically extract latent features at each input token, such as phonemes, but may produce unnatural speech due to discontinuities between tokens \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03788)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed sequential priors in discrete latent spaces, leveraging vector quantization (VQ) and autoregressive (AR) prior models to generate more natural speech samples \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03788)\]. Additionally, sequence-to-sequence models conditioned on acoustic speech features, such as pitch, duration, and energy, have been developed to learn a latent prosody space that enables intuitive control over speaking styles \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of latent representations to enhance both prosody control and speech quality, aligning with the broader goal of improving TTS systems for real-world applications.
