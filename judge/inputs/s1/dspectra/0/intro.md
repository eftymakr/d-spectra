## Related Works

Economic inequality remains a critical challenge, influencing education, healthcare, and social stability globally \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Traditional systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, aim to reduce inequality but lack adaptability to dynamic economic conditions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Recent research has explored more flexible approaches, such as the Saez Optimal Taxation model, which dynamically adjusts tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. However, these models often fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior, limiting their effectiveness in real-world applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This study introduces TaxAgent, a novel integration of large language models (LLMs) with agent-based modeling (ABM) to design adaptive tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. 

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has been widely used to simulate complex economic systems, including the behavior of heterogeneous agents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615)\]. ABMs allow for the exploration of emergent phenomena and the evaluation of policy interventions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615)\]. However, traditional ABMs often rely on heuristic decision-making rules or regressions trained on past data, which may not capture the full complexity of human behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.04615)\]. Recent work has explored the use of reinforcement learning (RL) to improve the validity of agent behavioral models in ABMs \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. RL models have been shown to produce reward-seeking or reward-maximizing behaviors in ABM agents, outperforming default adaptive behavioral models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. 

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable abilities to solve new tasks from a few examples or textual instructions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. However, they often struggle with basic functionalities such as arithmetic or factual lookup, where smaller models excel \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. To address this, researchers have developed methods that enable LLMs to use external tools via APIs, achieving the best of both worlds \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. This includes models like Toolformer, which can decide which APIs to call, when to call them, and how to incorporate results into future predictions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. These advancements highlight the potential of LLMs to enhance the capabilities of ABMs by providing more accurate and adaptable behavioral models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\].

In addition, the use of LLMs for policy analysis has gained traction, with approaches like the Multi-LLM-Agent-Based (MLAB) framework leveraging multiple LLMs as heterogeneous economic agents \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16879)\]. This framework maps different LLMs to specific educational groups and income brackets, enabling the simulation of policy impacts across diverse agents \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16879)\]. Such approaches demonstrate the potential of LLMs to contribute to economic and public policy analysis by modeling complex human behaviors and interactions \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16879)\].
