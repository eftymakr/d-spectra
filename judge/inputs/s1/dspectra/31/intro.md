## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning rich representations from unlabeled data. This approach has been widely applied in computer vision, where techniques such as predicting image rotations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\] and solving jigsaw puzzles \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\] have demonstrated significant success. These methods leverage self-supervised tasks to learn features that capture semantic information without requiring manual annotations. Similarly, in the field of natural language processing, contrastive predictive coding has been used to learn representations from high-dimensional data by predicting future samples in latent space \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. This approach has shown strong performance across multiple domains, including speech, images, and text.

In the context of genomics, recent work has explored the use of self-supervised learning to pre-train models on DNA sequences. For instance, DNABERT \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c43d9cade31600400a0f62beb5bbcc1b548e009e)\] leverages bidirectional transformers to learn representations from DNA sequences by considering both upstream and downstream nucleotide contexts. This model has achieved state-of-the-art performance on various sequence prediction tasks. Similarly, the work on Enformer \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e12e837cb2e9baeaefdcab06fe1c75add8f46389)\] demonstrated that deep learning architectures capable of integrating long-range interactions can significantly improve gene expression prediction accuracy. These models highlight the potential of self-supervised learning in genomics, particularly when dealing with large-scale, unlabeled datasets.

However, the paper argues that pure DNA sequence pre-training may be insufficient due to the lack of information about genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility. This limitation has led researchers to explore supervised training for genomic profile prediction as a more effective alternative. For example, the work on multi-modal self-supervised pre-training \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05231)\] introduced a method that simultaneously considers 1D genome sequences and 2D matrices of transcription factor interactions, enabling the model to better capture the complex regulatory mechanisms across different cell types.

Furthermore, the paper introduces the SPACE model, which leverages Mixture of Experts (MoE) to adapt to the multi-species and multi-profile nature of genomic data. This approach is inspired by prior work on domain-adversarial training of neural networks \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\], which aims to learn representations that are invariant to domain shifts. The MoE framework allows the model to dynamically select the most relevant experts for each input, thereby improving the generalization and adaptability of the learned representations.

In addition, the paper draws on the success of self-supervised contrastive pre-training in other domains, such as dense land cover classification \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\], where models trained on large satellite imagery datasets outperformed random initialization. These findings suggest that self-supervised learning can be a valuable tool
