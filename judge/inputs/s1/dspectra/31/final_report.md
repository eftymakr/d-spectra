## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning rich representations from unlabeled data. This approach has been widely applied in computer vision, where techniques such as predicting image rotations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\] and solving jigsaw puzzles \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\] have demonstrated significant success. These methods leverage self-supervised tasks to learn features that capture semantic information without requiring manual annotations. Similarly, in the field of natural language processing, contrastive predictive coding has been used to learn representations from high-dimensional data by predicting future samples in latent space \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. This approach has shown strong performance across multiple domains, including speech, images, and text.

In the context of genomics, recent work has explored the use of self-supervised learning to pre-train models on DNA sequences. For instance, DNABERT \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c43d9cade31600400a0f62beb5bbcc1b548e009e)\] leverages bidirectional transformers to learn representations from DNA sequences by considering both upstream and downstream nucleotide contexts. This model has achieved state-of-the-art performance on various sequence prediction tasks. Similarly, the work on Enformer \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e12e837cb2e9baeaefdcab06fe1c75add8f46389)\] demonstrated that deep learning architectures capable of integrating long-range interactions can significantly improve gene expression prediction accuracy. These models highlight the potential of self-supervised learning in genomics, particularly when dealing with large-scale, unlabeled datasets.

However, the paper argues that pure DNA sequence pre-training may be insufficient due to the lack of information about genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility. This limitation has led researchers to explore supervised training for genomic profile prediction as a more effective alternative. For example, the work on multi-modal self-supervised pre-training \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05231)\] introduced a method that simultaneously considers 1D genome sequences and 2D matrices of transcription factor interactions, enabling the model to better capture the complex regulatory mechanisms across different cell types.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-for-representation-learning'></a>Contrastive Learning for Representation Learning: 9 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for representation learning, enabling models to learn meaningful features without explicit supervision. This approach leverages self-supervised tasks, such as predicting spatial relationships between image patches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\], or learning to recognize 2D rotations applied to images \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09005)\], to capture rich visual representations. These methods often rely on contrastive losses, such as InfoNCE, to maximize the distinction between similar and dissimilar data points \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. Additionally, contrastive predictive coding has been extended to various domains, including speech, images, text, and reinforcement learning, by using probabilistic models to predict future samples in latent space \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. The success of these methods is further demonstrated in tasks like land cover segmentation, where self-supervised contrastive pre-training significantly improves performance, especially when ground truth data are scarce \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c43d9cade31600400a0f62beb5bbcc1b548e009e)\]. Moreover, recent work has focused on improving data efficiency, such as through non-parametric classification and noise-contrastive estimation, which enables models to learn representations that generalize across different regions and tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility and effectiveness of contrastive learning in capturing meaningful and transferable features across diverse modalities and applications.

In the context of genomic modeling, Mixture of Experts (MoE) has emerged as a powerful approach to handle the complexity of large-scale genomic data. Unlike traditional deep learning models that reuse the same parameters for all inputs, MoE selects different parameters for each incoming example, leading to a sparsely-activated model with an enormous number of parameters but a constant computational cost \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]. This approach has been further refined with the development of the Switch Transformer, which simplifies the MoE routing algorithm and reduces communication and computational costs \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]. The Switch Transformer also introduces training techniques that address the instability associated with training large sparse models, enabling the use of lower precision (bfloat16) formats for the first time \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]. These advancements have led to significant improvements in pre-training speed, with models based on T5-Base and T5-Large achieving up to 7x speedups with the same computational resources \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]. Furthermore, these models have been extended to multilingual settings, demonstrating gains across 101 languages \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\]. The application of MoE in genomic modeling, such as in the SPACE framework, highlights its potential to better capture the relationships between DNA sequences across different species and genomic profiles, thereby learning more effective DNA representations \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03961)\].

[Snippet]: «The prediction of phenotypic traits using high-density genomic data has many applications such as the selection of plants and animals of commercial interest; and it is expected to play an increasing role in medical diagnostics. Statistical models used for this task are usually tested using cross-validation, which implicitly assumes that new individuals (whose phenotypes we would like to predict) originate from the same population the genomic prediction model is trained on. In this paper we propose an approach based on clustering and resampling to investigate the effect of increasing genetic distance between training and target populations when predicting quantitative traits. This is important for plant and animal genetics, where genomic selection programs rely on the precision of predictions in future rounds of breeding. Therefore, estimating how quickly predictive accuracy decays is important in deciding which training population to use and how often the model has to be recalibrated. We find that the correlation between true and predicted values decays approximately linearly with respect to either FST or mean kinship between the training and the target populations. We illustrate this relationship using simulations and a collection of data sets from mice, wheat and human genetics.»
