## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area in natural language processing (NLP), aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. This task is particularly relevant for applications requiring fine-grained control over text representation, such as information retrieval, semantic similarity, and document clustering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. Prior work has explored various approaches to achieve this goal, but most methods suffer from significant computational overhead due to the need to re-encode the entire corpus for each new instruction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\].

Early efforts in text embedding focused on unsupervised methods, such as Skip-gram and Paragraph Vector, which learn distributed representations of words and phrases \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4546)\]. These methods, while effective for general-purpose tasks, lack the ability to adapt to specific instructions without retraining \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4546)\]. More recently, supervised approaches have been proposed to improve the quality of text embeddings by leveraging labeled data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.02364)\]. For example, the work of \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.02364)\] demonstrated that sentence representations trained using the Stanford Natural Language Inference datasets outperform unsupervised methods like SkipThought vectors on a wide range of transfer tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.02364)\].

Instruction-tuned models have also gained traction, particularly in the context of large language models (LLMs). The Flan Collection \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13688)\] introduced a set of design decisions for effective instruction tuning, emphasizing the importance of task balancing and enrichment techniques \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13688)\]. Similarly, the work of \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03277)\] showed that generating instruction-following data using GPT-4 can lead to superior zero-shot performance on new tasks compared to previous state-of-the-art methods \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03277)\]. This approach aligns with the idea of using machine-generated instruction data to reduce the reliance on human-labeled datasets \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03277)\].

Another notable contribution is the INSTRUCTOR model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\], which introduces a single embedder that can generate text embeddings tailored to different downstream tasks and domains without further training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. This model is trained on a multitask mixture with a contrastive loss and achieves state-of-the-art performance across 70 embedding evaluation tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. The success of INSTRUCTOR highlights the potential of instruction-tuned models to generalize across diverse tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\].

In addition to instruction-tuned models, there has been growing interest in lightweight transformation mechanisms that can adapt pre-computed embeddings in real time \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. The work of \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\] introduced InBedder, which treats instructions as questions about the input text and encodes the expected answers to obtain the representation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. This approach improves instruction-following capabilities by aligning embeddings with user instructions
