## Related Works

Deep-unrolling and plug-and-play (PnP) approaches have become the de-facto standard solvers for single-pixel imaging (SPI) inverse problems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13256)\]. PnP methods, a class of iterative algorithms where regularization is implicitly performed by an off-the-shelf deep denoiser, are flexible for varying compression ratios (CRs) but are limited in reconstruction accuracy and speed \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13256)\]. This limitation is due to the non-convex nature of the denoiser and the lack of theoretical guarantees for convergence \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03668)\]. In contrast, unrolling approaches, a class of multi-stage neural networks where a truncated iterative optimization process is transformed into an end-to-end trainable network, typically achieve better accuracy with faster inference but require fine-tuning or even retraining when CR changes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13256)\]. This is because unrolling networks are trained for specific CRs and may not generalize well to other scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10557)\].

Several works have explored the use of PnP methods for image restoration. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08616)\] showed that PnP methods can be used for MRI image recovery by iteratively calling a denoising subroutine. Similarly, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13247)\] proposed a new paradigm for solving regularized variational problems by replacing the operator related to the regularization with a more sophisticated denoiser. However, these methods often rely on assumptions about the denoiser, such as nonexpansiveness, which may not hold in practice \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3013361190851e886398543caa68534578593fb3)\].

This approach allows the network to handle varying CRs without requiring retraining \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b954a94af132294a4a4e2efce15027758a535742)\].

### <a id='category-regularization-and-denoiser-assumptions'></a>Regularization and Denoiser Assumptions: 17 papers found.
Plug-and-Play (PnP) methods have emerged as a powerful framework for solving image inverse problems by integrating deep neural networks as denoisers within iterative algorithms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13256)\]. These methods replace traditional proximal operators with denoising operations, enabling the use of advanced pre-trained denoisers to enhance restoration performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03668)\]. However, the theoretical convergence of PnP algorithms has been a challenge, especially when using nonexpansive or non-convex denoisers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04693)\]. Recent work has addressed this by showing that gradient-based denoisers can be interpreted as proximal operators of a scalar function, allowing the application of convergence theories for nonconvex settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13256)\]. This has led to the convergence of PnP-PGD and PnP-ADMM algorithms when built on smooth gradient denoisers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.13256)\]. Additionally, the use of scene-adapted denoisers within the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) has been proposed to ensure convergence while maintaining state-of-the-art performance in tasks like image fusion \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08616)\]. The integration of deep neural networks into PnP frameworks has also been explored for MRI image recovery, where PnP methods iteratively apply denoisers to solve the linearly approximated inverse problem \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10557)\]. Furthermore, the design of denoisers that align with the observation model has been emphasized to improve the performance of image restoration tasks such as denoising, super-resolution, and deblurring \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13247)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of PnP methods in leveraging deep learning for robust and flexible image restoration, while addressing theoretical challenges related to convergence and optimality \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01779)\].

### <a id='category-pnp-methods-and-their-limitations'></a>PnP Methods and Their Limitations: 4 papers found.
Plug-and-play (PnP) methods have emerged as a powerful framework for solving inverse imaging problems by integrating proximal algorithms with deep learning-based denoisers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09611)\]. These non-convex approaches, such as those using ADMM or Douglas–Rachford splitting, leverage pre-trained denoisers to implicitly enforce regularization, leading to significant empirical success, particularly in applications like compressed sensing MRI and phase retrieval \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09611)\]. However, a critical limitation of PnP methods is the reliance on manual parameter tuning, which complicates their application across diverse imaging conditions and scene contents \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09611)\]. To address this, recent work has introduced tuning-free PnP algorithms that automatically determine internal parameters such as penalty values, denoising strength, and termination time through policy networks trained with mixed model-free and model-based reinforcement learning \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09611)\]. This approach enables the customization of parameters for different states, often outperforming handcrafted criteria in efficiency and effectiveness \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09611)\]. Additionally, convergence analysis of PnP algorithms has been advanced, with new proofs that relax restrictive stepsize conditions, allowing broader regularization parameter ranges and improved restoration accuracy \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01216)\]. Furthermore, stochastic variants of PnP methods, such as stochastic PnP-ADMM, have been proposed to reduce computational costs by incorporating inner-loop stochastic gradient descent, while maintaining theoretical guarantees for fixed-point convergence \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11630)\]. These developments highlight ongoing efforts to enhance the flexibility, accuracy, and practicality of PnP methods in a variety of imaging tasks.
