## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural models that directly generate document identifiers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. This approach leverages the power of large language models to encode both the query and the document corpus, enabling seamless integration with generative tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. Prior work has explored various aspects of this paradigm, including scalability, efficiency, and the effectiveness of different decoding strategies.

Several studies have investigated the use of generative retrieval for knowledge-intensive tasks, such as open-domain question answering and document retrieval. For instance, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\] demonstrated that dense representations can outperform traditional sparse methods like BM25, achieving significant improvements in passage retrieval accuracy. Similarly, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03057)\] introduced a method called GLEN, which uses lexical index learning to generate document identifiers, achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks. These works highlight the potential of generative retrieval to replace traditional indexing structures with learned models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\].

The scalability of generative retrieval to large corpora has also been a focus of recent research. \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\] conducted the first empirical study of generative retrieval techniques across various corpus scales, scaling up to 8.8M passages and evaluating model sizes up to 11B parameters. They found that while generative retrieval is competitive with state-of-the-art dual encoders on small corpora, scaling to millions of passages remains a challenge due to issues such as the need for synthetic queries during indexing and the limitations of naive parameter scaling \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\] further advanced this area by proposing RIPOR, an optimization framework that improves the performance of generative retrieval models on large-scale benchmarks, achieving significant improvements in MRR scores \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\].

In addition to scalability, the decoding strategies used in generative retrieval have been scrutinized. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\] introduced PAG, a novel optimization and decoding approach that guides autoregressive generation of document identifiers through simultaneous decoding. This method constructs both set-based and sequential identifiers for documents, leading to substantial improvements in retrieval performance and query latency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\]. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18409)\] extended this idea by proposing GR$^2$, a framework that addresses the limitations of binary relevance data by incorporating multi-graded relevance signals, enabling more nuanced retrieval \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18409)\].

The theoretical underpinnings of generative retrieval have also been explored. \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\] examined the generalization capabilities of generative retrieval on out-of-distribution corpora, highlighting the importance of constraints and beam search in the decoding process. ### <a id='category-optimization-frameworks-for-retrieval'></a>Optimization Frameworks for Retrieval: 4 papers found.
Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in text retrieval, shifting from traditional sparse or dense retrieval methods by leveraging powerful generative language models to directly generate document identifiers (docids) as the retrieval target \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. This approach enables seamless integration with large language models and offers potential improvements in efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. However, existing generative retrieval models face challenges in scaling to large-scale benchmarks and handling dynamic document collections \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Recent work has addressed these limitations by introducing optimization frameworks such as LTRGR, which bridges the learning gap between autoregressive models and passage ranking by incorporating a rank loss during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. Similarly, RIPOR has advanced the field by introducing a prefix-oriented ranking optimization algorithm and constructing document IDs based on relevance associations between queries and documents, achieving significant performance improvements on standard benchmarks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Additionally, the CLEVER model tackles the continual learning challenge by enabling incremental indexing of new documents while preserving query accuracy over time \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Furthermore, GR$^2$ extends generative retrieval to handle multi-graded relevance by ensuring semantically relevant and distinct identifiers through joint optimization and implementing constrained contrastive training based on relevance grades \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to refine generative retrieval frameworks, enhance their scalability, and improve their generalization capabilities across diverse and dynamic retrieval scenarios.

### <a id='category-state-of-the-art-retrieval-models'></a>State-of-the-Art Retrieval Models: 4 papers found.
State-of-the-art retrieval models have seen significant advancements, particularly with the adoption of dense retrievers and contrastive learning techniques. These models leverage neural networks to replace traditional sparse methods like BM25, offering improved performance on large-scale datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Recent research highlights the effectiveness of fine-tuned language models for dense retrieval, although they face challenges such as sensitivity to training data noise and the need for large batches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. To address these issues, approaches like coCondenser have been proposed, which utilize unsupervised contrastive losses to enhance passage embedding spaces without requiring extensive data engineering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. Similarly, contrastive learning has been explored to train unsupervised dense retrievers, achieving strong performance across various retrieval settings and even demonstrating cross-lingual transfer capabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Additionally, generative retrieval has emerged as a new paradigm, aiming to directly generate document identifiers through neural networks, bypassing traditional index structures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. This approach, however, faces challenges related to constraints and beam search, which have been analyzed to improve theoretical understanding of its generalization capabilities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Overall, these developments reflect a shift towards more flexible and efficient retrieval methods, driven by advances in pre-training architectures and learning strategies.
