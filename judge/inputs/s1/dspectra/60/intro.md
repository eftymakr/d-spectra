## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been extensively studied, with a focus on improving the alignment between predicted probabilities and actual correctness. Early work on neural network calibration highlighted the poor calibration of modern models compared to older architectures, attributing this to factors such as depth, width, and Batch Normalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue is particularly critical in safety-critical applications where reliable uncertainty estimates are essential \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02530)\]. 

Several approaches have been proposed to address this challenge. Temperature scaling, a simple post-hoc calibration method, has been shown to effectively improve calibration across various architectures and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. More recently, focal loss has been introduced as a training objective that inherently promotes better calibration while maintaining high accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. This method, when combined with temperature scaling, achieves state-of-the-art results without compromising performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. 

The need for flexible and expressive calibration methods has also been emphasized. Traditional calibration techniques often rely on fixed forms, such as piece-wise linear functions, which limit their ability to model complex calibration landscapes \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00104)\]. To overcome this, researchers have explored deep ensemble methods, which provide high-quality predictive uncertainty estimates by leveraging multiple models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. These methods are particularly effective in capturing uncertainty in out-of-distribution scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. 

Another line of work focuses on improving calibration through training objectives that directly incorporate calibration constraints. For example, the Soft Calibration Objectives for Neural Networks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00106)\] propose differentiable losses based on a continuous version of binning, which achieve state-of-the-art results with minimal accuracy loss. Similarly, the work on Mixup training \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\] shows that training with label smoothing and feature mixing leads to better-calibrated models, particularly in out-of-distribution settings. 

The importance of monotonicity in calibration has also been explored. The Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Network (UMNN) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05164)\] is designed to learn arbitrary monotonic functions, offering greater flexibility compared to traditional calibration methods. This approach ensures that the calibration function preserves the relative order of predictions while avoiding excessive distortion. 

Recent studies have also investigated the effectiveness of post-hoc calibration methods in large-scale settings. For example, the work on Deep Ensembles \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\] demonstrates that ensembles of models can produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, which are as good or better than Bayesian methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. Additionally, the paper on Consistency Calibration \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12295)\] introduces a post-hoc method that leverages the consistency of predictions across perturbed inputs to improve calibration, particularly in scenarios where additional data is not available.
