## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been extensively studied, with a focus on improving the alignment between predicted probabilities and actual correctness. Early work on neural network calibration highlighted the poor calibration of modern models compared to older architectures, attributing this to factors such as depth, width, and Batch Normalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue is particularly critical in safety-critical applications where reliable uncertainty estimates are essential \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02530)\]. 

Several approaches have been proposed to address this challenge. Temperature scaling, a simple post-hoc calibration method, has been shown to effectively improve calibration across various architectures and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. More recently, focal loss has been introduced as a training objective that inherently promotes better calibration while maintaining high accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. This method, when combined with temperature scaling, achieves state-of-the-art results without compromising performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. 

The need for flexible and expressive calibration methods has also been emphasized. Traditional calibration techniques often rely on fixed forms, such as piece-wise linear functions, which limit their ability to model complex calibration landscapes \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00104)\]. To overcome this, researchers have explored deep ensemble methods, which provide high-quality predictive uncertainty estimates by leveraging multiple models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. These methods are particularly effective in capturing uncertainty in out-of-distribution scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. 

Another line of work focuses on improving calibration through training objectives that directly incorporate calibration constraints. For example, the Soft Calibration Objectives for Neural Networks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00106)\] propose differentiable losses based on a continuous version of binning, which achieve state-of-the-art results with minimal accuracy loss. Similarly, the work on Mixup training \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\] shows that training with label smoothing and feature mixing leads to better-calibrated models, particularly in out-of-distribution settings. 

The importance of monotonicity in calibration has also been explored. The Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Network (UMNN) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05164)\] is designed to learn arbitrary monotonic functions, offering greater flexibility compared to traditional calibration methods. This approach ensures that the calibration function preserves the relative order of predictions while avoiding excessive distortion. 

Recent studies have also investigated the effectiveness of post-hoc calibration methods in large-scale settings. For example, the work on Deep Ensembles \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\] demonstrates that ensembles of models can produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, which are as good or better than Bayesian methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. Additionally, the paper on Consistency Calibration \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12295)\] introduces a post-hoc method that leverages the consistency of predictions across perturbed inputs to improve calibration, particularly in scenarios where additional data is not available.

### <a id='category-calibration-of-deep-learning-models'></a>Calibration of deep learning models: 12 papers found.
The calibration of deep learning models is a critical area of research, particularly in applications where accurate uncertainty estimation is essential for reliable decision-making. Recent studies have highlighted that modern neural networks, despite their high accuracy, often exhibit poor calibration, meaning their predicted probabilities do not accurately reflect the true likelihood of correct predictions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue is exacerbated by factors such as model depth, width, and the use of Batch Normalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. To address this, various post-processing techniques have been proposed, including temperature scaling, which has been shown to be surprisingly effective in calibrating predictions across multiple datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. Additionally, methods like the AvUC loss function have been introduced to improve uncertainty calibration by leveraging the relationship between accuracy and uncertainty \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. The importance of accurate calibration is further underscored in safety-critical applications, where models must not only be accurate but also indicate high uncertainty when predictions are likely to be incorrect \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. Researchers have also explored the use of Bayesian methods to quantify uncertainty, though these often suffer from inaccuracies due to model misspecification and approximate inference \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\]. To mitigate these issues, new approaches such as spline-based recalibration have been developed, offering improvements over existing methods by using a binning-free calibration measure inspired by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01685)\]. Furthermore, the challenge of measuring calibration error has been addressed by evaluating different calibration measures, including adaptive binning schemes and the use of the L2 norm, which have been found to improve both optimization and metric consistency \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07923)\]. In the context of ranking models, the need for scale calibration has been recognized, as existing methods often fail to account for the absolute values of predictions, which are crucial for downstream tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05164)\]. To address this, new formulations have been proposed to calibrate ranking models during training, ensuring both ranking quality and score scale calibration \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05164)\]. Overall, the field has seen significant advancements in both theoretical understanding and practical calibration techniques, with a growing emphasis on flexibility, expressiveness, and data efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02530)\].

[Snippet]: «Calibration is crucial in deep learning applications, especially in fields like healthcare and autonomous driving, where accurate confidence estimates are vital for decision-making. However, deep neural networks often suffer from miscalibration, with reliability diagrams and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) being the only standard perspective for evaluating calibration performance. In this paper, we introduce the concept of consistency as an alternative perspective on model calibration, inspired by uncertainty estimation literature in large language models (LLMs). We highlight its advantages over the traditional reliability-based view. Building on this concept, we propose a post-hoc calibration method called Consistency Calibration (CC), which adjusts confidence based on the model's consistency across perturbed inputs. CC is particularly effective in locally uncertainty estimation, as it requires no additional data samples or label information, instead generating input perturbations directly from the source data. Moreover, we show that performing perturbations at the logit level significantly improves computational efficiency. We validate the effectiveness of CC through extensive comparisons with various post-hoc and training-time calibration methods, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on standard datasets such as CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet, as well as on long-tailed datasets like ImageNet-LT.»
