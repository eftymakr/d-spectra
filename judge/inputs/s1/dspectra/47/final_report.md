## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to improve recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge transfer across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Traditional MDR methods often assume a shared structure for transferring domain knowledge, which can lead to suboptimal results when the transferable information varies across domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. This issue is particularly pronounced in the presence of knowledge conflict or poor-quality domains, where unselective knowledge transfer can result in the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Several studies have attempted to address these challenges by introducing more sophisticated modeling techniques.

Early work in cross-domain recommendation (CDR) focused on transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain to alleviate data sparsity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. However, these methods typically assumed a single-target scenario, limiting their applicability to real-world settings where multiple domains coexist \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. More recent approaches, such as Dual-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation (DTCDR), have sought to improve performance on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously by leveraging multi-source content and adaptable embedding-sharing strategies \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. This aligns with the growing need for models that can handle multiple domains effectively \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\].

The challenge of domain-specific knowledge transfer has also been addressed in the context of Multi-Domain Sequential Recommendation (MDSR), where models attempt to capture domain-specific sequential preferences while also leveraging shared sequential patterns \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\]. However, these methods often struggle with accurately modeling domain-specific preferences when user interaction with a domain is limited \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed techniques such as Domain Space Learning, which uses cross-domain items to supplement missing sequential context \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\].

In the realm of multi-domain recommendation, the problem of negative transfer has been further explored using cooperative game theory, where the extent of negative transfer is assessed based on the marginal contribution of each domain to model performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. This approach enables the adaptive assignment of weights to prediction losses, thereby reducing the impact of negative transfer \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Additionally, methods such as HAMUR have been introduced to address parameter sharing limitations in multi-domain models by incorporating domain-specific adapters and domain-shared hyper-networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\].

The need for dynamic and selective domain knowledge transfer has also been recognized in the development of the Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP), which aims to dynamically select appropriate domains based on domain-level gaps and unsupervised distance measures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where domain quality varies and knowledge conflict is likely \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. ### <a id='category-cross-domain-recommendation-(cdr)-and-its-limitations'></a>Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR) and Its Limitations: 7 papers found.
Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR) has been widely studied to address data sparsity in recommender systems by leveraging information from richer domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. However, existing methods often assume at least one rich domain and rely heavily on domain-invariant preferences, which may not hold in real-world scenarios where sparsity is common \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. To overcome these limitations, several approaches have been proposed. For instance, MSDCR treats multiple domains as sparse and enhances domain-specific preferences through domain-specific and domain-invariant aspects \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Similarly, DTCDR improves performance on both richer and sparser domains by utilizing multi-source content and an adaptable embedding-sharing strategy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Additionally, DDTCDR introduces a dual learning mechanism to iteratively transfer information between domains, preserving user-item relations and improving recommendation accuracy \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. AutoTransfer addresses the challenge of transferring information by selectively transferring instances from the source domain, enhancing future model training \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Furthermore, PPGN tackles cross-domain sparsity by modeling interactions across domains through a unified multi-task framework, capturing high-order information propagation and simplifying optimization \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03357)\]. These methods highlight the evolving approaches in CDR, focusing on dynamic domain selection, transfer efficiency, and comprehensive modeling of cross-domain relationships.
