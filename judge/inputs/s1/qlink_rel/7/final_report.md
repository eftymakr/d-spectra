## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and express various behaviors, including refusal, within their activation spaces \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. Traditional methods for detecting such behaviors often rely on predefined templates or manual analysis, which can be limiting in their applicability and effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. Recent research has explored the use of activation space interventions to steer LLMs toward desired behaviors, such as improving truthfulness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\]. These methods typically require fine-tuning or manual prompt engineering, which can be resource-intensive and less flexible \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\].

The concept of steering LLMs using activation space modifications has gained traction, with approaches such as Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) demonstrating the ability to alter model behavior by adding steering vectors during inference \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. CAA leverages differences in residual stream activations between positive and negative examples to guide the model toward specific behaviors, such as reducing hallucinations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. This method is effective over traditional methods like fine-tuning and system prompt design, and it minimally impacts the model's general capabilities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\].

Another line of research focuses on the use of cosine similarity to measure semantic similarity between embeddings, which is crucial for tasks such as retrieval and classification \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440)\]. However, the use of cosine similarity can sometimes yield arbitrary or meaningless results, especially when the embeddings are derived from models with implicit regularization \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440)\]. This has led to a caution against its blind use and the exploration of alternative similarity measures \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440)\].

The idea of using pretrained models for multimodal tasks has also been explored, with models like Flamingo demonstrating the ability to perform few-shot learning across various visual and textual tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. Flamingo bridges vision-only and language-only models, enabling the handling of interleaved visual and textual data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. This capability is further extended in models like Video-LLaMA, which integrates audio-visual signals for video understanding \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\].

The importance of alignment in multimodal models has also been highlighted, with approaches such as Factually Augmented RLHF aiming to reduce hallucinations by incorporating factual information into the reward model \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14525)\]. This method improves the alignment between vision and language modalities, enhancing the model's ability to generate accurate and grounded responses \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14525)\].

In summary, the field of steering and aligning LLMs has seen significant advancements, with methods ranging from activation space interventions to multimodal alignment techniques. ### <a id='category-activation-space-interventions-for-steering'></a>Activation Space Interventions for Steering: 5 papers found.
Activation space interventions for steering have emerged as a promising approach to control language models without requiring fine-tuning or extensive retraining. Researchers have explored various methods to manipulate model behavior by modifying activations during inference, leveraging the latent representations encoded within pre-trained models. For instance, prior work has demonstrated that steering vectors can be extracted from pretrained language model decoders to generate target sentences with high accuracy, achieving BLEU scores over 99 for English sentences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that refusal behavior in chat models can be mediated by a one-dimensional subspace, and manipulating this direction can either suppress or elicit refusal responses, offering insights into safety mechanisms \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05124)\]. Another approach, Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA), uses steering vectors derived from residual stream activations to steer model outputs toward desired behaviors, such as factual responses, with minimal impact on other capabilities \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03744)\]. Similarly, Inference-Time Intervention (ITI) enhances truthfulness by shifting activations during inference, improving performance on benchmarks like TruthfulQA without requiring additional training data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Adaptive Activation Steering (ACT) further builds on these ideas by introducing a tuning-free method that adaptively shifts activations in the "truthful" direction, effectively addressing hallucinations across multiple models and scales \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. These techniques collectively highlight the potential of activation space interventions to control model behavior while maintaining performance and minimizing side effects.

[Snippet]: «Cosine-similarity is the cosine of the angle between two vectors, or equivalently the dot product between their normalizations. A popular application is to quantify semantic similarity between high-dimensional objects by applying cosine-similarity to a learned low-dimensional feature embedding. This can work better but sometimes also worse than the unnormalized dot-product between embedded vectors in practice. To gain insight into this empirical observation, we study embeddings derived from regularized linear models, where closed-form solutions facilitate analytical insights. We derive analytically how cosine-similarity can yield arbitrary and therefore meaningless 'similarities.' For some linear models the similarities are not even unique, while for others they are implicitly controlled by the regularization. We discuss implications beyond linear models: a combination of different regularizations are employed when learning deep models; these have implicit and unintended effects when taking cosine-similarities of the resulting embeddings, rendering results opaque and possibly arbitrary. Based on these insights, we caution against blindly using cosine-similarity and outline alternatives.»
