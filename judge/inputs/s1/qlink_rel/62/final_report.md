## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural models that directly generate document identifiers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This approach leverages the power of large language models to encode both queries and documents, enabling seamless integration with generative models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Early work on autoregressive search engines proposed using ngrams as document identifiers, allowing autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams, which are then mapped to full passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This method demonstrated significant improvements over traditional retrieval systems on the KILT benchmark \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\].

The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) represents a key advancement in this area, encoding all corpus information within a single transformer model, mapping queries directly to relevant document IDs \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This design simplifies the retrieval process and enables end-to-end optimization. However, early DSI models faced challenges in handling long documents and short queries, leading to data distribution mismatches \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address this, DSI-QG was introduced, which generates potential queries during indexing, allowing the model to better align with retrieval queries \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

Generative retrieval has also been explored in the context of open-domain question answering, where dense passage retrieval methods outperform traditional sparse models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. These methods use dual-encoder frameworks to learn dense representations of questions and passages, leading to significant improvements in retrieval accuracy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. Similarly, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines pre-trained models with dense vector indexes to enhance the quality of generated answers \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Blended RAG further improves accuracy by integrating semantic search techniques and hybrid query strategies \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\].

Recent work has focused on improving the scalability of generative retrieval to large-scale corpora. The ANCE framework addresses training bottlenecks by using approximate nearest neighbor indices to generate more representative negative samples \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. This leads to better performance compared to traditional dense and sparse retrieval baselines \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Similarly, the RIPOR framework introduces a novel optimization strategy for generative retrieval, significantly improving performance on large-scale benchmarks like MS MARCO \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\].

The generalization capabilities of generative retrieval on out-of-distribution corpora have also been a focus of recent research. The SEAL model, which uses ngrams as identifiers, demonstrated strong performance on the KILT benchmark while maintaining a lighter memory footprint \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. ### <a id='category-end-to-end-neural-retrieval-systems'></a>End-to-End Neural Retrieval Systems: 8 papers found.
End-to-end neural retrieval systems have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional index-based retrieval methods, aiming to integrate retrieval and generation in a unified framework \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. These systems leverage autoregressive models to generate document identifiers directly from queries, eliminating the need for explicit indexing \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. This approach allows for end-to-end optimization and better alignment with generative large language models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. However, existing methods often rely on fixed or rule-based document identifiers, such as titles or clustered embeddings, which may fail to capture the full semantic information of documents \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. To address this, GenRet was introduced, which learns semantic document identifiers through a discrete auto-encoding approach, enabling fully end-to-end training \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Similarly, SEAL proposes using all ngrams in a passage as identifiers, allowing autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams, which are then mapped to full passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of generative retrieval to improve efficiency and performance, particularly in knowledge-intensive tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. Additionally, CorpusBrain was developed as a pre-trained generative retrieval model that encodes all information about the corpus into its parameters, eliminating the need for additional indexing \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. The effectiveness of these models is further supported by the use of advanced training techniques, such as Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE), which improves the quality of negative samples during training \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00904)\]. Overall, these works highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance generative retrieval through better modeling of document identifiers and more effective training strategies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00904)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\].

### <a id='category-differentiable-search-index-(dsi)-design'></a>Differentiable Search Index (DSI) Design: 3 papers found.
The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) represents a paradigm shift in information retrieval, moving away from traditional index-based systems toward a unified, end-to-end approach using a single Transformer model \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. This model encodes the entire document corpus within its parameters, enabling direct query-to-docid generation without the need for separate indexing and retrieval stages \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. Early work on DSI highlighted its potential for efficiency and integration with large language models, but challenges emerged when scaling to larger corpora \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Specifically, existing DSI models faced issues with data distribution mismatches, where queries are typically shorter than indexed documents, exacerbating performance gaps, especially in cross-lingual settings \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address these limitations, researchers proposed DSI-QG, an indexing framework that generates and incorporates relevant queries during indexing, improving alignment between retrieval and indexing phases \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that generative retrieval, while competitive with dual encoders on small-scale tasks, faces significant hurdles when scaling to millions of passages, including the need for synthetic queries and the inefficacy of certain architectural modifications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. These findings underscore the importance of refining DSI designs to enhance scalability and performance across diverse corpus sizes and retrieval scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\].

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-paradigm-overview'></a>Generative Retrieval Paradigm Overview: 2 papers found.
The Generative Retrieval Paradigm Overview category highlights key advancements and challenges in integrating generative models with retrieval systems. It discusses how large pre-trained language models store factual knowledge and can achieve state-of-the-art results on downstream tasks, yet struggle with knowledge-intensive tasks due to limited access to explicit memory \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. To address this, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) models combine parametric and non-parametric memory, using a pre-trained seq2seq model and a dense vector index of Wikipedia accessed via a neural retriever \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This approach enables more specific, diverse, and factual language generation compared to parametric-only models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Additionally, generative retrieval aims to replace traditional search indexes with neural networks, generating document identifiers through constrained decoding \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the learning gap between autoregressive models and passage ranking targets, which is addressed by the LTRGR framework that optimizes models toward final ranking targets using rank loss \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. These developments underscore the evolving landscape of generative retrieval, balancing efficiency, integration with large language models, and generalization across diverse corpora.
