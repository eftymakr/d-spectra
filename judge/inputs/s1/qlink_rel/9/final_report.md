## Related Works

The segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been a focal point of research due to its critical role in disease monitoring and treatment evaluation. Early methods relied on manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-rater variability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. To address these limitations, automated methods have been proposed, ranging from traditional image processing techniques to more recent deep learning approaches \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/691751bfa44017d8041e17409411f9e9ffc3dec6)\]. 

A significant challenge in MS lesion segmentation is the class imbalance between rim and non-rim lesions, which complicates the training of classifiers \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. This issue has been addressed through the use of synthetic data generation, where artificial lesions are created to balance the dataset \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e9df6d7fa6b380c5db0ad4f29b9c32219aca05fe)\]. For example, QSMRim-Net employs a synthetic minority oversampling technique to improve classification performance in the presence of imbalanced data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\]. Similarly, the use of generative adversarial networks (GANs) has been explored to synthesize rim lesions, thereby enhancing the training of classifiers \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ecafc65d094c52300ffa2c9640303c584f4d569b)\].

Deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool for MS lesion segmentation, with various architectures being proposed to improve accuracy and robustness. For instance, a cascaded 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) approach has been shown to outperform other state-of-the-art methods in segmenting white matter lesions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. Similarly, a multi-branch CNN has been developed to handle multi-modal MRI data, enabling more accurate segmentation by combining information from different modalities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. The use of attention mechanisms has also been explored to improve lesion detection, particularly for small and boundary regions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11078)\].

The importance of accurate lesion segmentation is further underscored by the need for reliable quantitative measures, such as lesion volume and number, which are essential for clinical trials and disease progression analysis \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Automated segmentation tools have been evaluated against manual annotations, showing strong agreement in lesion volume quantification \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. However, challenges remain in accurately segmenting small lesions and distinguishing between different lesion types \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\].

In addition to segmentation, the detection of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) has gained attention due to their association with disease progression and disability \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02001)\]. Techniques such as random forest classifiers have been used to classify lesions based on radiomic features, achieving moderate to high accuracy \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6571f1c5630793b6c3871f890c61f228741e3190)\]. More recently, deep learning models have been adapted to detect PRLs by leveraging the unique signal characteristics of these lesions \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/11d9a8fa4b2e87bde4b9a3ff5d103dcca43767b8)\].

### <a id='category-automated-segmentation-methods'></a>Automated Segmentation Methods: 14 papers found.
Automated segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains a challenging task due to the high variability in lesion appearance, location, and size across patients \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Various methods have been proposed to address this issue, including atlas-based techniques, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and probabilistic models \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]\[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ecafc65d094c52300ffa2c9640303c584f4d569b)\]. Atlas-based methods leverage topological constraints to improve segmentation accuracy by aligning segmented structures with anatomical templates \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. CNNs have shown superior performance in lesion segmentation, particularly when trained on large datasets, but their performance often degrades when applied to new imaging domains or protocols \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e9df6d7fa6b380c5db0ad4f29b9c32219aca05fe)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. To mitigate this, domain adaptation techniques have been introduced, enabling models to generalize across different MRI scanners and acquisition protocols with minimal labeled data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. Probabilistic models, such as MSmetrix, use FLAIR and T1-weighted images to detect white matter lesions by identifying hyperintense regions that deviate from normal tissue \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\]. These models are designed to be independent of scanner-specific parameters, enhancing their applicability in clinical settings \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\]. Additionally, recent approaches integrate anatomical coordinate information and lesion-wise loss functions to improve the detection of small lesions and enhance robustness across different lesion sizes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. The integration of multi-channel data and 2.5D convolutional networks has also shown promise in improving segmentation accuracy by leveraging contextual information from multiple imaging planes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11078)\]. Despite these advancements, challenges persist in achieving consistent performance across diverse datasets and clinical scenarios, highlighting the need for further research and standardization in validation frameworks \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]\[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/691751bfa44017d8041e17409411f9e9ffc3dec6)\].

The category "Multi-Branch Architectures for Multi-Modal Data" encompasses research focused on leveraging multi-modal data, such as quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) and T2-FLAIR MRI, to improve lesion detection and segmentation in multiple sclerosis (MS). These studies emphasize the use of deep learning models with multi-branch architectures to integrate information from different imaging modalities, enhancing the accuracy of automated lesion identification \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. For instance, QSMRim-Net employs a two-branch feature extraction network that combines image-level features from QSM and T2-FLAIR with lesion-level radiomic features, addressing the challenge of class imbalance in lesion detection \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. Similarly, other works have proposed multi-branch convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that encode information from multiple modalities separately, enabling more robust feature fusion and improved segmentation performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. These models often incorporate multi-scale feature fusion blocks to combine features from different modalities at various stages of the network, allowing for better contextual understanding of lesion characteristics \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. Additionally, some approaches utilize multi-scale feature upsampling blocks to refine lesion boundaries and improve spatial resolution \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. The integration of multi-modal data through such architectures has shown significant improvements in both lesion detection and segmentation tasks, particularly in challenging scenarios with imbalanced data \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. These methods are evaluated on various datasets, including private and public benchmark datasets, demonstrating their effectiveness in clinical applications \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\].
