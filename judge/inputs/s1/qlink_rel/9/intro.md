## Related Works

The segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been a focal point of research due to its critical role in disease monitoring and treatment evaluation. Early methods relied on manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-rater variability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. To address these limitations, automated methods have been proposed, ranging from traditional image processing techniques to more recent deep learning approaches \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/691751bfa44017d8041e17409411f9e9ffc3dec6)\]. 

A significant challenge in MS lesion segmentation is the class imbalance between rim and non-rim lesions, which complicates the training of classifiers \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. This issue has been addressed through the use of synthetic data generation, where artificial lesions are created to balance the dataset \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e9df6d7fa6b380c5db0ad4f29b9c32219aca05fe)\]. For example, QSMRim-Net employs a synthetic minority oversampling technique to improve classification performance in the presence of imbalanced data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\]. Similarly, the use of generative adversarial networks (GANs) has been explored to synthesize rim lesions, thereby enhancing the training of classifiers \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ecafc65d094c52300ffa2c9640303c584f4d569b)\].

Deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool for MS lesion segmentation, with various architectures being proposed to improve accuracy and robustness. For instance, a cascaded 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) approach has been shown to outperform other state-of-the-art methods in segmenting white matter lesions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. Similarly, a multi-branch CNN has been developed to handle multi-modal MRI data, enabling more accurate segmentation by combining information from different modalities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. The use of attention mechanisms has also been explored to improve lesion detection, particularly for small and boundary regions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11078)\].

The importance of accurate lesion segmentation is further underscored by the need for reliable quantitative measures, such as lesion volume and number, which are essential for clinical trials and disease progression analysis \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Automated segmentation tools have been evaluated against manual annotations, showing strong agreement in lesion volume quantification \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. However, challenges remain in accurately segmenting small lesions and distinguishing between different lesion types \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\].

In addition to segmentation, the detection of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) has gained attention due to their association with disease progression and disability \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02001)\]. Techniques such as random forest classifiers have been used to classify lesions based on radiomic features, achieving moderate to high accuracy \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6571f1c5630793b6c3871f890c61f228741e3190)\]. More recently, deep learning models have been adapted to detect PRLs by leveraging the unique signal characteristics of these lesions \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/11d9a8fa4b2e87bde4b9a3ff5d103dcca43767b8)\].
