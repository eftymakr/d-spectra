## Related Works

The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) into large language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning capabilities has gained significant attention in recent years. While LLMs have demonstrated strong zero-shot reasoning abilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\], they often struggle with multi-step reasoning and leveraging structured knowledge from KGs without adaptation \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)\]. This limitation highlights the need for frameworks that can effectively combine the strengths of LLMs with the rich, semantically structured information provided by KGs.

Several approaches have explored the use of KGs for fact-checking and claim verification. For instance, the work by \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07129)\] introduces ERNIE, a model that integrates both textual corpora and KGs to enhance language representation, achieving improvements on knowledge-driven tasks. Similarly, \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14974)\] presents SciFact, a dataset and baseline models for scientific claim verification, demonstrating the importance of evidence-based reasoning. However, these methods primarily rely on unstructured text corpora, limiting their ability to fully leverage the structured knowledge available in KGs \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14974)\].

To address these limitations, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\] reviews Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) as a promising approach, combining LLMs with external databases to enhance accuracy and credibility for knowledge-intensive tasks. This aligns with the idea of using KGs to provide additional context and structured knowledge. Additionally, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)\] investigates the effectiveness of parametric and non-parametric memories in LLMs, finding that retrieval augmentation significantly improves performance on less popular factual knowledge, which is critical for claim verification.

The concept of using KGs for multi-document question answering (MD-QA) has also been explored. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11730)\] proposes Knowledge Graph Prompting (KGP), which constructs a graph over multiple documents to guide LLMs in MD-QA. This approach highlights the potential of KGs in structuring and organizing information for more accurate and interpretable reasoning. Furthermore, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\] explores RAG models that combine pre-trained seq2seq models with dense vector indexes of Wikipedia, demonstrating the effectiveness of integrating external knowledge into LLMs for open-domain QA tasks.

### <a id='category-retrieval-augmented-generation'></a>Retrieval-Augmented Generation: 4 papers found.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising approach to enhance the knowledge-intensive capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by integrating external knowledge sources. This paradigm combines pre-trained parametric models with non-parametric memory, such as dense vector indexes of Wikipedia, accessed via neural retrievers, to improve factual accuracy and reduce hallucinations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. RAG models have been shown to generate more specific, diverse, and factual responses compared to parametric-only seq2seq baselines, particularly on open-domain question-answering tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Additionally, systems like LLM-Augmenter further refine this approach by incorporating plug-and-play modules that iteratively revise prompts based on feedback from utility functions, such as factuality scores, to improve response quality without compromising fluency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12813)\]. The integration of external knowledge also addresses the limitations of LLMs in handling less popular factual knowledge, where retrieval augmentation significantly enhances performance \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)\]. These advancements highlight the potential of RAG to bridge the gap between LLMs' intrinsic knowledge and the dynamic, structured information available in external databases, enabling more reliable and adaptable reasoning across various tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\].

### <a id='category-fact-checking-frameworks'></a>Fact-Checking Frameworks: 2 papers found.
The category "Fact-Checking Frameworks" highlights the ongoing advancements in developing robust models for verifying real-world claims. Recent approaches emphasize the need for structured reasoning and the integration of external knowledge sources, such as knowledge graphs (KGs), to enhance the accuracy and reliability of fact-checking systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. For instance, Program-Guided Fact-Checking (ProgramFC) introduces a novel framework that decomposes complex claims into sub-tasks, leveraging large language models' in-context learning capabilities to generate reasoning programs that guide the verification process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. This approach not only improves data efficiency but also provides transparent explanations of the reasoning steps, which is crucial for human verification and debugging. Additionally, the integration of KGs with large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a promising direction, as seen in the ClaimPKG framework, which combines lightweight LLMs with structured knowledge retrieval to achieve state-of-the-art performance in claim verification \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. These developments underscore the importance of combining both unstructured text analysis and structured knowledge representation to address the limitations of traditional fact-checking methods. Furthermore, the challenges of hallucination in natural language generation (NLG) highlight the broader need for reliable and explainable models, as deep learning-based generation can introduce unintended inaccuracies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. These insights collectively emphasize the growing focus on hybrid models that integrate diverse data sources and reasoning mechanisms to improve the robustness of fact-checking systems.

### <a id='category-llm-reasoning-limitations'></a>LLM Reasoning Limitations: 2 papers found.
The category "LLM Reasoning Limitations" highlights critical challenges in the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). Existing studies show that while LLMs excel in natural language processing tasks, their performance on graph-structured data remains limited, with little research addressing their broader applicability to such data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Additionally, LLMs like ChatGPT, though capable of zero-shot learning and multimodal content generation, exhibit significant shortcomings in logical and commonsense reasoning, achieving only 63.41% accuracy across various reasoning categories \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. Furthermore, despite their strong reasoning abilities, modern LLMs struggle with multi-step modular pipelines and reasoning over knowledge graphs without adaptation, underscoring the need for specialized frameworks to integrate structured knowledge effectively \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. These limitations emphasize the necessity for further advancements in enhancing LLMs' graph processing and reasoning capabilities.
