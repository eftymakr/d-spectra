## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, leveraging the structural information of user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early approaches, such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), attempted to integrate user-item graphs into the embedding process by propagating embeddings through the graph \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. However, these models often relied on complex architectures involving feature transformations and nonlinear activations, which were found to contribute little to performance and even hinder training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. LightGCN, a simplified variant, focused on linear propagation and neighborhood aggregation, achieving significant improvements over NGCF \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. Similarly, UltraGCN further simplified GCNs by skipping infinite layers of message passing and using a constraint loss to approximate the infinite-layer graph convolution \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\].

The effectiveness of GCNs in recommendation has been attributed to their ability to capture high-order connectivity in user-item graphs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. However, challenges such as training difficulty with non-linear activations and the over-smoothing problem in deep layers have been identified \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. To address these, LightGCN removed non-linearities and focused on linear aggregation, leading to better scalability and performance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. The over-smoothing issue was further mitigated by introducing residual structures in models like LR-GCCF, which helped maintain distinct representations across layers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\].

Graph-based collaborative filtering has also been explored through spectral methods. SpectralCF directly operates in the spectral domain, capturing both proximity and connectivity information to alleviate the cold-start problem \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Similarly, BernNet proposed a method to learn arbitrary spectral filters using Bernstein polynomial approximations, enabling more flexible and accurate modeling of graph signals \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\]. These spectral-based approaches have shown promise in capturing complex patterns in user-item interactions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\].

In addition to GCNs, other models have been developed to enhance collaborative filtering. Knowledge Graph Attention Networks (KGAT) integrate side information and high-order relations from knowledge graphs, improving recommendation accuracy by explicitly modeling these interactions \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1211.0053)\]. Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) further advanced the field by disentangling user intents through intent-aware interaction graphs, leading to more interpretable and diverse recommendations \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07243)\].

The use of self-supervised learning has also been explored to improve robustness and accuracy in recommendation systems. Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL) augmented the classical recommendation task with auxiliary self-supervised tasks, improving the representation of nodes and enhancing robustness against noisy interactions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10699)\].
