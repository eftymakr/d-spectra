## Related Works

Fairness in machine learning has garnered significant attention due to its potential to perpetuate or exacerbate societal inequalities. Early work in this area focused on defining fairness through statistical parity, ensuring that outcomes are distributed equitably across different demographic groups \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. However, this approach has been criticized for being too simplistic and not capturing the nuanced ways in which bias can manifest \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. More recent research has sought to incorporate causal inference and counterfactual reasoning to define fairness in a way that accounts for the impact of protected attributes on decision-making \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This has led to the development of frameworks such as *counterfactual fairness*, which requires that a decision remains consistent across different hypothetical scenarios involving demographic changes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\].

A key challenge in fairness research is the tension between minimizing error disparity across groups and maintaining calibrated probability estimates \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. This tension highlights the difficulty of achieving both fairness and accuracy simultaneously, particularly when the data used to train models is inherently biased \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. Additionally, fairness interventions are often sensitive to variations in dataset composition and preprocessing steps, which can lead to inconsistent or brittle results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04422)\]. These findings underscore the need for robust and adaptable fairness-aware algorithms that can handle the complexities of real-world data.

Several studies have emphasized the importance of understanding the practical needs of industry practitioners when designing fairness-aware systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. This includes the development of tools that not only assess fairness but also support the implementation of fairer models in production environments. The concept of *fairness through awareness* has been proposed as a way to integrate fairness constraints into the learning process, ensuring that similar individuals are treated similarly while maintaining utility for the classifier \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This approach has been extended to include *fair affirmative action*, which aims to achieve statistical parity while preserving fairness \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\].

Recent work has also highlighted the limitations of existing fairness metrics, particularly their inability to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty on fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. For instance, conventional metrics like *Equalized Odds* may overlook cases where a model exhibits high confidence in incorrect predictions, leading to biased outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metrics that account for the internal bias in model decisions, offering a more nuanced evaluation of fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. This aligns with the broader goal of developing benchmarks that reflect the complexities of real-world applications and provide a more comprehensive assessment of model behavior.
