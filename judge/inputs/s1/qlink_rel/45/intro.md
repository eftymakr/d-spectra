## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has emerged as a critical area within sentiment analysis, particularly in the context of user-generated content such as app reviews. While traditional sentiment analysis often focuses on polarity (positive, negative, neutral) \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12d0353ce8b41b7e5409e5a4a611110aee33c7bc)\], recent work has emphasized the need for more nuanced emotion detection to better capture user sentiment. This is especially relevant in app reviews, where users express a wide range of emotional states that go beyond simple polarity \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\]. 

Several studies have explored the challenges of fine-grained emotion detection. GoEmotions, for instance, introduces a large-scale dataset of 58k Reddit comments labeled for 27 emotion categories or Neutral, demonstrating the potential of fine-grained emotion classification \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. However, the application of such datasets to app reviews remains underexplored. Similarly, EmoNet develops deep learning models for fine-grained emotion detection, achieving state-of-the-art results on 24 emotion types \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. These models rely on large, annotated datasets, which are often not tailored to specific domains like app reviews.

In the context of app reviews, research has focused on sentiment analysis and emotional sentiment extraction. A study analyzing over seven million Apple AppStore reviews highlights the importance of understanding emotional sentiment for software engineering and product development \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\]. Another study, AR-miner, presents a computational framework for mining informative reviews, emphasizing the need for effective review analytics tools \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff921a209cc9e1c2120669d3d81e627ca5e53572)\]. These works underscore the growing interest in leveraging user feedback for app improvement, yet they often focus on sentiment rather than fine-grained emotion classification.

Efforts to create emotion lexicons have also been explored. CROWDSOURCING A WORD–EMOTION ASSOCIATION LEXICON demonstrates how crowdsourcing can be used to generate large, high-quality emotion lexicons \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. This approach addresses the limitations of small, manually curated emotion lexicons by leveraging the wisdom of the crowd. However, the integration of such lexicons into app review analysis remains an open challenge.

The use of machine learning for emotion prediction has also been investigated. Emotions from Text explores the use of supervised machine learning for emotion prediction in children's fairy tales, showing promising results \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b)\]. Similarly, the SNoW learning architecture has been applied to classify emotional content in narrative texts \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b)\]. These studies highlight the potential of machine learning models in capturing emotional nuances, although they often focus on specific domains or text types.

In addition, the SemEval-2018 Task 1 on Affect in Tweets provides labeled data for emotion classification
