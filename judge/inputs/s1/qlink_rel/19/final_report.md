## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match speech content and emotion. Early approaches primarily relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, which lacked the flexibility and realism required for complex facial animations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. With the advent of deep learning, several methods have been proposed to improve the synchronization between speech and facial movements.

One notable approach is the use of self-supervised pre-trained audio models as encoders, which have shown promise in capturing meaningful audio features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. However, these models often suffer from the averaging effect, where phonetically similar syllables with distinct lip shapes are coupled in the feature space, leading to inaccurate lip motion generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. This issue is particularly pronounced in languages with rich phonetic variation, such as English, where near-homophones can significantly affect the realism of facial animations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\].

To address this limitation, several works have explored the integration of semantic information into the audio processing pipeline. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\] introduced a method that leverages phoneme-level features to better align audio and visual modalities, improving lip synchronization. However, these methods often focus on short audio windows and may not generalize well to longer sequences \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\] proposed a cross-modal attention mechanism to align audio and motion features, but the lack of long-term context remains a challenge \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

Recent advances have focused on transformer-based models that can encode long-term audio context and autoregressively predict 3D face meshes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. FaceFormer, for example, integrates self-supervised speech representations and introduces biased attention mechanisms to improve generalization \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\]. Another approach, MeshTalk, disentangles audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information using a cross-modality loss, enabling more realistic facial animations \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\].

The importance of emotional disentanglement has also been emphasized in recent studies. EmoTalk, for instance, proposes an end-to-end neural network to disentangle emotions from speech content, generating more expressive facial animations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00023)\]. Similarly, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12992)\] introduced a method that learns emotional dynamics from audio and visual signals, enabling the synthesis of emotionally rich facial expressions \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\].

In addition to emotional disentanglement, the problem of head pose control has been addressed in several works. ### <a id='category-emotional-disentanglement-in-facial-animation'></a>Emotional Disentanglement in Facial Animation: 12 papers found.
The category "Emotional Disentanglement in Facial Animation" focuses on improving the realism and expressiveness of speech-driven facial animation by addressing the disentanglement of emotional and content-related features in audio signals. Existing methods often struggle with accurately capturing emotional dynamics, leading to unrealistic or static facial expressions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. To overcome these limitations, researchers have proposed various techniques, such as the Emotion Disentangling Encoder (EDE), which separates emotion and content in speech through cross-reconstructed signals with different emotion labels \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. Additionally, the Cross-Reconstructed Emotion Disentanglement technique decomposes speech into duration-independent emotion and duration-dependent content spaces, enabling the generation of dynamic emotional facial landmarks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. These approaches aim to enhance the emotional expressiveness of synthesized faces by leveraging disentangled representations. Furthermore, methods like the Emotion-Aware Motion Model (EAMM) incorporate emotion-related facial dynamics as linearly additive displacements to motion representations, allowing for more realistic emotional expressions on arbitrary subjects \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. The use of temporal autoregressive models and code query tasks in learned codebooks also contributes to generating more vivid and synchronized facial motions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. These innovations collectively address the challenges of emotional disentanglement, leading to more natural and expressive facial animations driven by speech.

### <a id='category-pose-controllability-in-talking-face-generation'></a>Pose Controllability in Talking Face Generation: 8 papers found.
In the field of talking face generation, recent works have focused on improving the controllability of head pose and facial expressions while ensuring realistic lip synchronization with audio input \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Researchers have explored methods that disentangle audio-visual representations to enable arbitrary-subject generation, allowing for more flexible and controllable facial animations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. For instance, some approaches utilize a joint non-identity embedding space where both speech content and head pose information are encoded, enabling the model to learn both aspects simultaneously \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. Others have introduced frameworks that operate on non-aligned raw face images, using a single photo as an identity reference, and modularize audio-visual representations through an implicit low-dimension pose code \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\]. Additionally, there has been a growing interest in leveraging large-scale 4D face datasets to train models that can generate realistic 3D facial animations from speech inputs, even for unseen subjects \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. These models often incorporate viseme dynamics and deep audio feature models pretrained on multiple languages to support multilingual speech inputs and improve generalizability \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Furthermore, some studies have emphasized the importance of high-resolution datasets and flow-guided generation frameworks to produce high-definition talking face videos with detailed expression and head pose control \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. Overall, the field has seen significant progress in achieving realistic, controllable, and personalized talking face generation, with ongoing efforts to enhance performance and usability for various applications.
