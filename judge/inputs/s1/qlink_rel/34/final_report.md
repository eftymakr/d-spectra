## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a central challenge in various domains, including finance, energy, and weather prediction. Traditional approaches such as autoregressive models and Gaussian processes have been widely used but often struggle with capturing complex temporal dependencies and handling long-term forecasting \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. Recent advances in deep learning have led to the development of more sophisticated models that leverage neural networks to better model temporal dynamics \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of Transformers for time series forecasting, given their ability to capture long-range dependencies \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\]. However, Transformers face challenges in terms of computational efficiency and memory usage, especially for long sequences \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07436)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed variants such as the Informer, which introduces a ProbSparse self-attention mechanism to reduce time and memory complexity while maintaining performance \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07436)\]. Similarly, the Autoformer employs an Auto-Correlation mechanism to improve the efficiency and accuracy of long-term forecasting \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. 

Another line of research focuses on the interpretability of time series models. The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) was introduced to provide interpretable insights into temporal dynamics, combining recurrent layers with self-attention mechanisms to learn both local and long-term dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. This approach has been shown to improve forecasting accuracy and provide meaningful insights into model behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. 

In addition to forecasting, there has been growing interest in using pre-trained models for time series analysis. The Chronos framework, for instance, pre-trains Transformer-based models on a large collection of time series data, enabling zero-shot performance on new datasets \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\]. Similarly, the Time-LLM framework reprograms large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting, aligning the modalities of time series data and natural language \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of pre-trained models in improving forecasting accuracy across diverse domains. 

However, the effectiveness of LLMs for time series forecasting remains a topic of debate. Some studies have found that replacing the LLM component with a basic attention layer does not degrade forecasting performance, and in some cases, results in improvement \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. Furthermore, LLMs may not effectively capture sequential dependencies in time series, and their performance can be limited by the way they tokenize numerical data \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\].

### <a id='category-time-series-forecasting-models'></a>Time Series Forecasting Models: 15 papers found.
The related works in time series forecasting models focus on improving accuracy, interpretability, and efficiency, particularly in handling complex temporal dependencies and uncertainty. Several studies have introduced novel architectures to address these challenges. For instance, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) combines recurrent layers with self-attention mechanisms to capture both local and long-term dependencies, while also incorporating feature selection and gating layers for improved performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. Similarly, the PatchTST model segments time series into subseries-level patches, enabling efficient computation and better long-term forecasting accuracy by leveraging channel-independent embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. The DeepAR model, based on an autoregressive recurrent network, provides probabilistic forecasts and has demonstrated significant accuracy improvements over classical methods in retail demand forecasting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. However, recent work has questioned the effectiveness of Transformer-based models for long-term forecasting, with experiments showing that simple linear models like LTSF-Linear can outperform complex Transformer architectures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\]. To address the limitations of Transformers, such as quadratic memory usage and poor local context handling, researchers have proposed variants like the LogSparse Transformer and Convolutional Self-Attention, which enhance efficiency and accuracy for long-granularity time series \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\]. Additionally, models like TimesNet and Autoformer have been developed to better capture multi-periodicity and long-range dependencies, achieving state-of-the-art results in various forecasting tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00235)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. The iTransformer model further improves upon Transformers by reusing the architecture with inverted dimensions, enabling better handling of multivariate and long-lookback series \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to refine time series forecasting models for real-world applications, balancing performance, interpretability, and computational efficiency.

### <a id='category-pre-trained-models-for-time-series'></a>Pre-Trained Models for Time Series: 3 papers found.
In the realm of pre-trained models for time series, recent advancements have focused on leveraging transformer-based architectures to improve forecasting performance across diverse domains. Researchers have explored methods such as tokenizing time series data using scaling and quantization to train transformer models on a fixed vocabulary, leading to significant improvements in both in-distribution and zero-shot settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. Additionally, models like the Frozen Pretrained Transformer (FPT) have demonstrated that pre-trained language or image models, with minimal modifications to their self-attention and feedforward layers, can achieve state-of-the-art results in time series analysis tasks such as forecasting, classification, and anomaly detection \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. These models are trained on large corpora and can generalize well across different temporal granularities and forecasting horizons \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Furthermore, the design of patched-decoder style attention models has enabled models to perform well with varying forecasting history lengths and prediction lengths, enhancing their adaptability to real-world applications \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\]. These developments highlight the growing potential of pre-trained models in time series analysis, offering a unified approach that reduces the need for task-specific architectures.

### <a id='category-transformer-based-approaches'></a>Transformer-Based Approaches: 2 papers found.
Transformers have demonstrated significant potential in time series forecasting due to their ability to capture long-range dependencies and interactions, which is particularly advantageous for modeling complex temporal patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. However, their performance can be hindered by non-stationarity in real-world data, where the underlying distribution evolves over time, leading to issues such as over-stationarization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. This problem arises when stationarization techniques are applied to remove non-stationarity, resulting in a loss of critical information needed for accurate forecasting of bursty events \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. To address this, recent work has introduced Non-stationary Transformers, a framework that incorporates two interdependent modules—Series Stationarization and De-stationary Attention—to better handle non-stationary data while preserving essential temporal dynamics \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance Transformer-based models for time series tasks, ensuring they remain robust and effective in practical applications.

### <a id='category-large-language-models-in-forecasting'></a>Large Language Models in Forecasting: 3 papers found.
Time series forecasting has garnered significant attention in recent research, particularly with the emergence of large language models (LLMs) as potential tools for this task. Unlike traditional approaches that often rely on specialized models, LLMs offer a flexible framework for handling complex sequential data, leveraging their robust pattern recognition and reasoning capabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. However, the effectiveness of LLMs in time series forecasting remains a topic of debate, with some studies suggesting that removing the LLM component or replacing it with a basic attention layer does not degrade forecasting performance, and in some cases, even improves it \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. This highlights the need for careful evaluation of LLMs in this domain. Additionally, researchers have explored encoding time series as strings of numerical digits to frame forecasting as next-token prediction, demonstrating that LLMs like GPT-3 and LLaMA-2 can achieve performance comparable to or exceeding that of purpose-built time series models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. The success of LLMs in this context is attributed to their ability to represent multimodal distributions and align with features such as repeated seasonal trends \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Despite these advantages, challenges remain, including issues related to tokenization, uncertainty calibration, and the impact of alignment interventions like RLHF, which can affect model performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. These findings underscore the evolving landscape of time series forecasting and the potential of LLMs in this field.
