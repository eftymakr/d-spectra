## Related Works

Fairness in ranking and recommendation systems has been a growing area of research, with multiple studies addressing the trade-off between accuracy and fairness. Early work on fairness in ranking, such as Fairness of Exposure in Rankings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\], introduced the concept of fairness constraints in ranking systems, emphasizing the need to balance user utility with fairness to the items being ranked. This work laid the foundation for subsequent research on fairness-aware ranking algorithms.

The idea of fairness through awareness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\] further expanded the discussion by proposing a framework for fair classification, where similar individuals are treated similarly. This concept has been adapted to ranking systems, where fairness is not only about group-level parity but also about individual-level treatment. The paper also discusses the relationship between fairness and privacy, highlighting the importance of differential privacy in fairness-aware systems.

Ranking with fairness constraints \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\] introduced the problem of incorporating fairness or diversity constraints into ranking algorithms. The work demonstrated that fairness constraints can significantly impact the ranking quality and that optimizing for fairness requires careful balancing with utility. This study also showed that the problem is computationally challenging, even with simple constraints.

Equity of attention: amortizing individual fairness in rankings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\] introduced the concept of individual fairness in rankings, where the attention given to each ranked item is considered. This work emphasized that fairness should be measured at the individual level rather than just at the group level, and it proposed a mechanism for achieving amortized fairness across a series of rankings.

FairRec: two-sided fairness for personalized recommendations in two-sided platforms \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\] addressed the issue of fairness in two-sided platforms, where both customers and producers are affected by recommendation systems. This work proposed a novel approach to fairly allocate exposure among producers while maintaining customer satisfaction, demonstrating that fairness considerations must span both sides of the platform.

Fairness in recommendation ranking through pairwise comparisons \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\] introduced a new method for evaluating fairness in recommender systems based on pairwise comparisons. This approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of fairness in rankings and has been applied to large-scale production systems with significant improvements in pairwise fairness.

### <a id='category-fairness-constraints-in-ranking-optimization'></a>Fairness Constraints in Ranking Optimization: 6 papers found.
The category "Fairness Constraints in Ranking Optimization" explores the integration of fairness considerations into ranking systems, emphasizing the tension between accuracy and fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. Researchers have highlighted that ranking algorithms, when left unregulated, can lead to issues such as reduced diversity, stereotyping, and polarization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. To address these challenges, studies propose frameworks that incorporate fairness constraints, such as demographic parity, disparate treatment, and disparate impact, into the ranking optimization process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. These frameworks aim to balance user utility with fairness by formulating exposure allocation strategies and developing algorithms that respect specified constraints while maximizing ranking quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. Additionally, there is growing recognition that fairness in recommender systems must consider both consumer and producer-side impacts, as these systems operate within a two-sided marketplace \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. This necessitates joint optimization of fairness metrics from both perspectives \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. Furthermore, the literature acknowledges that fairness definitions are often inconsistent, with varying motivations, terminology, and assumptions, which complicates the comparison and evaluation of fairness-aware algorithms \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]. The concept of elasticity has been introduced to model the trade-off between fairness and accuracy, with the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) serving as a novel evaluation framework \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. This approach allows for a more nuanced assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms by considering how changes in fairness metrics affect overall performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\].

### <a id='category-individual-vs.-group-fairness-in-rankings'></a>Individual vs. Group Fairness in Rankings: 5 papers found.
The category "Individual vs. Group Fairness in Rankings" explores the tension between fairness at the individual level and fairness across groups, with significant implications for machine learning and decision-making systems. Researchers have highlighted that traditional fairness metrics, such as statistical parity or equalized odds, often fail to account for subgroup disparities, leading to potential discrimination against specific subgroups \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This issue is exacerbated by the computational challenges of enforcing fairness across exponentially many subgroups, which has been shown to be equivalent to weak agnostic learning, making it computationally intractable in the worst case \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. To address these challenges, some studies propose frameworks that combine individual fairness—ensuring similar individuals are treated similarly—with group fairness, such as statistical parity, to achieve a balance between fairness and utility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. Additionally, the philosophical underpinnings of fairness in machine learning are examined, drawing on moral and political philosophy to clarify the conceptual foundations of fairness, discrimination, and justice \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. In the context of re-ranking tasks, fairness considerations are often framed through an economic lens, where fairness constraints can be seen as analogous to commodity taxes, leading to a trade-off between accuracy and fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. This trade-off is formalized using the concept of elasticity, which measures how changes in fairness constraints affect overall accuracy, leading to the development of evaluation frameworks like the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. These insights underscore the need for more nuanced and comprehensive approaches to fairness in ranking tasks, particularly in domains where automated decisions have significant societal implications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\].

Some works have introduced novel evaluation metrics, such as pairwise fairness measures, which assess fairness based on randomized experiments and provide a tractable way to evaluate fairness in rankings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of developing robust fairness assessment tools that align with real-world applications and ethical considerations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\].

### <a id='category-fairness-aware-ranking-algorithms'></a>Fairness-Aware Ranking Algorithms: 7 papers found.
Fairness-aware ranking algorithms have been increasingly explored to address the trade-off between recommendation accuracy and fairness, particularly in scenarios involving multiple stakeholders such as lenders and borrowers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. These algorithms aim to ensure that items or users from different demographic groups have a fair chance of being recommended, which is crucial in domains like microlending and hiring \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. For instance, in microlending, fairness is essential to provide borrowers from various backgrounds with equitable access to financial services \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. Similarly, in hiring platforms, fairness-aware ranking ensures that candidates from underrepresented groups are not unfairly disadvantaged \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]. To achieve this balance, researchers have proposed re-ranking methods that incorporate fairness metrics while maintaining recommendation quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. These approaches often involve iterative optimization processes that adjust rankings based on fairness constraints, such as equality of opportunity or demographic parity \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]. Moreover, personalization plays a key role, as users may have varying tolerances for diversity in recommendations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. Some methods incorporate user preferences to tailor fairness considerations, ensuring that the trade-off between accuracy and fairness is managed effectively \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. Additionally, recent work has emphasized the importance of elasticity in evaluating fairness-accuracy trade-offs, using concepts from economics to model how fairness constraints impact user-side accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. These insights highlight the complexity of designing fair ranking systems that are both effective and equitable \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].
