## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Similarly, conventional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making them less practical in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. This section reviews related work on TTS, prosody modeling, and mispronunciation correction, highlighting their limitations and how they relate to the proposed method.

Prosody modeling is a critical component in modern TTS frameworks, as it enables control over the style of synthesized utterances \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. By explicitly providing prosody features to the TTS model, the style of the output can be controlled \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. However, predicting natural and reasonable prosody at inference time remains challenging \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Several works have proposed hierarchical architectures to improve prosody modeling, such as conditioning phoneme-level prosody features on word-level prosody features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. These methods have shown improvements in audio quality and prosody naturalness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Additionally, hierarchical prosody modeling has been explored in non-autoregressive TTS models, where the model is conditioned on both coarse and fine-grained acoustic speech features to learn a latent prosody space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. This approach enables more intuitive and meaningful control over prosody dimensions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\].

Non-autoregressive TTS models, such as FastSpeech and Fastpitch, have been developed to improve inference speed while maintaining high-quality speech synthesis \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. FastSpeech 2, for instance, directly trains the model with ground-truth targets instead of simplified outputs from a teacher model, leading to improved voice quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. Fastpitch, on the other hand, conditions the model on fundamental frequency contours, allowing for more expressive and semantically aligned speech \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of non-autoregressive approaches for efficient and high-quality TTS synthesis.

Mispronunciation correction in TTS has traditionally relied on grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) dictionaries, which are not always reliable in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Recent work has explored end-to-end TTS systems that can learn prosody and pronunciation directly from raw speech data without explicit prosody annotations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. ### <a id='category-non-autoregressive-tts-models'></a>Non-Autoregressive TTS Models: 3 papers found.
Non-autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) models have gained significant attention for their ability to generate speech in parallel, improving efficiency compared to autoregressive models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Models like FastSpeech and Parallel Tacotron address the one-to-many mapping problem by incorporating more variation information such as pitch, energy, and duration during training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. FastSpeech uses a teacher-student distillation pipeline with an autoregressive teacher model for duration prediction, but this approach has limitations in terms of accuracy and information loss \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. In contrast, Parallel Tacotron introduces a variational autoencoder-based residual encoder to relax the one-to-many mapping and improve naturalness, along with lightweight convolutions and an iterative spectrogram loss for better performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. Glow-TTS further advances parallel TTS by using a flow-based generative model that eliminates the need for external aligners, enabling faster and more controllable speech synthesis \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]. These models collectively highlight the progress in non-autoregressive TTS, focusing on parallelization, prosody control, and efficient training pipelines.

Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have enabled high-quality speech synthesis, but controlling prosody and correcting mispronunciations remain challenging \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. For instance, some approaches condition on fundamental frequency contours to improve expressiveness and alignment with utterance semantics \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\], while others focus on modeling prosody using quantized latent vectors pre-trained on large-scale unpaired data to capture pitch, duration, and energy attributes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. These methods often require explicit prosody annotations or high-quality training data, which may not be available in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have explored fully automatic methods that enable sentence-wise control of speaking pace and expressiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\], as well as models that incorporate attention mechanisms to improve attention convergence and pace control sensitivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Additionally, some studies have focused on improving prosody for multi-sentence inputs by leveraging long context and multi-speaker data, which enhance coherence and disambiguate prosody \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. Others have proposed training strategies that incorporate perceptual quality as a loss function to improve naturalness and expressiveness without requiring explicit prosody annotations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. These efforts highlight the ongoing challenges in modeling prosody, including the dependency on high-quality data, the complexity of capturing interdependent prosodic attributes, and the difficulty of achieving consistent prosody across different speaking styles and contexts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\].
