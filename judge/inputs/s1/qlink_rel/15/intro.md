## Related Works

Brain tumor segmentation is a critical task in medical imaging, with significant implications for diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis. Several approaches have been proposed to address this challenge, leveraging deep learning techniques to improve segmentation accuracy and efficiency. Early works focused on 2D convolutional networks, such as U-Net \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04597)\], which introduced an encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections to capture contextual information. While effective for 2D images, these methods often struggled with 3D medical data due to their limited ability to model spatial relationships \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4038)\]. To address this, 3D CNNs were proposed, such as 3D U-Net \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06650)\], which extended the U-Net framework to handle volumetric data. However, training 3D CNNs is computationally expensive and requires large annotated datasets \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.11052)\].

To mitigate these challenges, several studies have explored data augmentation and transfer learning. For example, the V-Net \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04797)\] was introduced as a fully convolutional network for 3D medical image segmentation, using a novel objective function based on the Dice coefficient to handle class imbalance. Similarly, the Brain Tumor Segmentation Challenge (BraTS) has driven the development of various methods, including those based on U-Net \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03820)\], which has become a popular baseline for 3D segmentation tasks. More recently, the nnU-Net \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\] was proposed as a self-configuring method that automatically adapts to different datasets, improving segmentation performance through region-based training and aggressive data augmentation. This method has been widely adopted in the BraTS challenge, achieving state-of-the-art results \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\].

In addition to CNN-based approaches, hybrid models have been explored to enhance segmentation performance. For instance, the integration of fully convolutional neural networks (FCNNs) with Conditional Random Fields (CRFs) \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04528)\] has been shown to improve segmentation robustness by incorporating spatial consistency. Similarly, the use of residual connections and multi-scale predictions has been investigated to improve the accuracy of segmentation models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.00382)\]. Furthermore, the application of attention mechanisms and contextual information has been explored to better capture the complex structures of brain tumors \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d04e43e0466151be1df5cb6cc1692453a316ee23)\].

Recent advances have also focused on reducing the computational burden of training and inference. For example, the use of test-time augmentation \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.07884)\] has been shown to improve segmentation accuracy while providing uncertainty estimates. Additionally, the development of efficient architectures, such as the 2.5D CNN \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b9c3631120c171570b94f946273b98c32505fc9a)\], has enabled faster inference while maintaining high accuracy.
