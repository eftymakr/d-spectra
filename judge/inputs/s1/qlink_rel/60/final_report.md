## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been extensively studied, with a focus on improving the reliability of predicted probabilities. Traditional calibration methods often rely on predefined transformation functions, such as temperature scaling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\], which applies a single parameter to adjust the output logits. While effective in many cases, these methods are limited by their fixed forms, which may not capture the complex relationships between model confidence and correctness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. For example, temperature scaling assumes a simple exponential relationship between logits and probabilities, which may not hold in all scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00263)\]. Similarly, Platt scaling, another popular method, is also constrained by its linear assumptions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01685)\].

Recent work has explored more flexible calibration techniques. For instance, Dirichlet calibration extends the idea of beta calibration to multiclass settings, allowing for natively multiclass calibration by transforming probabilities through a Dirichlet distribution \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8d115c3b2ee80e0754360a154a9369bc1658b607)\]. This approach is particularly useful for models that output probabilities directly, as it avoids the need for pairwise or one-vs-rest calibration. However, it still relies on a parametric form and may not fully capture the complexity of deep neural networks' output distributions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\].

Another approach, Bayesian Binning into Quantiles (BBQ), introduces a non-parametric calibration method that post-processes the output of a binary classifier, making it compatible with a wide range of algorithms \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\]. This method is computationally tractable and has shown empirical accuracy on both real and simulated datasets. However, it is limited to binary classification and does not generalize directly to multiclass settings \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\].

The use of ensembles has also been explored for calibration. For example, deep ensembles provide a simple and scalable way to estimate predictive uncertainty by aggregating multiple models \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\]. This approach has been shown to produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates that are as good or better than approximate Bayesian methods \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07329)\]. However, ensembles require significant computational resources and may not be suitable for large-scale deployment \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\].

Mixup training has been shown to improve calibration by introducing label smoothing, which reduces overconfidence in predictions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02530)\]. This technique generates additional training samples by linearly combining pairs of images and their labels, leading to better-calibrated models on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)\]. However, the benefits of mixup training are primarily observed in image classification tasks, and its applicability to other domains remains an open question \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04977)\].

### <a id='category-model-uncertainty-estimation'></a>Model Uncertainty Estimation: 11 papers found.
Model uncertainty estimation is a critical area in deep learning, aiming to provide reliable measures of confidence in model predictions. Traditional approaches, such as Bayesian neural networks, offer a principled framework for uncertainty quantification by learning distributions over model parameters \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. However, these methods often require significant computational resources and careful hyperparameter tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. In contrast, alternative methods like dropout-based uncertainty estimation have been proposed, where dropout is interpreted as an approximate Bayesian inference technique, enabling uncertainty estimation without explicit Bayesian modeling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. This approach has been shown to yield competitive results in terms of predictive uncertainty and model calibration \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. Additionally, methods such as mixup training have been explored, where data augmentation through convex combinations of input samples improves model calibration and reduces overconfidence in predictions, particularly on out-of-distribution data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\]. Furthermore, recent work has focused on calibrating predictive uncertainty through post-hoc calibration techniques, such as temperature scaling and Platt scaling, which can be applied to both Bayesian and non-Bayesian models to improve the reliability of uncertainty estimates \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. These methods have been evaluated across various architectures and tasks, demonstrating their effectiveness in improving calibration and robustness to distributional shifts \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. Additionally, the distinction between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty has been emphasized, with recent frameworks allowing for the modeling of both types of uncertainty in vision tasks, leading to improved performance in tasks like semantic segmentation and depth regression \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\]. Overall, the field has seen a shift towards more flexible and scalable methods that balance accuracy with uncertainty estimation, addressing the limitations of earlier approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\].

The development of non-parametric calibration techniques represents a significant advancement in improving the trustworthiness and performance of predictive models in practical settings.
