## Related Works

Event cameras, which capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis, offer significant advantages over traditional frame-based cameras, particularly in high-speed and high-dynamic-range scenarios \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cae8ffdd6e7adba915f7a001d9be56d543ace90e)\]. These sensors provide high temporal resolution, low power consumption, and high dynamic range, making them ideal for applications such as autonomous driving and robotics \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces challenges in processing and analysis, particularly due to the high bandwidth and computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\].

Prior work has explored various methods to address these challenges, including event downsampling. Most approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit adaptability to different scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. For instance, some studies have used simple thresholding to reduce the number of events, but these methods often fail to account for the structural importance of events in the scene \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. Additionally, these approaches typically require task-specific adaptation, which is not always feasible in real-time applications \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\].

To overcome these limitations, recent research has proposed more sophisticated methods for event processing. For example, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\] introduced a method that leverages transfer learning from traditional to event-based vision, demonstrating the potential of event cameras in challenging illumination conditions. Similarly, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\] proposed a novel recurrent neural network to reconstruct videos from event data, enabling the use of off-the-shelf computer vision algorithms on event-based data. This approach has shown significant improvements in image quality and performance compared to event-specific algorithms \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\].

Another line of research focuses on unsupervised learning for event-based vision. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\] proposed a framework for unsupervised learning that estimates optical flow, depth, and egomotion from event streams, without requiring labeled data. This method uses a discretized volume representation of events and trains neural networks to predict motion parameters, which can then be used to reduce motion blur in event images \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. This work highlights the potential of self-supervised learning in event-based vision, where labeled data is scarce \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\].

Additionally, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08336)\] introduced a dataset and simulator for event-based vision, which includes both real and synthetic event data, enabling the development and testing of new algorithms. This dataset has been used in several studies, including \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\], \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\], and \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\], to evaluate the performance of event-based vision systems in various tasks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08336)\].
