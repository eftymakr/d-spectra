## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has become the de facto standard for ensuring privacy in data analysis \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4ce10063cd25447dcde75c2d9ce327446ced952)\]. However, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, particularly in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. For instance, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. This issue has been explored in several works that examine the robustness of DP mechanisms against adversarial manipulation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. 

Local differential privacy (LDP) is a variant of DP where users perturb their data before sending it to the data collector, ensuring privacy even if the collector is untrusted \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. LDP has been widely studied and deployed in industry settings, such as telemetry data collection \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. However, LDP protocols are susceptible to manipulation attacks, where adversaries can alter their inputs to skew the results \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. For example, data poisoning attacks can inject fake users to manipulate the output of LDP protocols \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These attacks highlight the need for additional verification mechanisms to ensure the correctness of the execution of DP protocols \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\].

To address these issues, recent work has proposed verifiable differential privacy (VDP) frameworks that incorporate additional verification steps to ensure that the execution of DP mechanisms is faithful \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This idea is closely related to the concept of verifiable distributed differential privacy (VDDP), which we introduce in this paper. VDDP extends the notion of DP by incorporating verification mechanisms that allow a third party (verifier) to confirm that the data collector has followed the protocol correctly \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This approach is inspired by the use of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) in cryptographic protocols, which allow one party to prove the validity of a statement without revealing any additional information \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as our work demonstrates \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\].

Several LDP protocols have been proposed to improve the utility of privacy-preserving data collection. For example, RAPPOR uses randomized response to collect statistics on sensitive data while preserving privacy \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1407.6981)\]. Similarly, the Discrete Gaussian mechanism has been introduced to address the practical challenges of using continuous distributions in DP \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. These mechanisms have been evaluated for their accuracy and privacy guarantees, showing that they can achieve comparable or even better performance than existing methods \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\].

### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-(ldp)-protocols'></a>Local Differential Privacy (LDP) Protocols: 5 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) protocols have gained significant traction in industry applications due to their ability to protect user privacy while allowing for the collection of useful aggregate statistics. These protocols operate under the assumption that data is privatized at the user level before being sent to a central server, ensuring that individual contributions cannot be directly inferred \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. A fundamental building block of LDP systems is the frequency oracle (FO) protocol, which estimates the frequency of values while preserving privacy. However, early FO protocols often resulted in suboptimal accuracy, especially when handling complex queries such as frequencies of subsets or most frequent values \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Recent advancements have focused on improving the accuracy of LDP mechanisms by incorporating post-processing steps that leverage the non-negativity and normalization constraints of frequency distributions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, LDP has been applied to telemetry data collection, where repeated counter data, such as daily app usage, requires mechanisms that maintain privacy guarantees over long periods \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. The development of new LDP mechanisms has enabled comparable or improved accuracy for tasks like mean and histogram estimation, even with repeated data collection \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. Furthermore, new mechanisms such as hashed K-ary Randomized Response (KRR) have been introduced to enhance utility while maintaining strong privacy guarantees, often outperforming existing methods across various privacy levels \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to balance privacy and utility in LDP protocols, making them increasingly viable for real-world deployment.

### <a id='category-manipulation-attacks-on-ldp'></a>Manipulation Attacks on LDP: 3 papers found.
The category "Manipulation Attacks on LDP" explores vulnerabilities in local differential privacy (LDP) protocols where adversaries can manipulate data to distort the accuracy of aggregated results. These attacks exploit the fact that LDP mechanisms rely on users perturbing their data before sending it to a data collector, making them susceptible to malicious behavior such as injecting fake users or altering outputs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. For instance, it has been shown that a small fraction of users can significantly obscure the true distribution of honest users' data, especially when privacy levels are high or domain sizes are large \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. This vulnerability is further exacerbated by the fact that attackers can craft inputs to skew estimations, effectively controlling the outcomes of frequency estimation or heavy hitter identification tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. To counteract these threats, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms that allow data collectors to verify the correctness of the randomization process, thereby preventing output-manipulation attacks \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These approaches aim to enhance the robustness of LDP protocols without compromising their privacy guarantees, offering a promising direction for improving the security of LDP in practice.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-(dp)-fundamentals'></a>Differential Privacy (DP) Fundamentals: 11 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy (DP) Fundamentals" encompasses foundational research on differential privacy, including its theoretical underpinnings, practical implementations, and extensions to various applications. Early work established differential privacy as a rigorous framework for privacy in data analysis, ensuring that the output of a mechanism is insensitive to individual data points \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. This was further refined with the introduction of (ε, δ)-differential privacy, which allows for a relaxed definition that provides more flexibility in practical scenarios \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/0803.3946)\]. The use of Gaussian noise to perturb query results has been a central technique, though challenges arise from the continuous nature of such distributions, particularly in discrete data settings \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. To address this, the discrete Gaussian distribution was introduced, offering comparable privacy and accuracy guarantees while improving interpretability \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. Additionally, the framework has been extended to support more complex tasks, such as machine learning, where deep neural networks can be trained under differential privacy constraints with manageable trade-offs in model quality and computational efficiency \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. The local model of differential privacy, which does not require a trusted data collector, has been widely adopted in commercial applications, though it comes at the cost of reduced utility and algorithmic expressibility compared to the central model \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. Recent advancements have focused on hybrid approaches, such as Cryptε, which combines the benefits of both models by using encrypted data and untrusted servers to achieve high accuracy and privacy \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of differential privacy, addressing both theoretical and practical challenges in its implementation.

### <a id='category-privacy-preserving-data-collection-techniques'></a>Privacy-Preserving Data Collection Techniques: 2 papers found.
The category "Privacy-Preserving Data Collection Techniques" encompasses methodologies designed to collect and analyze data while ensuring user privacy. These techniques aim to balance the utility of data with the protection of individual information, often leveraging cryptographic and statistical methods. For instance, the shuffled model of differential privacy offers a middle ground between the local and central models, enabling greater accuracy than the local model without requiring trust in a central server \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Similarly, RAPPOR introduces a novel approach to anonymized data collection, allowing for the analysis of client-side data with strong privacy guarantees, ensuring that individual contributions cannot be traced \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance privacy while maintaining the effectiveness of data-driven analyses in distributed environments.
