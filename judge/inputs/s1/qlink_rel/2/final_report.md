## Related Works

Nonverbal communication plays a crucial role in human interaction, encompassing gestures, facial expressions, and body language that convey intent and emotion \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00837)\]. However, existing large language models (LLMs) have limited capabilities in incorporating these nonverbal elements, thereby constraining their ability to create immersive conversational experiences \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00837)\]. This section reviews related works that address multimodal understanding, nonverbal communication, and the integration of visual and textual modalities in language models.

Multimodal language models have been extensively studied, with a focus on integrating multiple modalities such as text, images, and audio \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09406)\]. Early approaches often relied on late fusion strategies, where features from different modalities were combined at the final stage \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09406)\]. More recent works, such as the Multimodal Transformer (MulT), have introduced end-to-end architectures that handle non-aligned multimodal time-series data by using directional pairwise crossmodal attention \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00295)\]. This approach enables the model to capture interactions between modalities across different time steps, improving performance on tasks such as sentiment analysis and visual question answering \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00295)\].

### <a id='category-multimodal-language-models'></a>Multimodal Language Models: 9 papers found.
The development of multimodal language models (MLLMs) has gained significant traction, driven by the need to better capture the complexities of human communication, which often involves multiple modalities such as text, images, and nonverbal cues \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. Early approaches, such as the Multimodal Transformer (MulT), aimed to address challenges like data non-alignment and long-range dependencies by introducing directional pairwise crossmodal attention, enabling end-to-end processing without explicit alignment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00295)\]. This was further advanced by models like Kosmos-1, which demonstrated the ability to understand and generate content across modalities, including OCR-free NLP tasks and vision-based reasoning, while supporting zero-shot and few-shot learning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. The emergence of models like GPT-4 and MiniGPT-4 highlighted the potential of aligning visual features with large language models to achieve advanced multi-modal generation capabilities, such as image description, website creation, and even story writing \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.10592)\]. However, these models still face challenges in accurately capturing nonverbal perception, as evidenced by benchmarks like Blink, which revealed significant gaps in tasks requiring visual reasoning and depth estimation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have also focused on creating comprehensive evaluation frameworks, such as the MME benchmark, which assesses MLLMs across 14 subtasks, emphasizing both perception and cognition \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. Additionally, models like Qwen-VL have been developed with specialized architectures to enhance visual understanding and grounding, achieving state-of-the-art results in tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07332)\]. These advancements collectively underscore the ongoing efforts to improve multimodal understanding and generation, aligning with the goals of MARS, which aims to integrate nonverbal cues into conversational AI through large-scale annotated datasets like VENUS \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\].
