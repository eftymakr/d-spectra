## Related Works

The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) with pre-trained language models (PLMs) has emerged as a critical research direction in natural language processing (NLP). Early work focused on improving link prediction in KGs using embedding-based approaches such as TransE \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6575)\], which models relations as translations between entities in a semantic space. However, these models often fail to capture complex relational patterns \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10197)\]. Subsequent models like TransH \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6575)\] and TransR \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/994afdf0db0cb0456f4f76468380822c2f532726)\] attempted to address this by introducing separate spaces for entities and relations, allowing for more nuanced modeling of entity relationships. These approaches laid the foundation for more sophisticated embedding techniques, such as DistMult \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103)\], which was later improved by R-GCNs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103)\] through graph-based message passing. R-GCNs demonstrated significant improvements in link prediction tasks, achieving a 29.8% improvement on the FB15k-237 dataset over decoder-only baselines \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103)\].

The limitations of traditional KG embedding methods have also been addressed through the development of convolutional models such as ConvE \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01476)\], which outperformed existing models in terms of parameter efficiency and performance on standard benchmarks. ConvE's ability to model high-indegree nodes effectively made it particularly suitable for large-scale knowledge graphs like Freebase and YAGO3 \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01476)\]. These advancements in KG embedding have been complemented by the rise of pre-trained language models (PLMs), which excel at capturing contextual and semantic information from unstructured text. ### <a id='category-integration-of-language-models-with-knowledge-graphs'></a>Integration of Language Models with Knowledge Graphs: 3 papers found.
The integration of language models with knowledge graphs (KGs) has emerged as a critical area of research, aiming to combine the strengths of both technologies. Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT and GPT4 offer strong generalization and emergent abilities but often lack precise factual knowledge due to their black-box nature \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. In contrast, KGs like Wikipedia and Huapu provide structured, explicit representations of factual knowledge, enhancing LLMs through external knowledge injection and improving interpretability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. However, KGs face challenges in dynamic knowledge evolution and fact generation, necessitating complementary approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed frameworks such as KG-enhanced LLMs, which integrate KGs during pre-training and inference to improve knowledge understanding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. Additionally, LLM-augmented KGs leverage LLMs for tasks such as embedding, completion, and graph-to-text generation, enhancing KG capabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. Recent work like KEPLER has further advanced this integration by combining pre-trained language representations with knowledge embedding, achieving state-of-the-art results on NLP tasks and link prediction \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103)\]. Similarly, models like QA-GNN have been developed to address the challenges of joint reasoning between LLMs and KGs, using relevance scoring and graph-based message passing to improve question-answering performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03975)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in unifying LLMs and KGs to enable more robust, interpretable, and factually accurate AI systems.

### <a id='category-knowledge-graph-embedding-techniques'></a>Knowledge Graph Embedding Techniques: 6 papers found.
Knowledge graph embedding techniques have been extensively explored for link prediction and entity classification tasks, with various models proposed to address the challenges of multi-relational data. Early approaches such as TransE and TransH focused on translating entities using linear or bilinear mappings, but they often struggled with modeling complex relational patterns \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03975)\]. More recent methods, like Relational Graph Convolutional Networks (R-GCNs), have been developed to handle the high complexity of knowledge bases by incorporating graph convolutional operations that aggregate information across multiple inference steps \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. These models have shown significant improvements, particularly on datasets like FB15k-237, where they outperformed decoder-only baselines by up to 29.8% \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. Similarly, ConvE, a multi-layer convolutional network, has demonstrated state-of-the-art results while being highly parameter efficient, achieving comparable performance to models like DistMult and R-GCN with fewer parameters \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01476)\]. Another notable approach, TransR, introduces separate entity and relation spaces, allowing for more nuanced modeling of entities and their relationships by projecting entities into relation-specific spaces before applying translation \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/994afdf0db0cb0456f4f76468380822c2f532726)\]. Additionally, the RotatE model extends this by modeling relation patterns such as symmetry, inversion, and composition using complex vector spaces and rotation operations \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10197)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of knowledge graph embedding techniques, with a growing emphasis on scalability, expressiveness, and the ability to capture diverse relational semantics.
