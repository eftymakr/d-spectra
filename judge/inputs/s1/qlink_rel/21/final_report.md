## Related Works

Video frame interpolation has been a focal point in video processing research, with numerous methods proposed to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones. Traditional approaches typically involve two stages: motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, these methods often suffer from computational inefficiency and poor performance in the presence of complex motion or occlusion \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10967)\]. Recent advances have shifted towards end-to-end deep learning approaches that combine motion estimation and synthesis into a single process \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. For instance, Video Frame Interpolation via Adaptive Separable Convolution \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\] introduces a method that uses local separable convolutions to reduce parameter count and improve efficiency. Similarly, Super SloMo \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\] proposes an end-to-end convolutional neural network for multi-frame interpolation, leveraging bi-directional optical flow and visibility maps to handle motion boundaries and occlusions effectively.

The use of optical flow remains a common strategy in video interpolation, but its limitations in handling large motion and occlusion have led to alternative approaches. For example, Depth-Aware Video Frame Interpolation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\] explicitly detects occlusion by incorporating depth information, which improves the quality of interpolated frames. Additionally, the work in \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11762)\] introduces a temporally adaptive multi-frame interpolator that handles complex motion segments using an advanced motion model and a pyramidal style network. This method is particularly effective for generating temporally consistent frames in high-resolution videos.

Another significant development is the introduction of perceptual losses for training deep neural networks, which helps produce visually pleasing results \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.08155)\]. This concept has been applied to video frame interpolation, as seen in the work of \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\], where a channel attention mechanism is used to replace optical flow computation, leading to improved performance in challenging scenarios. Similarly, the use of adversarial training and cycle consistency loss has been explored in \[[20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/380f46cd24a15271036ff49e9e0d91f39f89d8b4)\], where the cycle consistency loss enhances interpolation results by ensuring that synthesized frames can reconstruct the input frames with high quality.

Furthermore, the integration of context-aware synthesis has been proposed to improve the quality of interpolated frames. For instance, Context-Aware Synthesis for Video Frame Interpolation \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10967)\] warps not only the input frames but also their contextual information, enabling more accurate interpolation in the presence of occlusion and large motion. This approach is complemented by methods like PhaseNet \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\], which uses a phase-based motion representation to handle challenging scenarios with lighting changes or motion blur.

End-to-end deep learning approaches for video frame interpolation have gained significant attention due to their ability to combine motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a single process, thereby improving efficiency and performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Traditional methods often rely on optical flow estimation, which can introduce errors and computational overhead, especially in scenarios with large motion or occlusion \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Recent works have proposed deep neural networks that directly estimate spatially adaptive convolution kernels for each pixel, allowing for simultaneous kernel estimation and frame synthesis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. These models leverage the power of convolutional networks to learn complex patterns from raw pixel data, eliminating the need for explicit optical flow computation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. For instance, one approach uses local separable convolutions with 1D kernels, which significantly reduce the number of parameters compared to traditional 2D kernels, enabling efficient training and inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Another method introduces a feature reshaping operation, such as PixelShuffle, combined with channel attention mechanisms, to extract motion information at the pixel level without relying on optical flow \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Additionally, some studies have explored the use of deep networks trained end-to-end on large video datasets, demonstrating their ability to handle challenging scenarios like blur, occlusion, and abrupt brightness changes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. These methods not only improve the quality of interpolated frames but also enable the incorporation of perceptual loss functions to enhance visual fidelity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Overall, these approaches represent a shift towards more robust and efficient video frame interpolation techniques that can be applied to a wide range of real-world scenarios.

In this work, we propose a differentiable rendering formulation for implicit shape and texture representations.
