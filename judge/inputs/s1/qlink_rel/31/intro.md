## Related Works

Recent advances in deep learning have enabled significant progress in various domains, including natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision. In NLP, pre-training models like BERT \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] have demonstrated that bidirectional transformers can learn robust language representations. Similarly, in computer vision, self-supervised learning approaches such as SimCLR \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\] and MoCo \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\] have shown that contrastive learning can produce effective visual representations without explicit supervision. These methods often rely on large-scale unlabeled data and leverage techniques like contrastive predictive coding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\] to learn meaningful features.

In the field of genomics, pre-training models have also been explored. DNABERT \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c43d9cade31600400a0f62beb5bbcc1b548e009e)\] is a pre-trained bidirectional encoder representation for DNA sequences, designed to capture global and transferable understanding of genomic data. It leverages both upstream and downstream nucleotide contexts to improve performance on sequence prediction tasks. However, pure DNA sequence pre-training may lack sufficient information to fully capture the regulatory mechanisms that govern gene expression. This is because DNA function is influenced by genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility, which are not directly encoded in the DNA sequence \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c43d9cade31600400a0f62beb5bbcc1b548e009e)\].

To address this limitation, some studies have proposed integrating genomic profiles into the pre-training process. For example, DeepSEA \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e018d3883c82c9f85a4c8b7698c3fe52cc40eb39)\] uses deep learning to predict the effects of noncoding variants by learning a regulatory sequence code from chromatin-profiling data. This approach enables the model to capture the complex relationships between DNA sequences and their functional outcomes. Similarly, Enformer \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e12e837cb2e9baeaefdcab06fe1c75add8f46389)\] improves gene expression prediction by integrating long-range interactions in the genome, demonstrating the importance of considering broader genomic context.

In addition to these methods, contrastive learning has also been applied to genomics. The work by \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\] explores the use of spatial context for unsupervised visual representation learning, which has parallels in the genomic domain where long-range interactions are critical. Furthermore, the study by \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\] demonstrates that solving jigsaw puzzles can be used to pre-train features suitable for object detection and classification, suggesting that similar techniques could be adapted for genomic data.

Our approach, SPACE, builds upon these advancements by introducing a supervised training paradigm for genomic profile prediction. This method leverages the multi-species and multi-profile nature of genomic data, using a Mixture of Experts (MoE) architecture to better capture the relationships between DNA sequences across different species and genomic profiles \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c43d9cade31600400a0f62beb5bbcc1b548e009e)\]. By incorporating supervised learning, we aim to overcome the limitations of pure sequence pre-training and achieve more effective DNA representations.
