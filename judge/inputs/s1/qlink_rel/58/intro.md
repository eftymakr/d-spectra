## Related Works

Table discovery and semantic parsing have seen significant advancements in recent years, driven by the increasing availability of structured and semi-structured data. Early approaches focused on retrieving tables based on keyword matches or schema similarity, often leading to noisy or incomplete results \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00305)\]. These methods typically rely on hand-crafted features or simple heuristics, which are limited in their ability to handle complex, real-world scenarios \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08314)\]. More recently, researchers have explored the use of semantic parsing to better understand user intent and refine search results based on natural language queries \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01949)\].

Semantic parsing has been extensively studied in the context of question answering over structured data, such as databases and tables. Compositional semantic parsing, in particular, has been shown to improve the breadth and depth of knowledge representation by leveraging logical forms and strong typing constraints \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00305)\]. This approach has been applied to tasks such as answering complex questions on semi-structured tables, where the challenge lies in handling open-ended relations and combinatorial complexity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00305)\]. A notable dataset in this area is the WikiTableQuestions benchmark, which includes 22,033 complex questions on Wikipedia tables \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.00305)\].

Pretrained language models have also played a crucial role in advancing semantic parsing and table understanding. TaBERT, for instance, is a pretrained model that jointly learns representations for natural language sentences and structured tables, enabling better reasoning over both modalities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08314)\]. This model is trained on a large corpus of 26 million tables and their English contexts, making it well-suited for tasks that require understanding of both textual and tabular data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08314)\]. Neural semantic parsers using TaBERT have achieved state-of-the-art results on the WikiTableQuestions benchmark, demonstrating the potential of pretrained models in this domain \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.08314)\].

In addition to semantic parsing, the task of table detection and recognition has gained traction, especially in the context of document analysis and information retrieval. TableBank is a large-scale dataset designed for image-based table detection and recognition, built using weak supervision from Word and LaTeX documents \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01949)\]. This dataset contains 417K high-quality labeled tables and has been used to develop strong baselines using deep neural networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01949)\]. The availability of such datasets has enabled more robust evaluation of table discovery methods, particularly in real-world scenarios where data is noisy or incomplete \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.01949)\].

The nlcTD task proposed in this paper builds upon these advancements by combining natural language conditions with table queries to refine search results. This task is distinct from previous approaches, which often rely on static or limited query types \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08887)\].
