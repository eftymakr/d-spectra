Early methods relied on matrix factorization to learn latent representations of users and items \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. However, these methods often fail to capture the collaborative signal inherent in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Recent advances have shifted focus towards graph-based approaches, which explicitly model the bipartite user-item interaction graph \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. These models leverage graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to propagate embeddings through the graph, capturing high-order connectivity patterns \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. 

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant improvements with the introduction of models like Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. NGCF explicitly integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process, leading to more accurate representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, training GCN-based models for recommendation remains challenging due to non-linear activation functions and the over-smoothing problem \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. ### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering-models'></a>Graph-Based Collaborative Filtering Models: 4 papers found.
Graph-based collaborative filtering models have gained significant attention in recommender systems due to their ability to effectively capture collaborative signals through user-item interaction graphs. Early methods, such as matrix factorization, relied on pre-defined features to generate embeddings, but they often failed to incorporate the rich collaborative information inherent in the graph structure \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. To address this limitation, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) was introduced, which explicitly integrates the bipartite graph structure into the embedding process by propagating embeddings across the graph to model high-order connectivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, GCN-based models, such as NGCF, often suffer from training difficulties due to non-linear activation functions and the over-smoothing problem in deep layers \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. In response, LightGCN was proposed as a simplified version of GCN, focusing only on the essential neighborhood aggregation operation, which significantly improves performance and training efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, other works have explored ways to enhance graph-based models by addressing issues such as non-linear activation and over-smoothing, including the use of residual networks to alleviate the over-smoothing effect \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Additionally, recent studies have emphasized the importance of disentangling user intents in collaborative filtering, leading to the development of models like Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF), which aims to capture diverse user intents through intent-aware interaction graphs \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of graph-based collaborative filtering models.

### <a id='category-node-embedding-techniques-for-graphs'></a>Node Embedding Techniques for Graphs: 6 papers found.
Graph embedding techniques have gained significant attention for their ability to learn low-dimensional representations of nodes in complex networks, enabling effective prediction and analysis in various domains. These methods often rely on aggregating information from a node's neighborhood to capture structural and semantic relationships \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. For instance, GraphSAGE introduces an inductive framework that generates embeddings for unseen nodes by sampling and aggregating features from local neighborhoods, overcoming the limitations of transductive methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Similarly, DeepWalk leverages truncated random walks to learn latent representations, treating walks as sentences in a language model, which allows for capturing both local and global structures in graphs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Node2vec extends this idea by introducing a flexible notion of neighborhood through biased random walks, enabling the exploration of diverse network structures and leading to richer representations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. Additionally, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been widely adopted for their ability to encode both local graph structure and node features, with variants like Graph Convolutional Networks and GraphSAGE achieving state-of-the-art results in node and graph classification tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. However, despite their success, there remains limited understanding of their expressive power and limitations, highlighting the need for further theoretical analysis \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. These approaches collectively demonstrate the importance of neighborhood aggregation and the challenges of capturing complex graph structures, laying the foundation for more advanced and efficient embedding techniques.
