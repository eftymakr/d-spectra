## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods relied on matrix factorization to learn latent representations of users and items \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. However, these methods often fail to capture the collaborative signal inherent in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Recent advances have shifted focus towards graph-based approaches, which explicitly model the bipartite user-item interaction graph \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. These models leverage graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to propagate embeddings through the graph, capturing high-order connectivity patterns \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. 

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant improvements with the introduction of models like Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. NGCF explicitly integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process, leading to more accurate representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, training GCN-based models for recommendation remains challenging due to non-linear activation functions and the over-smoothing problem \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. To address these issues, LightGCN simplifies the GCN architecture by removing non-linearities and focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation, achieving substantial improvements over NGCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. 

Another line of work explores the use of variational autoencoders (VAEs) for collaborative filtering, extending them to handle implicit feedback \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. These models introduce a generative framework with multinomial likelihood and Bayesian inference, leading to better performance compared to linear factor models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. Additionally, the BPR algorithm optimizes models for personalized ranking by directly maximizing the posterior probability of user preferences \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\]. This approach outperforms standard learning techniques for matrix factorization and kNN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\].

Deep learning has also been applied to recommendation tasks, with models like Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) using multi-layer perceptrons to learn non-linear user-item interaction functions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. This framework generalizes matrix factorization and achieves significant improvements on real-world datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. Meanwhile, DeepWalk and node2vec have been used to learn latent representations of nodes in networks, treating walks as sentences to capture social relations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]\[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. These methods have shown effectiveness in multi-label classification and link prediction tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]\[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\].

In the context of recommendation systems, recent work has also focused on self-supervised learning to improve the robustness of GCNs \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. By introducing auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning, these methods enhance the accuracy and stability of recommendations, particularly for long-tail items \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\].
