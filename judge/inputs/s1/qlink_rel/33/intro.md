## Related Works

Catastrophic forgetting remains a central challenge in continual learning (CL), where models must learn sequentially from a stream of tasks without forgetting previously acquired knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Early approaches to mitigate this issue relied on replaying past data during training, a strategy that has since been refined and expanded \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Rehearsal-based methods, which store and train on a subset of past data alongside new data, have shown superior performance in addressing catastrophic forgetting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. These methods are widely used, yet their optimality remains unclear \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. 

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different rehearsal strategies. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] proposed a method that leverages experience replay buffers to store past experiences, enabling the model to generalize from old data when learning new tasks. This approach is particularly useful when task boundaries are not explicitly provided, a common scenario in real-world applications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\] introduced a framework that uses a dual model architecture—comprising a deep generative model and a task-solving model—to sample and interleave past and new data, improving performance on sequential learning tasks \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. 

The importance of task similarity in determining the effectiveness of rehearsal strategies has also been explored. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17269)\] demonstrated that when tasks are less similar, sequential rehearsal—where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data—can offer greater benefits compared to concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. This insight aligns with findings from \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6211)\], which showed that models trained with dropout consistently outperformed others in retaining knowledge across tasks, suggesting that regularization techniques can play a role in mitigating forgetting \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. 

Further, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12369)\] proposed a hybrid approach that combines concurrent and sequential rehearsal, training similar tasks concurrently and revisiting dissimilar tasks sequentially. This method was shown to reduce forgetting while maintaining computational efficiency \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\]. Additionally, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05922)\] introduced a memory-efficient experience replay algorithm, ExStream, which significantly reduces the memory and computational costs associated with full rehearsal while still preventing catastrophic forgetting \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\]. 

Recent work has also explored alternative strategies to rehearsal, such as knowledge distillation and parameter regularization. \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10628)\] demonstrated that L2 parameter regularization outperforms EWC in rehearsal-free continual learning, achieving strong performance on benchmarks like ImageNet-R \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07569)\]. Similarly, \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08654)\] proposed a method that learns to dynamically prompt a pre-trained model to handle sequential tasks without relying on task identity or a rehearsal buffer, showing competitive results against traditional methods
