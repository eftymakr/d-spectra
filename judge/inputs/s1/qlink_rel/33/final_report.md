## Related Works

Catastrophic forgetting remains a central challenge in continual learning (CL), where models must learn sequentially from a stream of tasks without forgetting previously acquired knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Early approaches to mitigate this issue relied on replaying past data during training, a strategy that has since been refined and expanded \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Rehearsal-based methods, which store and train on a subset of past data alongside new data, have shown superior performance in addressing catastrophic forgetting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. These methods are widely used, yet their optimality remains unclear \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. 

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different rehearsal strategies. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] proposed a method that leverages experience replay buffers to store past experiences, enabling the model to generalize from old data when learning new tasks. This approach is particularly useful when task boundaries are not explicitly provided, a common scenario in real-world applications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\] introduced a framework that uses a dual model architecture—comprising a deep generative model and a task-solving model—to sample and interleave past and new data, improving performance on sequential learning tasks \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. 

The importance of task similarity in determining the effectiveness of rehearsal strategies has also been explored. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17269)\] demonstrated that when tasks are less similar, sequential rehearsal—where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data—can offer greater benefits compared to concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. This insight aligns with findings from \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6211)\], which showed that models trained with dropout consistently outperformed others in retaining knowledge across tasks, suggesting that regularization techniques can play a role in mitigating forgetting \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. 

Further, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12369)\] proposed a hybrid approach that combines concurrent and sequential rehearsal, training similar tasks concurrently and revisiting dissimilar tasks sequentially. This method was shown to reduce forgetting while maintaining computational efficiency \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\]. Additionally, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05922)\] introduced a memory-efficient experience replay algorithm, ExStream, which significantly reduces the memory and computational costs associated with full rehearsal while still preventing catastrophic forgetting \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\]. 

Recent work has also explored alternative strategies to rehearsal, such as knowledge distillation and parameter regularization. \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10628)\] demonstrated that L2 parameter regularization outperforms EWC in rehearsal-free continual learning, achieving strong performance on benchmarks like ImageNet-R \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07569)\]. ### <a id='category-catastrophic-forgetting-in-continual-learning'></a>Catastrophic Forgetting in Continual Learning: 8 papers found.
Catastrophic forgetting, a significant challenge in continual learning, occurs when neural networks lose previously learned information upon training on new tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. This issue has been extensively studied, with various approaches proposed to mitigate it. One common strategy is rehearsal-based methods, which store and retrain on a subset of past data alongside new data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. However, the optimal approach remains unclear. Some studies suggest that sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first and past data is revisited later, may offer greater benefits, particularly when tasks are less similar \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\]. This idea is inspired by human learning, where revisiting tasks helps mitigate forgetting \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\]. Another approach involves task-based hard attention mechanisms, which preserve previous tasks' information without affecting current learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. Additionally, deep generative replay methods use a dual model architecture to sample and interleave training data for previous and new tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Task-conditioned hypernetworks, which generate weights based on task identity, have also shown promise in retaining previous memories \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to address catastrophic forgetting, though challenges remain in scalability and performance across diverse tasks \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\].

### <a id='category-experience-replay-and-memory-efficiency'></a>Experience Replay and Memory Efficiency: 5 papers found.
Experience replay and memory efficiency are critical in continual learning, where models must retain past knowledge while learning new tasks. Prior work has shown that catastrophic forgetting—a degradation of performance on old tasks after learning new ones—can be mitigated through experience replay, where past data is stored and retrained alongside new data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. This approach has been explored in both reinforcement learning and supervised settings, with methods such as reservoir sampling and controlled sampling of memories showing promise in reducing forgetting \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. In streaming learning, where data is non-iid and models must adapt continuously, rehearsal—mixing new examples with past ones—has been proposed as a solution, though full rehearsal is often memory-intensive \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05922)\]. Recent studies have also introduced biological-inspired mechanisms, such as intelligent synapses, which allow models to retain old knowledge while rapidly storing new memories \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of memory management and strategic replay in improving generalization and reducing forgetting, with ongoing research focusing on optimizing memory usage and enhancing performance across diverse learning scenarios.

In recent years, numerous methods have been proposed for continual learning, but due to differences in evaluation protocols it is difficult to directly compare their performance. Any sequence of well-defined tasks can be performed according to each scenario. [Snippet]: «Continual learning is a setting where machine learning models learn novel concepts from continuously shifting training data, while simultaneously avoiding degradation of knowledge on previously seen classes which may disappear from the training data for extended periods of time (a phenomenon known as the catastrophic forgetting problem). Current approaches for continual learning of a single expanding task (aka class-incremental continual learning) require extensive rehearsal of previously seen data to avoid this degradation of knowledge. Unfortunately, rehearsal comes at a cost to memory, and it may also violate data-privacy. Instead, we explore combining knowledge distillation and parameter regularization in new ways to achieve strong continual learning performance without rehearsal. Specifically, we take a deep dive into common continual learning techniques: prediction distillation, feature distillation, L2 parameter regularization, and EWC parameter regularization. We first disprove the common assumption that parameter regularization techniques fail for rehearsal-free continual learning of a single, expanding task. Next, we explore how to leverage knowledge from a pre-trained model in rehearsal-free continual learning and find that vanilla L2 parameter regularization outperforms EWC parameter regularization and feature distillation. Finally, we explore the recently popular ImageNet-R benchmark, and show that L2 parameter regularization implemented in self-attention blocks of a ViT transformer outperforms recent popular prompting for continual learning methods.»
