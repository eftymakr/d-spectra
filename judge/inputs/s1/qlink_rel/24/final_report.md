## Related Works

Diffusion models have become a cornerstone in generative modeling, particularly for image and video generation, due to their ability to produce high-quality, diverse outputs \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08295)\]. However, their computational and memory demands hinder their deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. To address this, post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising technique to reduce model size and accelerate inference without retraining \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. PTQ has been successfully applied to diffusion models, with methods such as Q-Diffusion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\], PTQ4DM \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\], and PTQD \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\] achieving 4-bit and 8-bit quantization while maintaining performance. These methods address the unique challenges of diffusion models, such as the changing output distributions over time steps and bimodal activation patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. 

Despite these advances, quantization of diffusion transformers (DiTs) remains a non-trivial task. DiTs, which replace traditional U-Net architectures with transformers, offer scalability and flexibility but come with high parameter counts and computational costs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. Existing PTQ methods, such as PTQ4DiT \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\], Q-DiT \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\], and HQ-DiT \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\], have made strides in quantizing DiTs to 4-bit and 8-bit precision. These approaches tackle challenges like spatial and temporal variance in activations and weights, using techniques such as channel-wise salience balancing and dynamic activation quantization. For example, PTQ4DiT \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\] introduces Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen’s ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) to improve quantization accuracy, while Q-DiT \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\] integrates automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization. 

In the realm of video generation, diffusion models face additional challenges due to the temporal coherence required in video sequences \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03458)\]. Video diffusion models, such as Stable Video Diffusion \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\] and VideoLDM \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\], have been developed to generate high-resolution, temporally consistent videos. However, these models still suffer from high computational demands, making quantization essential for deployment on edge devices. Recent work on video diffusion models has explored quantization techniques, but they often lack the specificity needed for video tasks. For instance, PTQD \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\] and Temporal Dynamic Quantization \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\] have been proposed to address the temporal variation in activation, improving output quality by dynamically adjusting quantization intervals based on time steps.

### <a id='category-post-training-quantization-techniques'></a>Post-Training Quantization Techniques: 14 papers found.
Diffusion models have seen significant advancements in image and video generation, but their high computational demands and parameter counts hinder deployment on edge devices \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12293)\]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising solution to reduce memory usage and accelerate inference without retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. However, applying existing PTQ methods to diffusion models, especially video generation models like Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), presents unique challenges due to the multi-timestep nature of the generation process and the need to preserve spatiotemporal coherence \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. For instance, quantization noise can accumulate over time steps, leading to a degradation in generated quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed specialized PTQ techniques tailored for diffusion models, such as PTQ4DM \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\], which introduces timestep-aware calibration and split shortcut quantization to maintain performance at 8-bit precision. Similarly, PTQ4DiT \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\] tackles the unique challenges of DiTs by addressing salient channel magnitudes and temporal activation variability through channel-wise salience balancing and dynamic calibration. These methods highlight the necessity of adapting PTQ strategies to the specific architecture and dynamics of diffusion models to ensure both efficiency and quality \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. Additionally, Q-DiT \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\] introduces automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization to handle the spatial and temporal variance in DiTs. These approaches demonstrate that effective quantization of diffusion models requires careful consideration of both architectural characteristics and the temporal nature of the generation process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03270)\].

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-generative-modeling'></a>Diffusion Models in Generative Modeling: 9 papers found.
Diffusion models have shown significant promise in generative modeling, particularly in image and video synthesis, with recent advancements focusing on improving efficiency and scalability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. For instance, latent diffusion models (LDMs) leverage pretrained autoencoders to operate in latent spaces, reducing computational demands while maintaining high visual fidelity \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. However, applying these models to video generation presents unique challenges, such as maintaining temporal coherence and handling the increased complexity of spatiotemporal data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. To address these issues, researchers have explored various quantization techniques to reduce model size and inference time, with methods like post-training quantization (PTQ) and quantization-aware training (QAT) being widely studied \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03270)\]. These approaches aim to preserve performance while minimizing resource usage, though they often struggle with maintaining quality in video tasks due to the dynamic nature of activation during diffusion \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. Additionally, diffusion transformers (DiTs), which replace traditional U-Net backbones with transformer architectures, offer improved scalability and performance in image generation, but their adaptation to video generation remains an active area of research \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. The proposed Q-VDiT framework specifically targets these challenges by introducing token-aware quantization and temporal maintenance distillation to enhance scene consistency and reduce computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\].
