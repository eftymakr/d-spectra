## Related Works

Long-tailed recognition remains a challenging problem in visual recognition due to the skewed class distribution in real-world datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. Prior work has primarily focused on class-balancing strategies such as loss re-weighting, data re-sampling, or transfer learning from head to tail classes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. However, these methods often suffer from poor generalization on minority classes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. 

Supervised contrastive learning (SCL) has shown promising performance on balanced data, but it fails to form a regular simplex for long-tailed data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13998)\]. This leads to imbalanced attraction and repulsion gradients between positive and negative pairs, which hinder model generalization \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.05160)\]. Several works have attempted to address these issues by introducing class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14267)\]. For example, Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) introduces parametric class-wise learnable centers to adaptively enhance the intensity of pushing samples of the same class close \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02413)\]. 

Another line of research decouples representation learning and classifier learning, showing that high-quality representations can be learned with simple instance-balanced sampling, and strong long-tailed recognition can be achieved by adjusting only the classifier \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. This approach has been further extended to decoupling the learning procedure into representation and classification, with findings suggesting that data imbalance might not be an issue in learning high-quality representations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.00466)\]. 

The problem of gradient conflicts and imbalanced attraction/repulsion gradients in SCL has also been analyzed in detail. For instance, the work by \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01809)\] shows that SCL can help improve performance, but past baselines suffer from poor uniformity in the feature space, leading to poor separability for minority classes. To address this, Targeted Supervised Contrastive Learning (TSC) was proposed, which improves the uniformity of the feature distribution on the hypersphere by generating targets uniformly distributed on a hypersphere \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16370)\]. 

Additionally, methods such as Balanced Meta-Softmax and Equalization Loss have been introduced to address the bias in gradient estimation and the discouragement of rare categories during training \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04596)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14197)\]. The Equalization Loss, for instance, protects the learning of rare categories from being at a disadvantage by ignoring gradients for rare categories during network parameter updating \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07314)\]. 

Recent work has also explored the use of domain adaptation and causal inference frameworks to address the long-tailed recognition problem. For example, the work by \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10740)\] establishes a causal inference framework that disentangles the paradoxical effects of SGD momentum

Several studies have explored methods to address this imbalance, such as modifying loss functions, adjusting gradients, and introducing new training paradigms. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\] highlights the challenges of using crossentropy minimization on long-tailed data, noting that it struggles to represent tail classes effectively. ### <a id='category-decoupled-representation-and-classifier-learning'></a>Decoupled Representation and Classifier Learning: 3 papers found.
The category "Decoupled Representation and Classifier Learning" explores strategies to address the long-tailed recognition problem by separating the learning of representations from the learning of classifiers. Existing approaches often rely on class-balancing strategies such as loss re-weighting, data re-sampling, or transfer learning, but these typically involve joint learning of representations and classifiers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. Recent work has shown that decoupling these processes can lead to more effective solutions. For instance, one study found that data imbalance may not hinder the learning of high-quality representations, and that strong long-tailed recognition can be achieved by adjusting only the classifier after learning representations with instance-balanced sampling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. Another paper highlights that class re-balancing methods, while effective in improving classifier learning, can inadvertently degrade the representational power of features, prompting the development of a Bilateral-Branch Network (BBN) that separately handles representation and classifier learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Furthermore, methods like label-aware smoothing and shifted batch normalization have been introduced to address miscalibration and dataset bias in decoupled frameworks, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmarks such as CIFAR-10-LT, ImageNet-LT, and iNaturalist \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. These findings underscore the importance of carefully designing decoupled learning strategies to enhance model performance in long-tailed scenarios.
