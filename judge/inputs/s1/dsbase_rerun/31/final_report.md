In the context of Mixture of Experts (MoE) in genomic modeling, several studies have explored the use of MoE to enhance model performance and efficiency. MoE models are particularly useful in genomic applications due to their ability to handle complex, high-dimensional data and capture diverse patterns by activating only relevant experts \[[1' 2024-10-23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17954v2)\]. However, challenges such as overfitting, sparse data allocation, and memory constraints have been identified, especially when scaling up the number of experts \[[2' 2024-11-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11158v2)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various improvements, including the use of variance-based constraints on the routing stage to encourage diverse expert learning \[[2' 2024-11-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11158v2)\], and the introduction of cluster-level expert dropout strategies to mitigate overfitting \[[2' 2024-11-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11158v2)\]. Additionally, MoE has been applied to model distributional shifts in graph data, where a gating mechanism identifies shift components and expert models produce referential representations \[[3' 2023-07-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13708v1)\]. The effectiveness of MoE in genomic modeling has also been demonstrated in tasks such as machine translation and natural language understanding, where MoE models outperform traditional approaches \[[2' 2024-11-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.11158v2)\]. Furthermore, the integration of MoE with large language models has enabled the development of interpretable and domain knowledge-aligned models, such as MoRE-LLM, which combines rule-based surrogates with LLM-derived knowledge \[[6' 2024-05-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09585v3)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility and potential of MoE in genomic modeling, particularly in handling complex data structures and improving model interpretability \[[6' 2024-05-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09585v3)\].
