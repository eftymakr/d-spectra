Economic inequality remains a critical challenge, influencing disparities in education, healthcare, and social stability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. Traditional tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, have been effective in reducing inequality but lack adaptability to changing economic conditions and heterogeneous taxpayer behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. The Saez Optimal Taxation framework has attempted to address these limitations by introducing dynamic tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. However, it fails to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior, which are essential for designing more equitable and efficient tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\].

Several studies have explored alternative approaches to taxation. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) has been examined for its impact on income distribution, showing potential to reduce inequality while maintaining productivity \[[2](http://www.nber.org/papers/w21340)\]. Similarly, the AI Economist introduces a novel approach using deep reinforcement learning to design tax policies that balance equality and productivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. Optimal taxation theory has long been a focus of economic research, with studies exploring the use of elasticities to derive optimal income tax rates \[[7](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937X.00166)\]. Similarly, research on the optimal taxation of top labor incomes emphasizes the role of three key elasticities in determining tax rates \[[8](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.6.1.230)\]. These findings align with the need for more adaptive and data-driven approaches to taxation.

Furthermore, the AI Economist shows that these policies are effective when tested with human participants, indicating their practical relevance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\].
