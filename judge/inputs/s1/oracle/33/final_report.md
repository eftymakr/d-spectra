## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a promising approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\]. This strategy has been widely adopted due to its effectiveness in mitigating forgetting, although its optimality remains an open question \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Prior work has explored various forms of rehearsal, including replaying past data during training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.09762)\], and has demonstrated that such methods can significantly improve performance on sequential tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02931)\]. However, most studies have focused on concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, without thoroughly analyzing the potential benefits of sequential rehearsal \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\].

Sequential rehearsal, inspired by human learning strategies where tasks are revisited in sequence, has been less explored in CL. Recent theoretical analyses have begun to address this gap by examining the performance of different rehearsal strategies in overparameterized linear models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]. These studies have shown that sequential rehearsal can offer advantages, particularly when tasks are less similar \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. This aligns with findings from other works that highlight the importance of task similarity in determining the effectiveness of continual learning strategies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11942)\]. For instance, the NTK overlap matrix has been introduced to quantify task similarity and its impact on catastrophic forgetting \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04003)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by leveraging prior knowledge. Progressive Neural Networks (PNNs) use lateral connections to previously learned features to avoid forgetting \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. Similarly, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) employs orthogonal gradient descent to minimize interference with past tasks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03534)\]. Trust Region Gradient Projection (TRGP) further refines this idea by selecting relevant old tasks based on gradient projections and reusing their frozen weights \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09588)\]. These methods demonstrate that careful control of the optimization process can significantly reduce forgetting while maintaining performance on new tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\].

The Deep Generative Replay framework introduces a dual model architecture to enable efficient replay of past data without requiring large memory \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\]. This approach is particularly useful in real-world applications where access to past data is limited \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06475)\]. Similarly, iCaRL introduces an incremental learning strategy that allows for class-incremental learning without requiring access to all previous data \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16939)\]. These methods highlight the importance of memory and replay in continual learning, although they often rely on either explicit memory storage or careful regularization to prevent forgetting \[[17]()\].

### <a id='category-task-similarity-and-catastrophic-forgetting'></a>Task Similarity and Catastrophic Forgetting: 10 papers found.
Catastrophic forgetting remains a significant challenge in continual learning, where models must learn sequentially without forgetting previous tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate this issue, such as progressive networks that use lateral connections to retain prior knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\], and methods that selectively slow down learning on weights critical for old tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. More recently, Trust Region Gradient Projection (TRGP) has been introduced to facilitate forward knowledge transfer by leveraging task correlation through a trust region mechanism \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02931)\]. Theoretical frameworks have also been developed to analyze continual learning algorithms, such as the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, which provides closed-form expressions for transfer learning and generalization \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11942)\]. Additionally, task similarity has been identified as a key factor influencing catastrophic forgetting, with the NTK overlap matrix serving as a measure of task alignment \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04003)\]. Overparameterization has also been shown to reduce forgetting in linear models, with theoretical bounds on forgetting performance depending on task similarity and ordering \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09588)\]. These insights have motivated hybrid rehearsal strategies that combine concurrent and sequential rehearsal based on task similarity, leading to improved performance in both theoretical and experimental settings \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\].

### <a id='category-memory-and-replay-mechanisms'></a>Memory and Replay Mechanisms: 3 papers found.
The category "Memory and Replay Mechanisms" explores approaches to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning, where models must learn sequentially without forgetting past tasks. Existing methods often rely on replaying past data, either by storing it explicitly or generating synthetic samples \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. One approach, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM), uses singular value decomposition to identify and store important gradient subspaces from past tasks, enabling orthogonal gradient descent that minimizes interference with prior knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.09762)\]. Another method, Deep Generative Replay, leverages a dual model architecture—comprising a generator and a solver—to sample and interleave past task data with new tasks, reducing the need for large memory storage \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Additionally, Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) introduces a framework that allows beneficial knowledge transfer while minimizing forgetting by episodically revisiting past tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. These methods highlight the importance of memory and replay in continual learning, with varying strategies to balance performance and efficiency. The proposed Hybrid Rehearsal method, inspired by human learning, further suggests that sequential rehearsal can be more effective for dissimilar tasks, complementing existing approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\].

### <a id='category-regularization-techniques-for-continual-learning'></a>Regularization Techniques for Continual Learning: 3 papers found.
The category "Regularization Techniques for Continual Learning" explores methods to prevent catastrophic forgetting in neural networks when learning sequentially from a stream of data. A key approach is the iCaRL method, which enables class-incremental learning by maintaining a small subset of past data and progressively adding new classes, while learning strong classifiers and data representations simultaneously \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]. This contrasts with earlier methods that were limited to fixed data representations and incompatible with deep learning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]. Another line of research focuses on regularization-based approaches, which aim to alleviate catastrophic forgetting by approximating the loss function using second-order Taylor expansions, leading to a unified framework that encompasses methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation and Kronecker factored Laplace approximation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. Theoretical analysis of regularization-based continual learning highlights the importance of accurate Hessian matrix approximation for convergence and generalization \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. Additionally, studies on continual learning in linear models show that weak regularization can be equivalent to solving a sequential max-margin problem, aligning with the Projection Onto Convex Sets (POCS) framework, and provide upper bounds on forgetting under various task ordering scenarios \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03534)\]. These works collectively emphasize the role of regularization in maintaining performance across tasks while learning incrementally.
