## Related Works

Speech-driven facial animation has been a focal point in recent research, with various approaches aiming to generate realistic 3D facial expressions that align with speech input. Early methods often relied on phoneme-level feature extraction and deterministic models, which limited the expressiveness and flexibility of generated animations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. For instance, FaceFormer introduced a Transformer-based autoregressive model to encode long-term audio context and predict 3D face meshes, improving lip synchronization but still facing challenges in emotional expressiveness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Similarly, CodeTalker addressed the regression-to-mean problem by formulating the task as a code query in a learned codebook space, promoting more vivid and realistic facial motions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

The importance of emotional expressiveness in facial animation has also been emphasized. Emotional Speech-Driven Animation with Content-Emotion Disentanglement proposed EMOTE, which decouples speech and emotion during training, allowing for explicit control over emotional expressions while maintaining lip synchronization \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. This approach was further refined in EmoTalk, which introduced an emotion disentangling encoder to separate content and emotion in speech signals, enabling more natural and controllable facial expressions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. These works highlight the growing recognition of the need for emotional disentanglement in generating expressive facial animations.

In addition to emotional expressiveness, the issue of lip synchronization and the averaging effect of phonetically similar syllables has been a significant challenge. FaceXHuBERT leveraged self-supervised speech representation learning to incorporate both lexical and non-lexical information, improving robustness to background noise and handling audio from varied recording conditions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05416)\]. This approach was further enhanced by the use of HuBERT, which masked speech inputs in the latent space and solved a contrastive task over quantized representations, leading to better speech recognition performance with limited labeled data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07447)\]. These models laid the groundwork for more robust and expressive audio encodings.

Recent work has also focused on non-deterministic models to capture the variability in facial expressions. FaceDiffuser introduced a diffusion-based approach to generate speech-driven facial animations, allowing for non-deterministic outputs that better reflect the natural variability in facial cues \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11306)\]. This was further extended by 3DiFACE, which proposed a lightweight audio-conditioned diffusion model for 3D facial motion, enabling stochasticity and motion editing \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00870)\]. These models address the inherent one-to-many relationship between speech and facial expressions, offering greater flexibility in animation generation.

Speech-driven facial animation techniques have seen significant advancements, with recent works focusing on improving realism, emotional expressiveness, and lip synchronization. Existing methods often rely on pre-trained self-supervised audio models as encoders, but they struggle with phonetically similar syllables that produce distinct lip shapes, leading to an averaging effect in lip motion generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed novel approaches such as emotion controller modules that enable controllable facial expressions based on emotional intensity and type \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008)\], and Transformer-based models like FaceFormer that encode long-term audio context for more accurate 3D facial animation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Additionally, some studies have introduced code query tasks and temporal autoregressive models to enhance the vividness and realism of generated facial motions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\]. Others have emphasized the importance of emotional control, such as the EMOTE model, which allows explicit emotion regulation while maintaining lip-sync with speech \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. Furthermore, the development of large-scale 4D facial datasets, such as the one used in VOCA, has enabled more realistic and generalized facial animation across diverse subjects and languages \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. Recent works also focus on disentangling speaking styles and semantic content to improve the realism and adaptability of generated animations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10877)\]. These efforts collectively aim to overcome the challenges of data scarcity, cross-modal mapping uncertainty, and emotional expressiveness in speech-driven facial animation.

### <a id='category-self-supervised-speech-representation-learning'></a>Self-Supervised Speech Representation Learning: 4 papers found.
In the field of self-supervised speech representation learning, recent advancements have focused on improving the ability to capture both lexical and non-lexical information from speech without relying on extensive annotated data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07447)\]. For instance, FaceXHuBERT leverages a self-supervised pretrained HuBERT model to generate 3D facial animations, enabling the incorporation of subtle cues like identity, emotion, and hesitation without the need for a large lexicon \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05416)\]. Similarly, the Deep Speech system demonstrates the effectiveness of end-to-end deep learning in speech recognition, eliminating the need for hand-designed components to model background noise or speaker variation \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.5567)\]. Additionally, wav2vec 2.0 has shown that learning powerful speech representations from unlabeled data followed by fine-tuning on transcribed speech can outperform semi-supervised methods, achieving significant improvements in word error rates even with limited labeled data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11477)\]. The HuBERT approach further advances this by addressing challenges in self-supervised learning through an offline clustering step, aligning target labels for a BERT-like prediction loss and improving performance on benchmarks like Librispeech \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07447)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of self-supervised methods in enhancing the expressivity and robustness of speech-driven applications.
