Protein conformation sampling and structure prediction have long relied on physics-based simulations such as molecular dynamics (MD) and Monte Carlo (MC) methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. These approaches, while accurate, are computationally intensive and often struggle with rare event sampling and long equilibration times \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. To address these limitations, recent advances in deep learning have introduced generative modeling techniques, particularly denoising diffusion models, which enable efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven models remains a challenge, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\].

Several studies have explored the use of deep learning for protein structure prediction. For instance, EigenFold employs diffusion models to generate a distribution of structures from a given protein sequence, modeling the structure as a system of harmonic oscillators and inducing a cascading-resolution generative process along the eigenmodes of the system \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. ### <a id='category-conformation-sampling-techniques'></a>Conformation Sampling Techniques: 2 papers found.
Conformation sampling techniques have seen significant advancements through the integration of deep learning and statistical mechanics, enabling more efficient and accurate generation of equilibrium samples for complex systems like proteins. Traditional methods, such as molecular dynamics simulations, are computationally intensive and often require extensive sampling to capture rare events or diverse conformations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have developed Boltzmann Generators, which utilize neural networks to learn coordinate transformations that allow for the direct sampling of equilibrium distributions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. This approach bypasses the need for extensive simulation steps and enables the accurate computation of free energy differences and the discovery of new configurations without prior knowledge of reaction coordinates \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. Similarly, the Distributional Graphormer (DiG) framework leverages deep learning to predict equilibrium distributions by conditioning on molecular descriptors such as chemical graphs or protein sequences, inspired by thermodynamic annealing processes \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. DiG not only generates diverse conformations but also provides state density estimations, offering a powerful tool for understanding molecular systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. These methods represent a shift towards data-driven approaches that incorporate physical principles, enhancing the statistical understanding of molecular systems and enabling applications in structural biology and drug discovery.
