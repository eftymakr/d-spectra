## Related Works

Video frame interpolation has been a focal point in video processing research, with numerous methods proposed to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones. Traditional approaches typically involve two stages: motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, these methods are often limited by the accuracy of optical flow estimation and the computational complexity of synthesizing intermediate frames \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent advances have shifted toward end-to-end deep learning approaches that combine motion estimation and synthesis into a single process \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\].

Several works have explored the use of adaptive convolutional networks for video frame interpolation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\] introduced a method that considers pixel synthesis as a local convolution over two input frames, allowing the network to capture both motion and synthesis in a single step. This approach eliminates the need for explicit optical flow estimation and enables robust handling of challenges like occlusion and blur \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\] proposed a separable convolution approach that reduces the number of parameters required to estimate motion and synthesis, leading to more efficient models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\].

The use of deep neural networks for video frame interpolation has also led to the development of models that can handle large motions and complex scenes. For example, \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\] introduced a method that formulates motion estimation as a classification task rather than regression, leading to improved performance on high-resolution datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12622)\]. Additionally, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\] proposed a dynamic architecture that assigns varying amounts of computation to different regions of the video, significantly reducing computational costs while maintaining performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\].

Another line of research focuses on the integration of perceptual loss functions to improve the visual quality of interpolated frames. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\] introduced a method that incorporates perceptual loss to train the neural network to produce visually pleasing frames, while \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00627)\] proposed a technique that uses a flow reversal layer to estimate flow fields starting from the unknown target frame to the source frame, improving the accuracy of interpolation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\].

Moreover, the use of deep learning for video compression and view synthesis has also been explored. \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3a6e72172b7e1fb3b45f53e9aaf7d42e427c26de)\] presented an end-to-end deep learning codec that treats video compression as repeated image interpolation, benefiting from recent advances in deep image interpolation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. Similarly, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\] proposed a motion estimation and compensation-driven neural network that integrates both optical flow and interpolation kernels to synthesize target frame pixels, leading to more efficient and accurate results \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05534)\].

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-techniques'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Techniques: 18 papers found.
Video frame interpolation techniques have evolved significantly, particularly with the integration of deep learning approaches that address traditional limitations such as motion estimation and synthesis. Early methods typically involved two separate steps—motion estimation and pixel synthesis—which were often dependent on optical flow and struggled with occlusion, blur, and brightness changes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. More recent approaches have merged these steps into a single convolutional process, using spatially adaptive kernels to capture both motion and synthesis simultaneously \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. This has enabled more efficient and high-quality interpolation, especially when dealing with large motions and complex scenes \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Some methods have also introduced unsupervised learning strategies, leveraging cycle consistency to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring ground-truth data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Additionally, techniques like deformable separable convolution have been proposed to adaptively estimate kernels and offsets, improving performance when motion exceeds predefined kernel sizes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\]. The use of phase-based motion representations has also shown promise in handling challenging scenarios with larger motion \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. Furthermore, methods such as softmax splatting have been introduced to address forward warping challenges, enabling more accurate and temporally consistent frame interpolation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\]. These advancements collectively contribute to the development of more robust and efficient video frame interpolation models, which are essential for applications in video processing and neural rendering \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\].

### <a id='category-end-to-end-deep-learning-approaches'></a>End-to-End Deep Learning Approaches: 8 papers found.
Video frame interpolation has been extensively explored in the context of end-to-end deep learning approaches, with several methods aiming to generate intermediate frames from consecutive video inputs while maintaining spatial and temporal coherence \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. These approaches often rely on optical flow estimation to model motion between frames, but this can introduce errors and computational overhead, especially in scenarios with large motion or heavy occlusion \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed alternative architectures that eliminate the need for explicit optical flow estimation, instead using feature reshaping operations like PixelShuffle combined with channel attention mechanisms to directly synthesize intermediate frames \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These models are trained end-to-end, allowing them to learn motion patterns and occlusion handling without relying on separate motion estimation modules \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Additionally, some methods focus on improving the quality of interpolated frames by incorporating visibility maps to exclude occluded pixels during the fusion process, thereby reducing artifacts \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Beyond frame interpolation, end-to-end methods have also been applied to broader tasks such as 3D scene reconstruction and novel view synthesis, where they leverage implicit representations and differentiable rendering techniques to learn geometric and appearance priors from 2D inputs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\]. These advancements highlight the growing trend of using deep learning for end-to-end video processing, enabling more robust and efficient solutions compared to traditional methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\].
