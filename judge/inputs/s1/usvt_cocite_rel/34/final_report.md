## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advancements focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as prediction-only or predict-then-optimize (PnO) methods suffer from goal mismatch, where the forecasting and optimization objectives are not aligned \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. This issue is further exacerbated by the introduction of state-of-the-art forecasting models, which can introduce additional uncertainty due to their inherent complexity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. To address these challenges, several works have explored hybrid or end-to-end frameworks that integrate forecasting and decision-making processes.

The predict-then-optimize paradigm has been widely studied, with models like DeepAR \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\] demonstrating significant improvements in probabilistic forecasting by leveraging autoregressive recurrent networks. However, these models often assume a fixed structure and may not adapt well to varying forecasting horizons or uncertainty levels \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\] introduces an interpretable architecture for multi-horizon forecasting, combining recurrent and attention-based layers to capture both local and long-term dependencies. While TFT provides insights into temporal dynamics, it still relies on a fixed model structure and does not explicitly address the alignment between forecasting and optimization objectives.

Recent work has focused on developing models that are more flexible and robust to uncertainty. The PatchTST \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\] framework introduces a segmentation-based approach to time series forecasting, where subseries-level patches are used as input tokens to Transformers. This design reduces computational complexity while retaining local semantic information. Similarly, the Non-stationary Transformers \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\] framework addresses the challenge of modeling non-stationary data by incorporating series stationarization and de-stationary attention mechanisms. These approaches aim to improve the robustness of forecasting models in real-world scenarios where data distributions can change over time.

Another line of research has explored the use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting. The Time-LLM \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\] framework reprograms LLMs for general time series forecasting, aligning time series data with natural language through text prototypes. This approach enables zero-shot learning and has shown promising results in both few-shot and zero-shot settings. However, recent studies have questioned the effectiveness of LLMs for time series forecasting, with some findings suggesting that replacing the LLM component with a basic attention layer can yield comparable or even better results \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. This highlights the need for more rigorous evaluation of LLMs in this domain.

### <a id='category-state-of-the-art-time-series-forecasting-models'></a>State-of-the-Art Time Series Forecasting Models: 17 papers found.
The related works in time series forecasting focus on improving accuracy, interpretability, and adaptability to real-world complexities. Recent advancements include Transformer-based models like PatchTST \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\], which enhance long-term forecasting by segmenting time series into subseries-level patches and leveraging channel-independent embeddings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\] introduces an attention-based architecture that combines local recurrent layers with interpretable self-attention mechanisms to handle multi-horizon forecasting and provide insights into temporal dynamics. Non-stationary Transformers \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\] address the issue of over-stationarization by incorporating modules that recover intrinsic non-stationary information, significantly improving performance on non-stationary data. Chronos \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\] leverages pre-trained transformer-based language models to tokenize time series data, achieving strong zero-shot performance across diverse datasets. iTransformer \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\] reimagines the Transformer architecture by applying attention and feed-forward networks on inverted dimensions, enabling better handling of multivariate and long-lookback time series. DeepAR \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\] employs auto-regressive recurrent networks to produce probabilistic forecasts, demonstrating accuracy improvements over classical methods. Time-LLM \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\] repurposes large language models for time series forecasting by aligning time series data with natural language through text prototypes and prompt-as-prefix strategies. Autoformer \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\] introduces an Auto-Correlation mechanism to capture long-range dependencies more efficiently than traditional self-attention, achieving state-of-the-art results in long-term forecasting. These models collectively highlight the shift towards more flexible, interpretable, and universally applicable forecasting frameworks that can handle diverse, real-world financial and temporal data.

[Snippet]: «Recently, there has been a surge of Transformer-based solutions for the long-term time series forecasting (LTSF) task. Despite the growing performance over the past few years, we question the validity of this line of research in this work. Specifically, Transformers is arguably the most successful solution to extract the semantic correlations among the elements in a long sequence. However, in time series modeling, we are to extract the temporal relations in an ordered set of continuous points. While employing positional encoding and using tokens to embed sub-series in Transformers facilitate preserving some ordering information, the nature of the permutation-invariant self-attention mechanism inevitably results in temporal information loss. 
To validate our claim, we introduce a set of embarrassingly simple one-layer linear models named LTSF-Linear for comparison. Experimental results on nine real-life datasets show that LTSF-Linear surprisingly outperforms existing sophisticated Transformer-based LTSF models in all cases, and often by a large margin. Moreover, we conduct comprehensive empirical studies to explore the impacts of various design elements of LTSF models on their temporal relation extraction capability. We hope this surprising finding opens up new research directions for the LTSF task. We also advocate revisiting the validity of Transformer-based solutions for other time series analysis tasks (e.g., anomaly detection) in the future.»

The category "Code Availability and Reproducibility in Research" highlights the importance of making research code publicly accessible to ensure transparency and reproducibility. This is exemplified by the paper introducing TimesNet, which provides its code on GitHub, allowing researchers to reproduce and build upon its results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. Similarly, the SparseTSF paper also makes its code available, emphasizing the practicality and efficiency of its lightweight model for long-term forecasting \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00946)\]. These examples underscore a growing trend in the research community to prioritize open-source contributions and reproducibility, which are essential for advancing scientific progress and validating findings. The availability of code not only facilitates collaboration but also enables other researchers to test and extend these models in different contexts, as seen in the RTS-PnO framework, which is also open-sourced for further experimentation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\].
