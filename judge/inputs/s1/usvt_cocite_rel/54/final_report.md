## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early works such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] emphasized the importance of integrating user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure. This approach aimed to capture high-order connectivity and explicitly inject collaborative signals into the embedding process. However, subsequent research has highlighted limitations in traditional GCN-based methods, particularly in their reliance on non-linear activations and feature transformations, which can hinder training and reduce performance \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

LightGCN \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] was introduced to address these issues by simplifying the GCN architecture, removing non-linearities, and focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation. This linear approach significantly improved performance over NGCF, demonstrating that simpler models can be more effective in collaborative filtering tasks. Similarly, the work on DeepICF \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04392)\] proposed a deep variant of item-based collaborative filtering that leverages nonlinear neural networks to model higher-order item interactions, leading to better performance in capturing complex user decision-making processes.

Another line of research, such as SpectralCF \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\], focused on spectral convolution operations in the spectral domain to capture both proximity and connectivity information from the user-item bipartite graph. This method was designed to alleviate the cold-start problem by learning from the spectral domain, which provides richer information about the graph structure. In contrast, the work on RippleNet \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] utilized knowledge graphs to enhance recommendations by propagating user preferences through the graph, allowing for more nuanced modeling of user-item relationships.

The challenge of modeling high-order interactions has also been addressed in Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\], which aims to disentangle user intents from the interaction data. By modeling a distribution over intents for each interaction, DGCF iteratively refines intent-aware interaction graphs and representations, leading to more interpretable and accurate recommendations. This approach highlights the importance of modeling diverse user intents in collaborative filtering.

### <a id='category-graph-convolutional-networks-in-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Convolutional Networks in Collaborative Filtering: 8 papers found.
Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, with numerous studies exploring their integration into recommendation frameworks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early works like NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] proposed embedding propagation on user-item graphs to explicitly encode collaborative signals, leading to improved performance over traditional matrix factorization methods. Subsequent research, such as the Pinterest-scale GCN implementation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\], focused on scalability and efficiency, introducing efficient random walks and training strategies to handle massive graphs with billions of nodes. These methods demonstrated the potential of GCNs to capture both structural and feature-based information effectively. However, challenges such as over-smoothing and non-linear activation difficulties persisted, especially in large-scale user-item graphs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. To address these, researchers proposed residual structures and linear models that enhance training efficiency and mitigate over-smoothing \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. Additionally, models like GraphRec \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] aimed to jointly model user-user and user-item graphs, incorporating heterogeneous strengths and opinions to improve social recommendation performance. The deep influence propagation model \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\] further advanced the field by simulating recursive social diffusion processes to refine user embeddings. Meanwhile, geometric deep learning approaches \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\] combined graph convolutions with recurrent networks to capture non-linear diffusion patterns, reducing parameter complexity. These advancements highlight the evolving strategies to enhance GCN-based CF models, addressing limitations in expressiveness, scalability, and modeling depth \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\].

### <a id='category-simplification-of-gcn-architecture'></a>Simplification of GCN Architecture: 2 papers found.
In the context of simplifying GCN architectures for recommendation tasks, several studies have focused on reducing unnecessary complexity while maintaining or improving performance. For instance, LightGCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] simplifies the original GCN by removing non-linear activation functions and feature transformations, which were found to hinder training and degrade performance in collaborative filtering. Instead, it relies solely on neighborhood aggregation, a core component of GCNs, to propagate user and item embeddings across the interaction graph. This approach leads to a more straightforward and effective model, achieving significant improvements over existing GCN-based recommenders like NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, another work \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\] reduces complexity by eliminating nonlinearities and collapsing weight matrices between layers, resulting in a linear model that corresponds to a fixed low-pass filter followed by a linear classifier. This simplification not only improves scalability and interpretability but also achieves substantial speedups over FastGCN. These efforts highlight a growing trend toward simplifying GCN designs for recommendation systems, emphasizing the importance of model efficiency and performance in practical applications.

Here is the paper abstract: Graph convolutional networks have recently gained prominence in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendations. However, we identify potential bottlenecks in two foundational components. First, the embedding layer leads to a latent space with limited capacity, overlooking locally observed but potentially valuable preference patterns. Also, the widely-used neighborhood aggregation is limited in its ability to leverage diverse preference patterns in a fine-grained manner. Building on spectral graph theory, we reveal that these limitations stem from graph filtering with a cut-off in the frequency spectrum and a restricted linear form. To address these issues, we introduce ChebyCF, a CF framework based on graph spectral filtering. Instead of a learned embedding, it takes a user's raw interaction history to utilize the full spectrum of signals contained in it.
