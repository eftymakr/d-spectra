## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early works such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] emphasized the importance of integrating user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure. This approach aimed to capture high-order connectivity and explicitly inject collaborative signals into the embedding process. However, subsequent research has highlighted limitations in traditional GCN-based methods, particularly in their reliance on non-linear activations and feature transformations, which can hinder training and reduce performance \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

LightGCN \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] was introduced to address these issues by simplifying the GCN architecture, removing non-linearities, and focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation. This linear approach significantly improved performance over NGCF, demonstrating that simpler models can be more effective in collaborative filtering tasks. Similarly, the work on DeepICF \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04392)\] proposed a deep variant of item-based collaborative filtering that leverages nonlinear neural networks to model higher-order item interactions, leading to better performance in capturing complex user decision-making processes.

Another line of research, such as SpectralCF \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\], focused on spectral convolution operations in the spectral domain to capture both proximity and connectivity information from the user-item bipartite graph. This method was designed to alleviate the cold-start problem by learning from the spectral domain, which provides richer information about the graph structure. In contrast, the work on RippleNet \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] utilized knowledge graphs to enhance recommendations by propagating user preferences through the graph, allowing for more nuanced modeling of user-item relationships.

The challenge of modeling high-order interactions has also been addressed in Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\], which aims to disentangle user intents from the interaction data. By modeling a distribution over intents for each interaction, DGCF iteratively refines intent-aware interaction graphs and representations, leading to more interpretable and accurate recommendations. This approach highlights the importance of modeling diverse user intents in collaborative filtering.

Additionally, the work on Multi-Component Graph Convolutional Collaborative Filtering (MCCF) \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10699)\] proposed a method to distinguish latent purchasing motivations by decomposing and recomposing edges in the user-item graph. This approach introduces modules to identify and recombine latent components, improving the ability to capture fine-grained user preferences.
