## Related Works

Multimodal language models have emerged as a critical area of research, aiming to integrate textual and nonverbal cues for more natural and immersive human-computer interaction. Early efforts in multimodal processing focused on combining text with basic visual features, often using handcrafted features or limited modalities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. However, recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have enabled more sophisticated integration of visual and textual information, allowing models to perform tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and even zero-shot learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14824)\]. These models typically rely on pre-trained vision encoders and LLMs, with some approaches using cross-modal alignment techniques to bridge the gap between modalities \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03378)\].

The integration of nonverbal cues, such as facial expressions and body language, has been a particular challenge. While some models have attempted to incorporate these elements, they often rely on limited datasets or simplistic feature extraction methods \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. For instance, the CMU-MOSEI dataset has been used to study multimodal sentiment analysis, focusing on the interaction between text, audio, and video \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.07250)\]. Similarly, the Tensor Fusion Network (TFN) was introduced to model both intra-modal and inter-modal dynamics, achieving state-of-the-art results in multimodal sentiment analysis \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/75d17e8fa5165a849ebe5f0475bdf77bf0b6be74)\]. These models, however, are typically limited to specific tasks and lack the flexibility to handle open-ended, conversational scenarios.

More recently, researchers have begun exploring the use of large-scale, annotated datasets to train models that can understand and generate nonverbal cues alongside text. The GrIT dataset, for example, was introduced to support grounded image-text pairs, enabling models to better understand the relationship between visual elements and language \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00923)\]. Similarly, the Shikra model was designed to handle spatial coordinates and natural language descriptions, allowing for more accurate referential dialogue \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. These models demonstrate the potential for integrating nonverbal cues into language models, but they often require extensive manual annotation or specialized architectures.

The introduction of models like Flamingo and Video-LLaMA has further advanced the field by enabling few-shot learning and cross-modal understanding \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/efe353682061e0f929e8c916b64b84ff88297e47)\]. These models leverage pre-trained vision and language encoders, with some incorporating additional modules to handle temporal dynamics in video data \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15195)\]. However, these approaches still face challenges in handling the complexity and variability of real-world nonverbal cues, particularly in conversational settings.

Furthermore, models like Shikra have been developed to handle spatial coordinates in natural language, enabling referential dialogue and enhancing vision-language tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.07250)\]. These contributions highlight the ongoing efforts to create more robust and versatile multimodal architectures that can better mimic human-like communication and understanding.
