## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the domain of Web search. These models often rely on large-scale labeled query-document pairs for training, which are typically expensive to obtain \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09268)\]. However, the availability of Web document collections like ClueWeb22 has enabled the use of synthetic queries generated by large language models as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, synthetic training queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal performance in downstream retrieval tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Existing methods typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, we propose a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11907)\].

DPO has been shown to improve the alignment of large language models with human preferences by directly optimizing the policy from collected preference data without the need for a reward model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach is more stable and computationally efficient compared to traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. RLHF involves training a reward model to reflect human preferences and then fine-tuning the large unsupervised language model using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, RLHF is complex and often unstable, requiring significant hyperparameter tuning and sampling from the LM during fine-tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO bypasses these challenges by introducing a new parameterization of the reward model that allows for closed-form optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

In addition to DPO, other methods have explored the use of neural models for document ranking. For example, K-NRM uses a kernel-based neural model that incorporates word embeddings and kernel-pooling techniques to improve ranking performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06613)\]. Similarly, ColBERT introduces a late interaction architecture that independently encodes the query and document using BERT and then employs a cheap yet powerful interaction step to model their fine-grained similarity \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. These models have shown significant improvements in retrieval performance, but they often require substantial computational resources \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\].

The use of contextualized embeddings for document ranking has also been explored. CEDR, for instance, leverages contextualized language models like BERT to improve ad-hoc document ranking by incorporating fine-grained similarity features \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07094)\]. This approach has been shown to outperform traditional baselines and improve recall performance on benchmark datasets \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07094)\].

### <a id='category-human-preference-alignment-in-llms'></a>Human Preference Alignment in LLMs: 4 papers found.
The category "Human Preference Alignment in LLMs" explores methods to align large language models with human preferences through various techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). RLHF involves training a reward model based on human preferences and then fine-tuning the LM to maximize this reward, but it is often complex and unstable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO, on the other hand, simplifies this process by directly optimizing the policy using a classification loss, eliminating the need for reward modeling and sampling during fine-tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach has shown stability and performance comparable to or better than existing methods, particularly in controlling sentiment and improving response quality in tasks like summarization and dialogue \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Additionally, research has highlighted the importance of pairwise preferences over pointwise rewards, leading to the development of new objectives like $\Psi$PO that bypass key approximations in RLHF and DPO \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. Furthermore, methods like Direct Large Model Alignment (DLMA) leverage contrastive prompts and self-rewarding scores to align models without relying on human-annotated data, demonstrating the potential for automated alignment \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325)\]. These approaches collectively emphasize the need for more efficient and effective methods to align LLMs with human preferences, particularly in the context of training and evaluation bottlenecks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)\].
