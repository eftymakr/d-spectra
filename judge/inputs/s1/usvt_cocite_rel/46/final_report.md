## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in recommender systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Traditional approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms, such as those found in the Transformer architecture, to model sequential user interactions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. However, these methods typically incorporate domain-specific components as additional modules, which may not fully leverage the inherent power of the self-attention mechanism \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. 

Several works have explored the use of self-attention in CDSR. For instance, C2DSR proposes a method that simultaneously leverages intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to capture precise user preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Similarly, DREAM introduces a decoupled representation approach that uses extraction attention modules and supervised contrastive learning to enhance cross-domain performance \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. These models focus on capturing both single- and cross-domain user preferences, but they often rely on complex architectures and may not fully exploit the self-attention mechanism's potential \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\].

The Dual Attentive Sequential Learning (DASL) model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\] and the π-Net \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\] further explore cross-domain knowledge transfer by introducing dual learning and parallel information-sharing mechanisms. DASL uses dual embeddings and dual attention to simultaneously transfer information between domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\], while π-Net addresses shared-account settings by filtering out irrelevant user behaviors \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of domain-specific and cross-domain information but may not fully optimize the self-attention mechanism for cross-domain tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\].

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research direction to address data sparsity and cold-start issues in sequential recommendation systems by leveraging interactions across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms within transformers, augmenting them with domain-specific modules to enhance performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. However, these methods typically overlook the inherent power of the self-attention module itself in capturing correlations among behaviors \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Recent works such as C2DSR \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\], DASL \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\], and DA-GCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\] have explored ways to better integrate intra- and inter-sequence item relationships, as well as cross-domain interactions, by employing graph neural networks, dual learning, or domain-aware attention mechanisms. For instance, C2DSR introduces a novel contrastive infomax objective to enhance the correlation between single- and cross-domain user representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\], while DASL utilizes a dual learning mechanism to iteratively transfer information between domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Similarly, DA-GCN constructs domain-aware graphs to capture user-specific preferences and employs attention mechanisms to guide message passing \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Other studies, such as DREAM \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\] and Tri-CDR \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\], focus on decoupled representations and triple sequence learning to jointly model single- and cross-domain preferences, respectively. These approaches highlight the importance of effectively capturing both domain-specific and cross-domain patterns to improve recommendation accuracy. Additionally, methods like AMID \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] and CD-ASR \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\] address open-world assumptions and knowledge transfer by incorporating adaptive mechanisms and attention-based aggregation. Despite these advances, challenges such as data sparsity, class imbalance, and the need for robust cross-domain alignment remain active areas of research \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\].
