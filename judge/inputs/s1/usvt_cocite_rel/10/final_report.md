## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) in language model pre-training. While NTP focuses on predicting one token at a time, MTP extends this by predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has shown improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency, particularly for large models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has identified challenges in applying MTP to smaller language models (SLMs), where the complexity of predicting multiple tokens at once can hinder learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several studies have explored the limitations of NTP and the potential benefits of MTP. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\] highlights that the assumption that teacher-forcing leads to an accurate next-token predictor is often flawed, especially in certain task classes. This suggests that MTP may offer a more robust training objective by avoiding the pitfalls of teacher-forcing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02226)\] demonstrates that delaying the output of tokens by allowing the model to process more hidden vectors can improve inference performance, particularly in reasoning tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02226)\].

The concept of curriculum learning has also been applied to MTP training. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\] introduces a forward curriculum, which gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, enabling SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training. This approach improves downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. In contrast, a reverse curriculum, which starts with MTP and gradually reduces complexity, achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide the same self-speculative decoding benefits \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\].

Additionally, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\] proposes speculative decoding as a method to accelerate inference by generating multiple tokens in parallel using a draft model. This technique has been successfully applied to large models, achieving significant speedups without compromising output quality \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11143)\] further extends this idea with Medusa, which adds multiple decoding heads to predict multiple tokens in parallel, reducing the number of decoding steps required and improving inference speed \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11143)\].

The effectiveness of MTP has also been demonstrated in the context of sequence-to-sequence pre-training. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\] introduces ProphetNet, which optimizes models by predicting the next $n$ tokens simultaneously, encouraging the model to plan for future tokens and avoid overfitting on local correlations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. This aligns with the broader trend of using MTP to improve both training efficiency and model capabilities \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\].

### <a id='category-speculative-decoding-techniques'></a>Speculative Decoding Techniques: 3 papers found.
Speculative decoding techniques aim to enhance the efficiency and performance of language models during inference by introducing mechanisms that allow models to process additional computation before committing to a token prediction. These approaches often involve the use of pause tokens or speculative models to generate multiple tokens in parallel, reducing the overall latency of decoding. For instance, pause-training introduces a learnable pause token, allowing the model to process extra computation before outputting the next token, which has been shown to improve performance on various downstream tasks, particularly when combined with both pre-training and fine-tuning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02226)\]. Similarly, speculative sampling leverages a faster draft model to generate multiple tokens per transformer call, with a modified rejection sampling scheme that maintains the target model's distribution \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01318)\]. SpecInfer further extends this idea by using small speculative models to predict token sequences, which are then verified in parallel by a larger LLM, significantly reducing end-to-end latency without compromising model quality \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09781)\]. These methods collectively highlight the potential of speculative decoding to accelerate inference while maintaining or even improving the quality of generated outputs.
