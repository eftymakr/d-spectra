## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has garnered significant attention in recent years, with a growing body of research addressing the trade-offs between recommendation accuracy and fairness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. Early work on fairness in recommendation systems primarily focused on user-side fairness, ensuring that recommendations are not biased against certain demographic groups \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10671)\]. However, this approach often overlooked the fairness implications for item providers, leading to imbalanced exposure and potential harm to smaller or less prominent producers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\]. This oversight has been increasingly recognized, prompting a shift towards two-sided fairness, which considers both user and item fairness simultaneously \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\].

Several studies have explored fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms, aiming to balance recommendation quality with fairness constraints. For example, the Fairness-Aware Re-ranking (FAR) algorithm \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] introduces a mechanism to ensure provider-side fairness while maintaining a reasonable level of recommendation accuracy. Similarly, the Personalized Fairness-Aware Re-ranking (PFAR) algorithm \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1042e3396aae4457106938149c5d5ef5e1667169)\] extends this idea by incorporating user-specific preferences, allowing for more tailored fairness considerations. These approaches highlight the importance of integrating fairness into the re-ranking process without compromising the overall quality of recommendations.

The concept of fairness in rankings has also been examined through the lens of economic principles, such as elasticity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. This perspective draws parallels between the accuracy-fairness trade-off and the dynamics of economic transactions, where fairness considerations in re-ranking can be likened to a commodity tax on suppliers, ultimately translating into a cost for users \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. This analogy has led to the development of the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve), which evaluates the trade-off between fairness and accuracy based on the elasticity of utility between item groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. This framework enables a more nuanced assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms by considering how changes in fairness affect overall recommendation quality.

In addition to economic models, fairness in recommendation systems has been studied through the use of fairness metrics and regularization techniques. For instance, the work of \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\] proposes a framework for evaluating fairness in rankings based on pairwise comparisons, providing a tractable method to quantify fairness in recommendation systems. This approach has been applied to large-scale production systems, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving pairwise fairness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. Similarly, the FairRec algorithm \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01180)\] introduces a novel method for allocating exposure to producers while ensuring fairness for users, drawing on concepts from fair allocation of indivisible goods.

The concept of elasticity is central to understanding these trade-offs, as it formalizes how fairness constraints affect overall performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. Furthermore, recent work has highlighted the need for multi-dimensional fairness considerations, moving beyond single sensitive attributes to account for individual preferences across multiple fairness dimensions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04056)\]. These studies collectively underscore the complexity of achieving fairness in re-ranking while maintaining recommendation quality, setting the stage for more nuanced evaluation frameworks like the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) and algorithms such as ElasticRank \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\].
