## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Early approaches relied on matrix factorization to learn latent representations of users and items \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.01585)\]. However, these methods struggled with scalability and capturing complex interactions between users and items. To address these limitations, recent work has explored deep learning techniques, such as Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF), which uses neural networks to model user-item interactions more flexibly \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. NCF replaces the inner product used in traditional matrix factorization with a neural architecture, allowing for non-linear modeling of interactions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\].

Graph-based methods have also gained traction in recommendation systems. Graph Convolutional Neural Networks (GCNs) leverage the structure of user-item graphs to generate embeddings that encode both local graph structure and node features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. These models have been successfully applied to large-scale recommendation tasks, such as those at Pinterest, where they outperform traditional methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. Similarly, GraphSAGE extends this idea by generating embeddings for unseen nodes through sampling and aggregating features from a node's neighborhood \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. This inductive approach enables the generalization of embeddings to new data, which is crucial for evolving recommendation systems.

In addition to graph-based models, collaborative filtering has been enhanced by incorporating auxiliary information. Collaborative Deep Learning (CDL) integrates content information with collaborative filtering by jointly learning deep representations for both \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]. This approach addresses the sparsity problem in traditional collaborative filtering and improves recommendation accuracy. Similarly, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) explicitly models user-item interactions by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This method captures high-order connectivity in the user-item graph, effectively injecting collaborative signals into the embedding process.

Recent work has also focused on incorporating knowledge graphs (KGs) into recommendation systems to improve accuracy and explainability. Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) explicitly models high-order relations in KGs by recursively propagating embeddings and using attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of neighbors \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. This approach outperforms existing KG-based methods by better capturing complex interactions between items and their attributes.

Furthermore, Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR) has been proposed to optimize recommendation models for personalized ranking tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\]. BPR is derived from a Bayesian analysis of the problem and provides a learning algorithm based on stochastic gradient descent with bootstrap sampling. This method has been shown to outperform standard learning techniques for matrix factorization and adaptive kNN.
