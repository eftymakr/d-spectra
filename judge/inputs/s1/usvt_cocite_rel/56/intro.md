## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems by leveraging user behavior across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Traditional approaches often rely on overlapping users to transfer knowledge between domains, but this limits the applicability of these methods to real-world scenarios where user overlap is rare \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Several studies have attempted to address this by proposing novel architectures that enable cross-domain knowledge transfer without requiring overlapping users \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. For instance, CoNet introduces a deep transfer learning approach using neural networks to model complex user-item interactions and enables dual knowledge transfer across domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. This approach outperforms matrix factorization-based methods by a relative improvement of 7.84% in NDCG \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\].

Another line of research focuses on modeling the sequential nature of user interactions while capturing cross-domain relationships. The work in \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\] proposes a mixed information flow network that considers both behavioral and knowledge transfer between domains, allowing for more accurate recommendations by incorporating cross-domain information at the right time \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. Similarly, the Dual Attentive Sequential Learning (DASL) model \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\] introduces a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between two domains, leading to improved click-through rate predictions in online commerce platforms \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]. This model employs dual embeddings and dual attention mechanisms to capture user preferences across domains \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\].

The challenge of modeling mixed behavior sequences and capturing inter-sequence relationships has also been addressed in several works. For example, the C2DSR model \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\] leverages both intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences, achieving better performance than existing methods that focus only on intra-sequence interactions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Similarly, the Tri-CDR framework \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\] jointly models source, target, and mixed behavior sequences to highlight global and target preferences, using a triple cross-domain attention mechanism to emphasize informative knowledge \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\].

Moreover, the issue of data sparsity and class imbalance in cross-domain settings has been tackled through contrastive learning and focal loss techniques. The DREAM model \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] introduces a supervised contrastive learning framework to model inter-sequence relationships and address data sparsity through data augmentation, while also using focal loss to improve performance in imbalanced scenarios \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. The MGCL framework \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\] further advances this by using a multi-view graph contrastive learning approach to capture both intra-domain and inter-domain relationships, leading to significant improvements over state-of-the-art methods \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].
