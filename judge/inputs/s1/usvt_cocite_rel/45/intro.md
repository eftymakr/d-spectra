## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has emerged as a critical area within sentiment analysis, particularly in the context of user-generated content such as app reviews. While traditional approaches often focus on sentiment polarity (positive, negative, neutral) \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\], recent work has emphasized the need for more nuanced emotion detection that captures the complexity of human emotional expression \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\]. This is especially relevant in app reviews, where users express a wide range of emotions that go beyond simple sentiment categories \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\].

Several datasets have been introduced to address the limitations of coarse-grained sentiment analysis. The GoEmotions dataset, for instance, includes 58k Reddit comments labeled for 27 emotion categories, demonstrating the potential of large-scale annotated data for fine-grained emotion detection \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Similarly, the SemEval-2018 Task 1: Affect in Tweets provides labeled data for emotion classification, intensity, and valence, supporting research in affective computing \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66c8ab0b3b7698cf5333c3f7350a6eaa5302294b)\]. These datasets highlight the importance of diverse and representative data for training robust emotion classification models.

Annotation frameworks have also been developed to improve the quality and consistency of emotion labeling. The EmoBank corpus, for example, distinguishes between writer’s and reader’s perspectives in emotion annotation, offering insights into how different viewpoints affect annotation quality \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01996)\]. This aligns with findings that the writer’s perspective often yields superior annotation quality, although domain-specific variations exist \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62024027ef084f94b7355e84a2562738f0b02542)\]. These studies underscore the challenges of achieving inter-annotator agreement and the need for well-defined annotation guidelines \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62024027ef084f94b7355e84a2562738f0b02542)\].

Crowdsourcing has been explored as a method to generate large-scale emotion lexicons efficiently. The CROWDSOURCING A WORD–EMOTION ASSOCIATION LEXICON paper demonstrates how crowdsourcing can be used to create high-quality word-emotion associations, with strategies to mitigate annotation bias and improve reliability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. This approach has been particularly useful in overcoming the limitations of small, manually curated emotion lexicons \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\].

Deep learning models have also been applied to emotion classification, with models like EmoNet achieving state-of-the-art results on 24 fine-grained emotion types \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. These models leverage large annotated datasets to capture the nuances of emotional expression, although challenges remain in generalizing across domains and taxonomies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Additionally, the use of emojis as a form of distant supervision has shown promise in improving model performance by providing diverse and noisy labels \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.00524)\].
