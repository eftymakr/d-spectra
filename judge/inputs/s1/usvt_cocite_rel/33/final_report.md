## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a prominent approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is widely used, its optimality remains unclear \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Inspired by human learning, where sequentially revisiting tasks helps mitigate forgetting, this work explores whether sequential rehearsal can offer greater benefits compared to standard concurrent rehearsal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\].

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of rehearsal in CL. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\] proposed Deep Generative Replay, a framework that leverages a dual model architecture to sample and interleave training data for previous tasks with new ones, enabling effective sequential learning. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\] introduced a method that uses a very small episodic memory for continual learning, showing that joint training on current and stored data can outperform state-of-the-art approaches. These works highlight the importance of replay in mitigating catastrophic forgetting, though they do not directly compare concurrent versus sequential strategies.

Experience replay, a common technique in reinforcement learning, has also been explored for CL. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\] examined the use of experience replay buffers for all past events, demonstrating that this strategy can reduce catastrophic forgetting in both Atari and DMLab domains. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\] further proposed a task-based hard attention mechanism that preserves previous tasks' information without affecting current task learning, reducing forgetting by up to 80%. These approaches emphasize the role of replay in maintaining knowledge across tasks but do not directly address the comparative benefits of sequential rehearsal.

Theoretical analysis of rehearsal-based CL has been limited, but recent work has begun to address this gap. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\] introduced a theoretical framework analyzing the impact of task similarity on forgetting, showing that task similarity increases the risk of catastrophic forgetting. \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\] extended this analysis to overparameterized linear models, providing explicit forms of expected forgetting and generalization error. These studies lay the groundwork for understanding how rehearsal strategies affect learning dynamics.

In addition to theoretical analysis, several works have proposed hybrid approaches to rehearsal. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\] introduced a method that combines concurrent and sequential rehearsal, training similar tasks together and revisiting dissimilar tasks sequentially. This approach was shown to outperform standard concurrent rehearsal in experiments with deep neural networks. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] further explored the use of memory-efficient rehearsal strategies, such as ExStream, which reduces memory and computational costs while maintaining performance.

### <a id='category-experience-replay-techniques'></a>Experience Replay Techniques: 5 papers found.
Experience replay techniques have been extensively explored to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL), a critical challenge in training models to learn sequentially without overwriting prior knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These techniques typically involve storing past data in a buffer and replaying it during training to maintain performance on previous tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. However, traditional methods often rely on random sampling from the replay buffer, which may not be optimal for all scenarios \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. Recent work has proposed more sophisticated strategies, such as generative replay, where a deep generative model is used to sample past data, enabling efficient and flexible memory usage \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Additionally, methods like reservoir sampling have been shown to effectively manage small episodic memories by ensuring diverse representation of past experiences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Furthermore, controlled sampling strategies that prioritize samples most affected by parameter updates have demonstrated significant improvements in reducing forgetting \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. These approaches highlight the evolving landscape of experience replay, aiming to balance memory efficiency with performance on both current and past tasks.

### <a id='category-task-similarity-and-forgetting'></a>Task Similarity and Forgetting: 7 papers found.
The category "Task Similarity and Forgetting" explores the challenges of catastrophic forgetting in continual learning, where models lose previously learned knowledge when adapting to new tasks. Researchers have proposed various strategies to mitigate this issue, such as task-based hard attention mechanisms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\], Learning without Forgetting \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\], and Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. These methods aim to preserve prior knowledge while enabling effective learning on new tasks. Additionally, the concept of task similarity has been identified as a critical factor influencing forgetting, with more similar tasks leading to greater interference \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. To address this, methods like Orthogonal Gradient Descent (OGD) have been introduced, leveraging data structure through techniques like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce forgetting \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. Furthermore, theoretical analyses have been conducted to understand the dynamics of forgetting in continual learning, particularly in overparameterized models, leading to insights on how sequential rehearsal can offer advantages over concurrent rehearsal in certain scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\]. These contributions collectively highlight the importance of task similarity and forgetting in continual learning and the ongoing efforts to develop robust solutions.
