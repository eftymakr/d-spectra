## Related Works

Video super-resolution (VSR) has been a focal point in computer vision, with recent advances leveraging deep learning to address the challenges of temporal consistency and degradation modeling. Early methods relied on motion estimation and compensation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04590)\], which, while effective, often introduced computational overhead and artifacts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\]. The introduction of recurrent architectures allowed for more efficient processing by leveraging temporal dependencies \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.05250)\], but these methods still struggled with long-range dependencies and computational efficiency \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\]. 

Recent work has shifted towards transformer-based models, which offer parallel processing capabilities and better modeling of long-range dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\]. The Video Restoration Transformer (VRT) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\] proposed a parallel frame prediction approach using temporal reciprocal self attention (TRSA) and parallel warping, achieving significant improvements in tasks such as video super-resolution and deblurring. Similarly, the Recurrent Video Restoration Transformer (RVRT) \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02146)\] integrated the advantages of parallel and recurrent processing, offering a balanced trade-off between model size, effectiveness, and efficiency. These models demonstrated superior performance on benchmark datasets, but they often required extensive computational resources \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\].

Diffusion models have also gained traction in VSR due to their ability to generate high-quality, realistic details \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\]. However, the inherent randomness in diffusion processes can lead to artifacts and inconsistencies in generated videos \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed methods to enhance temporal consistency, such as the motion-guided latent diffusion (MGLD) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00853)\], which uses temporal dynamics to guide the diffusion process and ensure content consistency across frames. Additionally, the Temporally-Deformable Alignment Network (TDAN) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02898)\] introduced a feature-level alignment mechanism without optical flow, improving the quality of aligned frames.

The use of self-supervised learning has also been explored to improve the robustness of VSR models. The Self-supervised ControlNet \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\] leverages high-resolution features as guidance and employs contrastive learning to extract degradation-insensitive features from low-resolution videos. This approach helps reduce artifacts and improve perceptual quality. Furthermore, the RealBasicVSR \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12704)\] introduced a new dataset and a carefully designed cleaning module to reduce noises and artifacts prior to propagation, enhancing the overall quality of restored videos.

In summary, while existing methods have made significant strides in VSR, challenges remain in balancing computational efficiency with perceptual quality. The proposed approach builds on these advancements by incorporating self-supervised learning and Mamba into pre-trained latent diffusion models, ensuring robustness against degradation and artifacts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\].

### <a id='category-transformer-based-vsr-models'></a>Transformer-Based VSR Models: 4 papers found.
Video restoration and super-resolution (VSR) have seen significant advancements with the integration of transformer-based models, which leverage self-attention mechanisms to effectively model temporal dependencies across video frames. Early approaches either processed frames sequentially, limiting parallelizability and long-range dependency modeling \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c6a8ff4d9dcdf9e5d82310b847af999ed6655f65)\], or restored all frames in parallel, leading to high computational costs and memory usage \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02146)\]. To address these limitations, methods like the Video Restoration Transformer (VRT) introduced parallel frame prediction capabilities using temporal reciprocal self attention (TRSA) and parallel warping, enabling efficient cross-clip interactions and feature fusion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\]. Similarly, the Recurrent Video Restoration Transformer (RVRT) combined the benefits of both paradigms by processing local frames in parallel within a globally recurrent framework, achieving a balance between model efficiency and performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02146)\]. However, these models often relied on alignment modules to handle frame misalignment, which could sometimes hinder performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12704)\]. Subsequent work, such as the Trajectory-aware Transformer for Video Super-Resolution (TTVSR), further improved efficiency by focusing self-attention on relevant spatio-temporal trajectories, reducing computational costs while maintaining long-range feature modeling \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04216)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance transformer-based VSR models by improving temporal modeling, reducing computational overhead, and optimizing alignment strategies for better performance.

Experimental results demonstrate that the TDAN is capable of alleviating occlusions and artifacts for temporal alignment and the TDAN-based VSR model outperforms several recent state-of-the-art VSR networks with a comparable or even much smaller model size.
