## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) have significantly improved speech naturalness, increasing the demand for precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Existing approaches for prosody manipulation often depend on specialized modules or additional training, limiting their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. Similarly, traditional mispronunciation correction relies on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making it less practical in low-resource settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges.

Prosody modeling is an essential component in modern TTS frameworks. By explicitly providing prosody features to the TTS model, the style of synthesized utterances can be controlled \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. However, predicting natural and reasonable prosody at inference time is challenging \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. Several works have focused on improving prosody control through prosody embeddings, which enable fine-grained control of the speaking style of the synthesized speech \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. These embeddings are trained without any other supervision except for the target speech for synthesizing \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. By adjusting the learned prosody features, we could change the pitch and amplitude of the synthesized speech both at the frame level and the phoneme level \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\].

Non-autoregressive TTS models have also been proposed to address the one-to-many mapping problem in TTS. These models can generate speech waveform from text in parallel, enjoying the benefit of full end-to-end training and even faster inference than autoregressive models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. For instance, FastSpeech 2 and FastSpeech 2s outperform FastSpeech in voice quality with much simplified training pipeline and reduced training time \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. These models directly train the model with ground-truth target instead of the simplified output from teacher, and introduce more variation information of speech (e.g., pitch, energy and more accurate duration) as conditional inputs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\].

Several works have also focused on improving the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech through prosody control. For example, Fastpitch, a fully-parallel TTS model based on FastSpeech, is conditioned on fundamental frequency contours \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. The model predicts pitch contours during inference, which enables more expressive speech and better matching to the semantic of the utterance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. Similarly, the use of a variational autoencoder-based residual encoder in Parallel Tacotron improves the naturalness of synthesized speech \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\].

### <a id='category-non-autoregressive-tts-frameworks'></a>Non-autoregressive TTS Frameworks: 7 papers found.
Non-autoregressive text-to-speech (TTS) frameworks have gained significant attention for their ability to generate speech in parallel, improving efficiency compared to autoregressive models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Models like FastSpeech and Parallel Tacotron address the one-to-many mapping problem by incorporating more variation information such as pitch, energy, and duration during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. FastSpeech 2 further improves upon FastSpeech by directly using ground-truth targets instead of teacher-student distillation, leading to better voice quality and a simpler training pipeline \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Parallel Tacotron introduces a variational autoencoder-based residual encoder, enhancing naturalness and enabling efficient synthesis on parallel hardware \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Additionally, Glow-TTS eliminates the need for external aligners by using flow-based generative models and dynamic programming to find monotonic alignments, achieving a significant speed-up over autoregressive models like Tacotron 2 \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. These frameworks also emphasize prosody modeling, where explicit prosody features can be used to control the style of synthesized utterances \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. The use of hierarchical architectures and iterative refinement techniques further improves audio quality and prosody naturalness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. Overall, non-autoregressive TTS models offer a promising direction for efficient and high-quality speech synthesis, with ongoing research focusing on improving naturalness and enabling post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\].

### <a id='category-prosody-embeddings-and-their-applications'></a>Prosody Embeddings and Their Applications: 9 papers found.
The category "Prosody Embeddings and Their Applications" explores methods to enhance expressive speech synthesis by modeling and transferring prosody effectively. Several approaches focus on embedding prosody into neural TTS systems to enable fine-grained control over speech characteristics such as pitch, energy, and duration. For instance, prosody embeddings are integrated into end-to-end speech synthesis networks, allowing for direct manipulation of synthesized speech attributes without additional supervision \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. These embeddings are often trained on large-scale datasets, leveraging both high-quality and low-quality data to capture the variability of prosody \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. Techniques like temporal normalization and variational auto-encoders are introduced to improve robustness against speaker variations and enhance the quality of prosody transfer \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Additionally, methods such as global style tokens (GSTs) and text-predicting GSTs (TP-GSTs) are proposed to disentangle and model expressive factors of speech, enabling style transfer and control over speaking style independently of text content \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Cross-utterance context vectors derived from BERT embeddings are also used to incorporate paragraph-level contextual information, improving the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. These approaches collectively demonstrate the potential of prosody embeddings in achieving more natural and controllable speech synthesis, addressing challenges such as mispronunciation and limited contextual awareness in traditional TTS systems \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\].

### <a id='category-prosody-control-in-tts'></a>Prosody Control in TTS: 11 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have enabled high-quality speech synthesis, but controlling prosody and correcting mispronunciations remain challenging \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. Traditional methods often rely on specialized modules or additional training, limiting post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. For instance, some models condition on fundamental frequency contours to improve expressiveness and speech quality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\], while others use non-autoregressive architectures to enable faster synthesis and better control over prosodic features like pitch, duration, and energy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. These approaches typically require explicit prosody annotations or pre-defined style tokens, which can be restrictive \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. More recent work has focused on learning latent prosody spaces from acoustic features, allowing for intuitive and interpretable control over speaking style and emphasis \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. For example, hierarchical models conditioned on both coarse and fine-grained acoustic features have been shown to generate diverse speaking styles while maintaining high quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. Additionally, some models incorporate variational autoencoders (VAEs) or discrete latent spaces to enable more natural and controllable speech generation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. These techniques aim to improve the flexibility and expressiveness of TTS systems, aligning them closer with human speech patterns \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. However, post-hoc editing of prosody and pronunciation remains an open challenge, as many models lack the ability to modify internal representations without retraining \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. The introduction of model-agnostic methods like Counterfactual Activation Editing addresses this by enabling inference-time adjustments to internal representations, offering a promising direction for editable speech synthesis \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\].
