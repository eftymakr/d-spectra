## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has become the de facto standard for ensuring privacy in data analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. However, its implementation in distributed settings is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, such as incorrect noise sampling or correlated noise generation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These issues have been explored in various works. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\] highlights the vulnerability of local differential privacy (LDP) to manipulation attacks, where adversaries can skew estimations by manipulating their outputs. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\] discusses how LDP protocols are susceptible to data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users to distort results. These findings underscore the need for robust verification mechanisms in distributed DP systems.

To address these challenges, several approaches have been proposed. One line of work focuses on improving the utility of LDP protocols while maintaining privacy. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] introduces the Discrete Gaussian mechanism, which provides better accuracy than continuous Gaussian noise for discrete data. This is particularly relevant in settings where data is inherently discrete, such as population counts. Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00133)\] presents the Distributed Discrete Gaussian Mechanism for federated learning, which combines on-device noise addition with secure aggregation to enhance privacy and accuracy. These techniques are foundational in developing more robust DP protocols.

Another significant area of research is the development of verifiable DP mechanisms. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\] introduces the concept of Interactive Proofs for Differential Privacy, which requires the publishing entity to provide a zero-knowledge proof that the output is both differentially private and reliable. This approach ensures that the randomness used in DP is not exploited by malicious actors. This work demonstrates how cryptographic methods can be used to enhance the security of DP protocols in untrusted environments.

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-of-differential-privacy'></a>Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy: 4 papers found.
The shuffled model of differential privacy offers a middle ground between the local and central models, enabling accurate computation of statistics while maintaining user privacy without requiring full trust in a central server \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes user-submitted messages, allowing for improved accuracy compared to the local model while avoiding the trust assumptions of the central model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Research has shown that the shuffled model can achieve the same level of accuracy as the central model for certain tasks, such as sum queries, while still preserving privacy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, the shuffled model has been demonstrated to provide better privacy guarantees than the local model in some cases, particularly when considering privacy amplification techniques that leverage the anonymity of the shuffled messages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. These findings suggest that the shuffled model may offer a more practical approach for real-world applications where both privacy and accuracy are critical \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Furthermore, recent work has highlighted the potential of the shuffled model to significantly reduce sample complexity compared to the local model, especially for tasks like histogram estimation, where error is independent of domain size \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. This implies that the shuffled model could be a valuable tool for developing more efficient and privacy-preserving protocols in distributed settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\].

### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-vulnerabilities'></a>Local Differential Privacy Vulnerabilities: 3 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has been widely studied for its role in enabling privacy-preserving data analytics in distributed settings, where users perturb their data before sending it to an untrusted collector \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. However, recent research highlights critical vulnerabilities in LDP protocols, particularly their susceptibility to adversarial manipulation. For instance, noninteractive LDP protocols can be significantly manipulated by a small fraction of users, leading to the obscuring of the true data distribution when privacy levels are high or domain sizes are large \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Additionally, malicious data providers can exploit the inherent randomness in LDP mechanisms to skew estimations, as they can manipulate their outputs before transmission, leveraging the plausible deniability provided by randomization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These vulnerabilities are further exacerbated by the fact that attackers can inject fake users who send carefully crafted data to distort the results of frequency estimation or heavy hitter identification tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms that allow data collectors to verify the correctness of the randomization process performed by each user, thereby preventing output manipulation and mitigating the impact of attacks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. While some countermeasures have been explored, such as modifying existing LDP protocols to enhance robustness, their effectiveness varies across different scenarios, indicating a need for more comprehensive defenses \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. These findings underscore the importance of developing more secure and robust LDP protocols, particularly in light of the growing reliance on differential privacy for privacy-preserving analytics.

### <a id='category-efficient-dp-mechanism-improvements'></a>Efficient DP Mechanism Improvements: 4 papers found.
Efficient DP Mechanism Improvements focus on enhancing the practicality and performance of differentially private algorithms by addressing limitations in noise addition and data collection. For instance, the use of discrete Gaussian noise instead of continuous Gaussian noise mitigates issues related to numerical precision and interpretability, particularly when dealing with discrete data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. Similarly, improvements in Local Differential Privacy (LDP) protocols, such as incorporating post-processing steps to enforce non-negative frequency constraints, lead to better accuracy in estimating frequencies and other statistical properties \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, the development of new LDP mechanisms tailored for repeated data collection ensures strong privacy guarantees over time, even when telemetry data is collected regularly \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. Furthermore, revisiting the Gaussian mechanism has led to more accurate variance calibration, improving the efficiency and accuracy of noise addition in high-dimensional settings \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.06530)\]. These advancements collectively aim to make differential privacy more robust, scalable, and applicable to real-world scenarios.

### <a id='category-distributed-dp-protocols'></a>Distributed DP Protocols: 3 papers found.
Distributed DP Protocols focus on training models on private data spread across user devices while ensuring privacy through mechanisms like on-device noise addition and secure aggregation. These protocols aim to balance privacy, communication efficiency, and model accuracy by discretizing data, adding discrete Gaussian noise, and carefully analyzing the impact of quantization and modular arithmetic \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.06387)\]. Unlike the central model, which requires a trusted data collector, distributed approaches often rely on the local model, which offers stronger privacy guarantees but at the cost of reduced utility and algorithmic flexibility \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. Recent work, such as Cryptε, seeks to bridge this gap by enabling accurate and expressive DP programs without a trusted collector, using encrypted data processed by non-colluding servers \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. This approach leverages secure multi-party computation but addresses practical challenges like performance and security proofs. Additionally, the integration of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP) with DP has been explored, showing that while ZKPs can support verifiable DP, they are not strictly necessary \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. These advancements highlight ongoing efforts to enhance both the practicality and theoretical foundations of distributed DP systems.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-foundations'></a>Differential Privacy Foundations: 5 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy Foundations" explores the theoretical underpinnings and extensions of differential privacy (DP), focusing on both its core principles and advanced analytical tools. A key contribution is the introduction of a relaxation of DP based on Rényi divergence, which allows for more precise analysis of privacy guarantees, particularly in handling composite mechanisms \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This approach offers a compact and accurate representation of privacy, especially for tail behaviors of privacy loss, and supports tighter bounds on heterogeneous mechanisms \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, the foundational work on DP emphasizes the role of noise calibration in preserving privacy, where the standard deviation of added noise is adjusted according to the sensitivity of the query function \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This insight has led to significant improvements in the efficiency of privacy-preserving mechanisms, as demonstrated by reduced noise requirements for certain applications \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Furthermore, the category also examines the feasibility of private learning, where algorithms must ensure that outputs do not overly depend on individual data points, thereby maintaining confidentiality while enabling useful statistical inferences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of DP, addressing both theoretical challenges and practical implementations in diverse computational settings.
