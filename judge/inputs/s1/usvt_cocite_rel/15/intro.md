## Related Works

Brain tumor segmentation is a critical task in medical imaging, with significant implications for diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis. Several approaches have been proposed to address this challenge, leveraging deep learning techniques to improve segmentation accuracy and efficiency. Early works focused on 2D convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which, while effective for certain tasks, struggled with the complexity of 3D medical images \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4038)\]. The introduction of 3D CNNs, such as V-Net \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04797)\], enabled more accurate volumetric segmentation by capturing spatial dependencies across all three dimensions. However, 3D CNNs often suffer from high computational costs and memory consumption due to the increased number of parameters and operations \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.12188)\].

To mitigate these limitations, researchers have explored more efficient architectures. For instance, the U-Net architecture, originally designed for biomedical image segmentation, has been adapted for 3D applications through the development of 3D U-Net \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06650)\]. This architecture combines a contracting path for capturing context with an expanding path for precise localization, achieving competitive performance on challenging segmentation tasks. Similarly, the nnU-Net framework was introduced to automate the segmentation process, incorporating data augmentation and adaptive training strategies to improve performance on the BraTS datasets \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\]. nnU-Net has since become a popular baseline for brain tumor segmentation due to its robustness and adaptability.

Recent advances have also focused on integrating pre-trained models from other domains, such as ImageNet, into medical segmentation tasks. For example, the work by \[[28](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/97e975aa410035b087418df7dbfb1b7535c5141f)\] proposed a network architecture that partially utilizes depthwise separable convolutions to reduce computational costs while maintaining performance. This approach is particularly relevant for 3D brain tumor segmentation, where computational efficiency is crucial. Additionally, the integration of pre-trained weights from classification tasks, such as brain glioma grade classification, has shown promise in improving segmentation performance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.00382)\]. This is because pre-trained encoders can capture high-level features that are useful for segmentation, especially for challenging tumor labels.

Furthermore, the use of conditional random fields (CRFs) for post-processing has been explored to refine segmentation results and reduce false positives \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05959)\]. For instance, the work by \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05959)\] introduced a 3D fully connected CRF to post-process the output of a deep CNN, leading to improved segmentation accuracy on multi-channel MRI data. Similarly, the integration of FCNNs with CRFs has been proposed to enhance the robustness of segmentation models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04528)\].
