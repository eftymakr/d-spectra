## Related Works

The growing use of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in how they handle biases and uncertainties \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations and fail to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty, such as higher confidence in incorrect predictions across different groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more nuanced fairness assessments that consider model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\].

Several studies have explored fairness in machine learning, particularly in classification tasks. For instance, the concept of *counterfactual fairness* posits that a decision is fair if it remains consistent in both the actual world and a counterfactual world where the individual belongs to a different demographic group \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. This idea aligns with the need for fairness metrics that go beyond simple accuracy parity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. Similarly, *fairness through awareness* introduces a framework where fairness is ensured by treating similar individuals similarly, based on a task-specific similarity metric \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. These approaches emphasize the importance of fairness constraints in classification tasks, which is relevant to the development of fairness metrics for LLMs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\].

The issue of *disparate impact* has also been widely studied, particularly in the context of automated decision-making systems. Legal definitions of disparate impact, such as those used in U.S. law, highlight the importance of ensuring that selection processes do not disproportionately disadvantage certain groups \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. In machine learning, this has led to the development of fairness criteria that aim to balance error rates across different population groups \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. However, these criteria often fail to account for model uncertainty, which can lead to biased predictions despite similar accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\].

Recent work has also focused on the challenges of evaluating fairness in real-world applications, such as recidivism prediction instruments. These studies demonstrate that fairness criteria cannot always be simultaneously satisfied when prevalence rates differ across groups, and that disparate impact can arise even when error rate balance is maintained \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\]. This underscores the complexity of designing fairness metrics that are both effective and robust \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\].

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed new fairness definitions and evaluation frameworks. For example, *disparate mistreatment* is a new notion of unfairness that focuses on differences in misclassification rates across groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. This concept is particularly relevant to the development of fairness metrics for LLMs, as it highlights the need to account for differences in model confidence and prediction accuracy \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\].
