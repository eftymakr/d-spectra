## Related Works

The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) into large language models (LLMs) for enhancing reasoning capabilities in claim verification has gained significant attention in recent research. While KGs offer structured and semantically rich representations suitable for reasoning, most existing verification methods rely on unstructured text corpora, limiting their ability to effectively leverage KGs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. This limitation is particularly evident in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning and modular pipelines, where LLMs often struggle without adaptation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\]. 

Several approaches have explored the use of KGs in conjunction with LLMs. For instance, the work by \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07129)\] introduces a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) framework that combines pre-trained sequence-to-sequence models with dense vector indexes of Wikipedia, enabling more accurate and factually grounded language generation. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\] proposes a model that integrates knowledge graphs into language representation, demonstrating improved performance on knowledge-driven tasks. However, these methods often focus on extractive tasks rather than complex reasoning pipelines \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\].

In the realm of fact-checking, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\] presents a novel model called Program-Guided Fact-Checking (ProgramFC), which decomposes complex claims into sub-tasks and executes them using a shared library of specialized functions. This approach enhances explainability and data efficiency, making it a promising direction for structured knowledge integration. Additionally, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14974)\] explores the use of knowledge graphs in the context of scientific claim verification, proposing a system that leverages T5 for abstract retrieval and label prediction. These models demonstrate the potential of KGs in improving the accuracy of claim verification tasks.

Another line of research focuses on the zero-shot reasoning capabilities of LLMs. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.07606)\] shows that by simply adding a "Let's think step by step" prompt, LLMs can perform complex multi-step reasoning without any few-shot examples. This finding highlights the inherent reasoning abilities of LLMs and suggests that integrating structured knowledge from KGs could further enhance these capabilities. However, the lack of access to external knowledge bases often leads to hallucinations, as noted in \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)\].

### <a id='category-retrieval-augmented-generation-frameworks'></a>Retrieval-Augmented Generation Frameworks: 3 papers found.
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) frameworks have emerged as a promising approach to enhance the knowledge-intensive reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). These frameworks integrate pre-trained LLMs with external knowledge sources, such as dense vector indexes of Wikipedia, to improve factual accuracy and reduce hallucinations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. By combining parametric memory (the LLM) with non-parametric memory (external databases), RAG models can dynamically retrieve relevant information during generation, enabling more precise and context-aware responses \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. This approach has been shown to significantly improve performance on open-domain question-answering tasks, surpassing both parametric-only seq2seq models and task-specific retrieve-and-extract architectures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Furthermore, RAG models can adapt to new knowledge by continuously updating their external memory, allowing for more transparent and traceable reasoning processes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\]. Recent studies have also explored the effectiveness of RAG in handling less popular factual knowledge, demonstrating that retrieval augmentation can significantly enhance performance in these cases, while scaling primarily improves memorization of common knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07129)\]. The integration of RAG with structured knowledge sources, such as knowledge graphs, further extends its applicability to tasks requiring complex reasoning and claim verification \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\].

### <a id='category-fact-checking-approaches-with-subtask-decomposition'></a>Fact-Checking Approaches with Subtask Decomposition: 4 papers found.
The category "Fact-Checking Approaches with Subtask Decomposition" focuses on advanced methodologies for verifying factual claims by breaking down complex tasks into manageable subtasks. These approaches leverage large language models (LLMs) and specialized functions to enhance the accuracy and explainability of fact-checking systems. For instance, Program-Guided Fact-Checking (ProgramFC) utilizes in-context learning to generate reasoning programs that guide the verification process, allowing for modular execution of subtasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Similarly, the VerT5erini system adapts pretrained sequence-to-sequence models to perform critical subtasks such as abstract retrieval, sentence selection, and label prediction, demonstrating strong performance on scientific claim verification tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. The SciFact dataset, which includes 1.4K expert-written scientific claims paired with evidence-containing abstracts, provides a valuable resource for training and evaluating these models, highlighting the importance of combining large-scale datasets with domain-specific knowledge \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\]. Additionally, the integration of structured knowledge from knowledge graphs (KGs) into fact-checking pipelines, as seen in the ClaimPKG framework, represents a significant advancement by enabling LLMs to reason over structured data and improve generalizability across different datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\]. These approaches collectively emphasize the need for modular, explainable, and data-efficient fact-checking systems that can effectively handle complex claims in both structured and unstructured domains.

### <a id='category-knowledge-graph-integration-in-language-representation'></a>Knowledge Graph Integration in Language Representation: 4 papers found.
The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) into pre-trained language models has gained significant attention for enhancing language understanding and reasoning capabilities. Several studies have explored how incorporating structured knowledge from KGs can complement the semantic representations learned by models like BERT. For instance, ERNIE \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07129)\] leverages both large-scale textual corpora and KGs to train a model that simultaneously captures lexical, syntactic, and knowledge information, achieving improvements on knowledge-driven tasks. Similarly, KEPLER \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\] proposes a unified framework that integrates factual knowledge from KGs with pre-trained language representations, enabling better performance on NLP tasks and serving as an effective inductive knowledge embedding model. K-BERT \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.07606)\] further extends this idea by injecting domain-specific knowledge from KGs into BERT, allowing for domain adaptation without requiring extensive retraining. It addresses the challenge of knowledge noise by introducing mechanisms to limit the impact of injected knowledge. CoLAKE \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00309)\] takes a different approach by jointly learning contextualized representations for both language and knowledge, extracting knowledge context from large-scale knowledge bases and integrating it with language context in a unified structure. These approaches highlight the potential of combining KGs with pre-trained models to improve reasoning and task performance, although challenges remain in effectively integrating heterogeneous information and ensuring robustness across different domains.

### <a id='category-llm-hallucinations-and-external-knowledge-sources'></a>LLM Hallucinations and External Knowledge Sources: 3 papers found.
The category "LLM Hallucinations and External Knowledge Sources" highlights critical challenges in the reliability and accuracy of large language models (LLMs), particularly their tendency to generate non-factual or hallucinated outputs. Studies show that LLMs like ChatGPT and GPT-3.5 often produce factual outputs less than 25% of the time, underscoring the need for external knowledge verification \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\]. This issue is exacerbated by the lack of access to real-time or external knowledge bases, leading to extrinsic hallucinations based on parametric memory \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Furthermore, LLMs exhibit varying performance across reasoning tasks, with strengths in deductive reasoning but weaknesses in inductive and commonsense reasoning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Research also indicates that while LLMs can be repurposed as effective fact verifiers, their performance depends on high-quality evidence and robustness, with some models like FLAN-T5-11B outperforming others in verification tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\]. These findings emphasize the importance of integrating external knowledge sources and improving model alignment to enhance the reliability of LLM outputs. Additionally, the interactive nature of models like ChatGPT allows for human collaboration to improve performance through prompt engineering, though this remains a limited solution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Overall, the category underscores the necessity of developing more trustworthy LLMs capable of accurate reasoning and fact verification.
