## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in traditional sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges, primarily leveraging self-attention mechanisms and domain-specific components to improve recommendation accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. However, existing CDSR frameworks often overlook the core self-attention module, which is a powerful tool for learning correlations among user behaviors \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\].

Early works on cross-domain recommendation, such as CoNet \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\], introduced deep transfer learning to bridge knowledge between domains by connecting hidden layers of neural networks. This approach enabled dual knowledge transfer across domains, improving recommendation performance. However, these methods often rely on overlapping users and ignore the complex interactions between items and users across domains \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\].

More recently, models like DASL \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] and π-Net \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\] have focused on capturing cross-domain user preferences through dual learning mechanisms and shared account filtering. DASL uses dual embeddings and dual attention to jointly learn representations across domains, while π-Net addresses shared account scenarios by filtering out irrelevant user information \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of modeling both intra- and inter-domain interactions to enhance recommendation accuracy.

The use of graph neural networks has also been explored in cross-domain settings, such as in DA-GCN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\], which constructs domain-aware graphs to capture user-item relationships. Similarly, the Mixed Information Flow Network \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] considers both behavioral and knowledge flows to improve recommendations across domains. These approaches highlight the need for modeling complex interactions between items and users across multiple domains.

Attention-based models, such as SASRec \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6dd0605f7de7581c69b4acf0ab78000748a09f3e)\] and BERT4Rec \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\], have been widely adopted in sequential recommendation due to their ability to capture long-term dependencies. However, they often assume a rigidly ordered sequence, which may not be suitable for real-world scenarios with varying interaction patterns \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08272)\]. Recent work has attempted to address this by incorporating time interval information, as seen in TiSASRec \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\], which models both the absolute positions of items and the time intervals between them.

In the context of cross-domain sequential recommendation, models like DREAM \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\] and CMVCDR \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\] have introduced novel mechanisms to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences.
