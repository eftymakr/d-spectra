## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in traditional sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges, primarily leveraging self-attention mechanisms and domain-specific components to improve recommendation accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. However, existing CDSR frameworks often overlook the core self-attention module, which is a powerful tool for learning correlations among user behaviors \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\].

Early works on cross-domain recommendation, such as CoNet \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\], introduced deep transfer learning to bridge knowledge between domains by connecting hidden layers of neural networks. This approach enabled dual knowledge transfer across domains, improving recommendation performance. However, these methods often rely on overlapping users and ignore the complex interactions between items and users across domains \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\].

More recently, models like DASL \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] and π-Net \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\] have focused on capturing cross-domain user preferences through dual learning mechanisms and shared account filtering. DASL uses dual embeddings and dual attention to jointly learn representations across domains, while π-Net addresses shared account scenarios by filtering out irrelevant user information \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of modeling both intra- and inter-domain interactions to enhance recommendation accuracy.

The use of graph neural networks has also been explored in cross-domain settings, such as in DA-GCN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\], which constructs domain-aware graphs to capture user-item relationships. Similarly, the Mixed Information Flow Network \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] considers both behavioral and knowledge flows to improve recommendations across domains. These approaches highlight the need for modeling complex interactions between items and users across multiple domains.

Attention-based models, such as SASRec \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6dd0605f7de7581c69b4acf0ab78000748a09f3e)\] and BERT4Rec \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\], have been widely adopted in sequential recommendation due to their ability to capture long-term dependencies. However, they often assume a rigidly ordered sequence, which may not be suitable for real-world scenarios with varying interaction patterns \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08272)\]. Recent work has attempted to address this by incorporating time interval information, as seen in TiSASRec \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\], which models both the absolute positions of items and the time intervals between them.

### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation-frameworks'></a>Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation Frameworks: 23 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research direction in recommender systems, aiming to enhance recommendation performance by leveraging sequential interactions across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Existing approaches typically rely on self-attention mechanisms within transformer architectures, often augmenting them with domain-specific modules to capture cross-domain correlations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. However, these methods often overlook the inherent power of the self-attention module itself in learning complex behavioral correlations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Recent studies have explored alternative strategies, such as dual learning frameworks that iteratively transfer information between domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\], and graph-based models that explicitly model cross-domain relationships \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Additionally, some works focus on improving the modeling of inter-sequence and intra-sequence item interactions to better capture user preferences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Others address the limitations of traditional transfer methods by incorporating contrastive learning and attention mechanisms to enhance cross-domain alignment \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. Furthermore, there is growing interest in handling data sparsity and cold-start problems through adaptive and open-world assumptions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These approaches highlight the diversity of techniques used in CDSR, ranging from attention-based models to graph neural networks and contrastive learning, all aiming to improve recommendation accuracy while addressing key challenges such as domain adaptation and user preference modeling \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\].

### <a id='category-self-attention-mechanisms-in-recommender-systems'></a>Self-Attention Mechanisms in Recommender Systems: 3 papers found.
Sequential recommendation systems aim to predict user actions based on their historical interactions, with recent approaches leveraging self-attention mechanisms to capture complex sequential patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Traditional methods such as Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been widely used, with MCs excelling in sparse datasets due to their simplicity, while RNNs offer better performance in dense environments by modeling longer-term dependencies \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, these methods often fail to fully exploit the power of self-attention, which allows for more flexible and powerful modeling of sequential data \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Recent works like SASRec and BERT4Rec have introduced self-attention-based models that improve recommendation accuracy by capturing both short- and long-term dependencies \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Furthermore, models such as TiSASRec have extended self-attention to incorporate temporal information, explicitly modeling time intervals between interactions to enhance prediction accuracy \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of self-attention mechanisms in sequential recommendation, offering a more nuanced understanding of user behavior across different contexts \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]\[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\].

Transfer learning is a class of algorithms underlying these techniques. In this paper, we propose a novel transfer learning approach for cross-domain recommendation by using neural networks as the base model. In contrast to the matrix factorization based cross-domain techniques, our method is deep transfer learning, which can learn complex user-item interaction relationships. We assume that hidden layers in two base networks are connected by cross mappings, leading to the collaborative cross networks (CoNet). CoNet enables dual knowledge transfer across domains by introducing cross connections from one base network to another and vice versa.
