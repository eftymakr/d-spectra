## Related Works

Modern databases often adopt weak isolation levels to achieve high availability and performance, sacrificing some consistency guarantees in favor of scalability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. This trade-off has led to the emergence of isolation bugs in production systems, which can compromise data consistency \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. Black-box testing has emerged as a key technique for detecting such bugs by verifying whether transaction histories adhere to prescribed isolation levels \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. However, testing large-scale databases remains challenging due to the computational complexity of existing isolation checkers \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\].

Several works have explored the complexity of checking transactional consistency. For instance, it has been shown that consistency models like read committed, read atomic, and causal consistency are polynomial-time checkable, while snapshot isolation and prefix consistency are NP-complete in general \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04509)\]. These results highlight the computational difficulty of verifying strong isolation guarantees, which has motivated the development of efficient tools for weak isolation levels \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. 

The paper introduces AWDIT, a highly-efficient and provably optimal tester for weak database isolation, which improves upon existing methods by reducing the complexity of testing for Read Committed (RC), Read Atomic (RA), and Causal Consistency (CC) to $O(n^(3/2))$, $O(n^(3/2))$, and $O(n \cdot k)$, respectively \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. This represents a significant improvement over the state of the art, where existing testers have large polynomial complexity, limiting their applicability to moderate-sized workloads \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. 

AWDIT's efficiency is further supported by theoretical lower bounds, which establish that any weak isolation level between RC and CC has a conditional lower bound of $n^(3/2)$ \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. This implies that AWDIT is essentially optimal, as it matches the best possible performance for these types of checks. Experimental results also demonstrate that AWDIT is significantly faster than existing, highly optimized testers, achieving average speedups of up to $245\times$, $193\times$, and $62\times$ for RC, RA, and CC, respectively, on the largest histories \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\].

Other works have also addressed the challenge of testing weak isolation levels. For example, Plume is a black-box checker for weak isolation levels that leverages vectors and tree clocks to accelerate isolation checking \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cbe191bda20907994d45bc835d20446968370add)\]. MonkeyDB, on the other hand, is a mock storage system designed to test storage-backed applications under weak isolation levels by simulating all possible return values for read operations \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/615ff2896b52bee2cbfd64ced324106a29b73397)\].
