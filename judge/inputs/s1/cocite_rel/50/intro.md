## Related Works

Knowledge graphs (KGs) have emerged as a critical tool for representing and reasoning over structured knowledge, enabling applications such as question answering, information retrieval, and link prediction \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.00388)\]. Early approaches focused on embedding entities and relations into continuous vector spaces, with models like TransE \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2a3f862199883ceff5e3c74126f0c80770653e05)\] and DistMult \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01476)\] being among the first to achieve state-of-the-art results. However, these models often struggled with capturing complex relational patterns and preserving mapping properties such as reflexivity and many-to-many relations \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2a3f862199883ceff5e3c74126f0c80770653e05)\]. To address these limitations, more advanced models such as TransH \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2a3f862199883ceff5e3c74126f0c80770653e05)\] and ConvE \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01476)\] were introduced. TransH models relations as hyperplanes, allowing for better preservation of relational properties while maintaining efficiency, whereas ConvE leverages multi-layer convolutional networks to achieve high performance on link prediction tasks with fewer parameters \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.01476)\]. 

The importance of relational modeling in KGs has also been highlighted in the development of Relational Graph Convolutional Networks (R-GCNs) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103)\], which are specifically designed to handle the multi-relational nature of knowledge bases. R-GCNs have shown significant improvements over traditional factorization models, particularly in tasks such as link prediction and entity classification \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06103)\]. Additionally, the challenge of handling long-tail relations, where many relations have few known triples, has led to the development of one-shot relational learning frameworks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09040)\]. These frameworks aim to predict new facts with minimal training data, leveraging both learned embeddings and graph structures to improve performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09040)\].

Recent work has also explored the integration of large language models (LLMs) with KGs to enhance reasoning and knowledge representation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. While LLMs excel in generalization and natural language understanding, they often lack structured factual knowledge, which KGs can provide. Conversely, KGs are difficult to scale and update, making it essential to develop methods that allow for the dynamic integration of new knowledge \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. This has led to the development of hybrid approaches, such as KEPLER \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\], which combines pre-trained language models with knowledge embedding techniques to better capture factual knowledge and improve performance on both NLP tasks and KG link prediction.

In parallel, the need for robust and scalable KGs has driven the development of large-scale knowledge graph benchmarks and datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03975)\]. For example, ConceptNet \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03975)\] provides a multilingual graph of general knowledge, enhancing word embeddings with semantic relationships. Such resources are crucial for training and evaluating KG embedding models, as well as for advancing research in knowledge acquisition and completion \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.00388)\].
