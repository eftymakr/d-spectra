## Related Works

Video frame interpolation has been a focal point in computer vision, aiming to generate intermediate frames between existing ones to enhance temporal resolution. Early methods typically followed a two-step approach: motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, these methods were often limited by the accuracy of optical flow estimation and struggled with challenges like occlusion and blur \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. More recent approaches have sought to merge these steps into a single process, leveraging deep learning for more robust and efficient results \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\].

Several works have explored the use of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for video frame interpolation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\] proposed a method that combines motion estimation and synthesis through a single convolutional process, allowing for high-quality interpolation by adapting kernels spatially. Similarly, \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a285a442317f24068f7cc262531976e82a5c41d6)\] introduced a method that uses separable convolutions to reduce the number of parameters while maintaining performance, which is particularly useful for handling large motions. These approaches have laid the groundwork for more advanced models that incorporate additional features such as depth and context \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\].

The use of optical flow remains a cornerstone in many interpolation techniques, as it provides a way to estimate motion between frames \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01280)\]. However, traditional optical flow methods often fail in complex scenarios due to their reliance on hand-crafted features \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. To address this, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00627)\] introduced a method that uses bi-directional optical flow and visibility maps to refine the interpolation process, effectively reducing artifacts caused by occlusions. This approach has been further refined in \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\], where a U-Net architecture is used to compute and refine optical flow, leading to more accurate intermediate frame generation.

Another significant advancement comes from \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12622)\], which proposed a method based on adaptive separable convolutions. This approach not only reduces computational complexity but also improves the robustness of the model against challenging scenarios. The work in \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\] further enhances this by introducing a channel attention mechanism, which allows the model to focus on relevant features during the interpolation process, leading to better performance in the presence of motion and occlusion.

In addition to optical flow-based methods, some works have explored alternative approaches such as phase-based motion representation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05534)\]. These methods aim to capture motion in a more perceptually meaningful way, which can lead to more natural-looking interpolated frames. However, they are often limited in the amount of motion they can handle \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\]. To overcome this, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\] proposed a method that explicitly detects occlusion using depth information, leading to more accurate synthesis of intermediate frames.
