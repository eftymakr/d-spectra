## Related Works

Brain tumor segmentation remains a critical challenge in medical imaging due to the high variability in tumor shapes, sizes, and intensities across different patients and imaging modalities \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/420f3f1078a6d8e0696572c032877079286051c6)\]. Traditional manual segmentation methods are time-consuming and prone to human error, while automated approaches have gained traction with the advent of deep learning techniques \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05959)\]. Several studies have explored convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for this task, with U-Net and its variants being among the most successful \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\]. The U-Net architecture, introduced by Ronneberger et al., features an encoder-decoder structure with skip connections, enabling precise localization of tumor regions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.03540)\]. Subsequent works have extended U-Net to 3D for volumetric MRI data, such as the 3D U-Net \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04597)\], which addresses the challenges of 3D segmentation by incorporating 3D convolutions and dense connections \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11654)\].

The integration of pre-trained models has also been explored to improve segmentation performance. For instance, the work by Zhang et al. proposed using pre-trained weights from ImageNet to initialize the CNNs, which helped reduce training time and improve generalization \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04797)\]. Similarly, the nnU-Net framework, introduced by Isensee et al., automatically configures the network architecture and data augmentation strategies, leading to state-of-the-art results on the BraTS dataset \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03820)\]. However, nnU-Net requires extensive training and may underutilize pre-trained weights, which limits its efficiency in certain scenarios \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/580062407427236ced45253a2ff7df2e147a81e2)\].
