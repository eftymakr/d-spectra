## Related Works

Protein dynamics are essential for understanding biological functions and properties, yet traditional methods such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are computationally expensive and time-consuming \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling have enabled more efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven approaches remains a challenge, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges and contribute to the field of protein structure prediction and conformation sampling.

Several studies have explored the use of generative models for protein structure prediction. For example, AlphaFold has achieved atomic accuracy in predicting protein structures, even in cases where no similar structure is available \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. Similarly, EigenFold uses diffusion models to sample a distribution of structures from a given protein sequence, achieving a median TMScore of 0.84 \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\]. These models rely on learning from large datasets of known protein structures and have demonstrated significant improvements in accuracy and efficiency \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18a93dc1558bf9d7534d0b416633cebaf75c1145)\].

Diffusion models have also been applied to protein conformation sampling. For instance, the work by \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\] presents a diffusion-based generative model that generates protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state towards a stable folded structure. This approach mirrors the natural folding process and has been shown to generate highly realistic protein structures \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c49a0912595a1cc70aab63524f64ed08c92194a8)\]. Similarly, the Str2Str framework leverages an amortized denoising score matching objective trained on general crystal structures to enable zero-shot conformation sampling without reliance on simulation data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of diffusion models to generate diverse and accurate protein conformations.

The integration of physical supervision into generative models has also been explored. For example, the Boltzmann generators use neural networks to learn a coordinate transformation from a system’s configurations to a latent space representation, enabling efficient sampling of equilibrium states \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. This approach combines deep learning with statistical mechanics to generate unbiased equilibrium samples of many-body systems and protein molecules \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1ccd3a55ec1200295ce99ec8d934668dc8f753d8)\]. Similarly, the Distributional Graphormer (DiG) framework employs deep neural networks to transform a simple distribution towards the equilibrium distribution, conditioned on a descriptor of a molecular system \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d9bb6d9590949392cae342b75323826188b1b8a2)\]. These methods highlight the importance of incorporating physical principles into generative models to improve their accuracy and efficiency.

### <a id='category-zero-shot-conformation-sampling-techniques'></a>Zero-Shot Conformation Sampling Techniques: 2 papers found.
Recent advances in zero-shot conformation sampling techniques have aimed to overcome the computational limitations of traditional molecular dynamics (MD) and Monte Carlo (MC) simulations by leveraging machine learning models. These methods seek to generate physically realistic protein conformations without the need for extensive simulation data or sampling, thereby reducing computational costs \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. For instance, idpGAN, a generative adversarial network based on a transformer architecture, has been trained on coarse-grained simulations of intrinsically disordered peptides to produce sequence-dependent conformational ensembles, demonstrating transferability beyond the training data \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Similarly, Str2Str, a structure-to-structure translation framework inspired by simulated annealing, enables zero-shot conformation sampling with roto-translation equivariant properties, relying on an amortized denoising score matching objective trained on general crystal structures without simulation data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of machine learning to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of conformational sampling, offering promising avenues for studying protein dynamics in structural biology and drug discovery.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-protein-conformation-sampling'></a>Diffusion Models in Protein Conformation Sampling: 4 papers found.
Recent advances in diffusion models have significantly impacted protein conformation sampling, offering new approaches to generate and predict protein structures with high accuracy and diversity. These models, such as Chroma \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\], EigenFold \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\], and others, leverage diffusion processes to simulate the natural folding behavior of proteins, enabling the generation of novel yet physically plausible structures. Chroma introduces a diffusion process that respects the conformational statistics of polymer ensembles, allowing for the design of proteins with desired properties and functions \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. Similarly, EigenFold employs a diffusion framework that models protein structures as harmonic oscillators, providing a cascading-resolution generative process and improving the modeling of conformational ensembles \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Another approach, described in a work focused on diffusion on SE(3), introduces FrameDiff, a framework that enables the generation of functional protein backbones without relying on pretrained structure prediction networks, thus enhancing the flexibility and generalization of the models \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02277)\]. Additionally, a diffusion-based generative model inspired by the natural folding process generates protein backbone structures by denoising from an unfolded state to a folded one, capturing the structural complexity of naturally occurring proteins \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. These models collectively address the challenges of generating diverse and foldable protein structures, advancing the field of protein design and conformation sampling.

### <a id='category-large-language-models-for-protein-prediction'></a>Large Language Models for Protein Prediction: 3 papers found.
Recent advances in protein prediction have focused on leveraging large language models and deep learning to predict protein structures with high accuracy and efficiency. These models, such as AlphaFold \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\], have demonstrated the ability to predict protein structures with atomic accuracy, even in the absence of homologous structures \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These models have shown significant improvements in predicting mutational effects and secondary structure, as well as enhancing long-range contact prediction \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. Furthermore, large language models have been scaled to handle extensive datasets, enabling the prediction of protein structures at an unprecedented scale and speed \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. These developments highlight the potential of integrating large-scale data and advanced machine learning techniques to address the challenges in protein structure prediction and conformation sampling \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\].

### <a id='category-integration-of-statistical-mechanics'></a>Integration of Statistical Mechanics: 2 papers found.
The integration of statistical mechanics into the study of macromolecular systems focuses on characterizing slow relaxation processes by identifying structural changes and estimating the associated timescales \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\]. This is typically achieved by discretizing the high-dimensional state space and analyzing the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of a transition matrix, which underpins methods like Markov models, master-equation models, and kinetic network models \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\]. A key challenge lies in the accurate discretization of slow order parameters without relying on subjective user input \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\]. To address this, the variational principle of conformation dynamics is employed to derive an optimal method for identifying the "slow subspace" from a set of prior order parameters, either generic internal coordinates or user-defined parameters \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\]. This approach leverages time-lagged independent component analysis (tICA) as an optimal solution, enabling the identification of optimal indicators that serve as reaction coordinates \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\]. These indicators reveal the structural changes associated with slow processes, as demonstrated in the analysis of molecular dynamics simulations for the 6-residue fluorescent peptide MR121-GSGSW and the 30-residue intrinsically disordered peptide kinase inducible domain (KID) \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\]. This integration of statistical mechanics thus provides a robust framework for constructing accurate kinetic models of complex molecular systems \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1302.6614)\].
