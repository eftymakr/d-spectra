## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area in natural language processing, aiming to generate dynamic embeddings that adapt to user instructions. This concept aligns with recent efforts to enhance the flexibility and applicability of text embeddings in diverse downstream tasks. Early work in this area includes Skip-Thought Vectors \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06726)\], which introduced an unsupervised approach to learn generic sentence representations by reconstructing surrounding sentences. While effective, these models lacked the ability to adapt to specific instructions. 

More recently, methods such as SimCSE \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\] and Sentence-T5 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.08877)\] have explored contrastive learning and encoder-decoder architectures to improve the quality of sentence embeddings. SimCSE, in particular, demonstrated that unsupervised contrastive learning can achieve performance comparable to supervised methods, while Sentence-T5 extended this idea by scaling up to larger parameter counts, showing consistent improvements in performance. These models, however, typically require re-encoding the entire corpus for each new instruction, leading to significant computational overhead \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07899)\].

The need for efficient instruction-following embeddings has also been addressed in the context of information retrieval. For instance, INSTRUCTOR \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\] introduces a single embedder that can generate task-specific embeddings without additional training, leveraging instruction finetuning. Similarly, the INSTRUCTIR benchmark \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14334)\] focuses on evaluating how well retrievers can follow user instructions, highlighting the gap in current models' ability to align with user intent. These works emphasize the importance of instruction-aware models but often rely on extensive training or re-encoding, which limits scalability.

To address these limitations, recent approaches such as Dense Passage Retrieval \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\] and FollowIR \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15246)\] have explored dense retrieval methods that can better handle complex instructions. FollowIR, in particular, introduces a dataset and evaluation framework to assess how well IR models can follow detailed instructions, showing that existing models struggle to interpret and apply long-form instructions effectively. These studies underscore the need for more efficient and adaptable embedding methods.

In the realm of instruction-following, the Self-Instruct framework \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\] proposes a method to bootstrap instruction data from the model's own generations, significantly reducing the reliance on human-annotated datasets. This approach enables the creation of large-scale synthetic instruction datasets, which can then be used to fine-tune models. This is a crucial step toward developing more generalizable and instruction-aware models.
