## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match speech content. Early approaches often relied on phoneme-level feature extraction, which limited the ability to capture complex facial dynamics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. For instance, the work in \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\] proposed a Transformer-based model to encode long-term audio context and autoregressively predict 3D face meshes, improving upon phoneme-level feature learning. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\] introduced a method that disentangles emotion and content in speech, enabling more expressive facial animations. These methods, however, often struggle with capturing the full range of facial movements, particularly in the upper face and non-lip regions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\].

Recent advances have focused on improving lip synchronization and facial expression realism. The paper \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\] introduced a method that learns viseme dynamics from speech videos, producing animator-friendly viseme curves and supporting multilingual speech inputs. This approach was further enhanced in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\], where a parametric viseme fitting algorithm was proposed to better correlate viseme curves with phonemes, leading to more controllable and realistic facial animations. Additionally, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\] proposed a framework that learns audio-visual representations in a disentangled manner, enabling arbitrary-subject talking face generation with clearer lip motion patterns than previous work.

Another key challenge in this field is the lack of high-quality, aligned 3D emotional datasets with synchronized audio. To address this, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\] introduced a large-scale 3D emotional talking face dataset (3D-ETF), which was used to train models that can generate facial expressions with rich emotional content. This dataset was also utilized in \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\], where a method was proposed to generate one-shot emotional talking faces by involving an emotion source video. These works highlight the importance of emotional disentanglement and the need for diverse, high-quality datasets to train robust models.

The use of self-supervised learning has also gained traction, as it allows models to learn from large amounts of unlabeled data. For example, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\] proposed a method that learns audio-driven facial animations by integrating self-supervised pre-trained speech representations, improving the generalization of the models to unseen data. Similarly, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\] introduced a system that generates high-quality video portraits with vivid emotional dynamics driven by audio, leveraging cross-reconstructed emotion disentanglement techniques.

In the realm of Transformer-based audio-visual models, recent research has focused on improving the alignment and generation of facial animations driven by speech. These approaches highlight the growing interest in leveraging Transformer models to better capture the complex interplay between audio and visual modalities in speech-driven facial animation tasks.

### <a id='category-emotion-and-content-disentanglement-techniques'></a>Emotion and Content Disentanglement Techniques: 5 papers found.
In the field of emotion and content disentanglement techniques for speech-driven 3D face animation, recent research has focused on addressing the limitations of existing methods that either neglect emotional facial expressions or fail to disentangle them from speech content \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. For instance, some studies have introduced techniques such as the Cross-Reconstructed Emotion Disentanglement method, which decomposes speech into emotion and content spaces to enable more natural and expressive facial animations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Others, like the Emotion-Aware Motion Model (EAMM), incorporate emotion source videos to generate emotional talking faces on arbitrary subjects by modeling emotion-related facial dynamics as additive displacements to motion representations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. Additionally, methods such as EMOTE have been developed to generate 3D talking-head avatars with explicit emotional control, using decoupled losses for speech and emotion to ensure lip-sync while allowing for emotional expression \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. These approaches highlight the growing emphasis on integrating emotional dynamics with speech content to achieve more realistic and controllable 3D facial animations. Furthermore, the use of facial blendshapes and large-scale datasets, such as the 3D-ETF dataset, has been proposed to improve the quality and diversity of generated facial expressions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. Overall, these works demonstrate a shift towards more sophisticated and emotionally expressive speech-driven facial animation systems.

### <a id='category-viseme-dynamics-and-lip-synchronization'></a>Viseme Dynamics and Lip Synchronization: 11 papers found.
In the field of speech-driven facial animation, several approaches have been proposed to improve lip synchronization and facial expression generation. Researchers have focused on leveraging audio-visual correlations, often using phoneme-based models or deep learning techniques to align speech with facial movements \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. For instance, some works utilize viseme dynamics, learning from speech videos to produce animator-friendly viseme curves that correlate closely with phonemes, enabling more controllable and realistic facial animations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\]. Furthermore, some studies have focused on generating talking-head videos from a single reference image, using motion-aware recurrent neural networks and dense motion field representations to produce natural head motions and maintain speaker appearance consistency \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09293)\]. These methods collectively address challenges such as lip synchronization, facial expression realism, and speaker identity preservation, contributing to the advancement of speech-driven facial animation techniques.
