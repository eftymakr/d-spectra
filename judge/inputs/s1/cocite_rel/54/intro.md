## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, owing to their ability to model complex interactions between users and items \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early GCN variants, such as LightGCN, simplified the design by removing feature transformations and nonlinear activations, focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation to improve efficiency and performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This approach aligns with the broader trend of simplifying GCNs to make them more suitable for recommendation tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. However, despite these advancements, existing GCN-based models still face limitations in capturing high-order interactions and modeling diverse user intents \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\].

A significant body of work has focused on improving the expressiveness of GCNs by incorporating more sophisticated graph convolution mechanisms. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure, thereby capturing high-order connectivity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. Similarly, Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) introduces a more nuanced approach by disentangling user intents and modeling them at a finer granularity \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. These methods highlight the importance of incorporating structural information from the user-item graph to enhance recommendation accuracy.

Another line of research has explored the use of spectral methods for graph-based recommendation. Spectral Collaborative Filtering (SpectralCF) directly operates in the spectral domain, leveraging the connectivity information hidden in the graph to discover deeper relationships between users and items \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. This approach is particularly effective in addressing the cold-start problem, which remains a challenge for traditional CF methods \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. Additionally, BernNet introduces a novel framework for learning arbitrary graph spectral filters using Bernstein polynomial approximations, enabling more flexible and accurate modeling of graph signals \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\].

Efforts to improve the scalability and efficiency of graph-based recommendation systems have also led to the development of simplified models. UltraGCN, for example, skips infinite layers of message passing and instead approximates the limit of infinite-layer graph convolutions via a constraint loss, achieving significant speedups while maintaining performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\]. These advancements underscore the ongoing efforts to balance model complexity with computational efficiency.

In addition to these approaches, recent work has focused on self-supervised learning for recommendation, aiming to improve the robustness and generalization of GCNs. Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL) introduces auxiliary tasks to reinforce node representation learning, particularly beneficial for long-tail items and noisy interactions \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\].
