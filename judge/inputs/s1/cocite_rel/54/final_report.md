## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, owing to their ability to model complex interactions between users and items \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early GCN variants, such as LightGCN, simplified the design by removing feature transformations and nonlinear activations, focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation to improve efficiency and performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This approach aligns with the broader trend of simplifying GCNs to make them more suitable for recommendation tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. However, despite these advancements, existing GCN-based models still face limitations in capturing high-order interactions and modeling diverse user intents \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\].

A significant body of work has focused on improving the expressiveness of GCNs by incorporating more sophisticated graph convolution mechanisms. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure, thereby capturing high-order connectivity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. Similarly, Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) introduces a more nuanced approach by disentangling user intents and modeling them at a finer granularity \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. These methods highlight the importance of incorporating structural information from the user-item graph to enhance recommendation accuracy.

Another line of research has explored the use of spectral methods for graph-based recommendation. Spectral Collaborative Filtering (SpectralCF) directly operates in the spectral domain, leveraging the connectivity information hidden in the graph to discover deeper relationships between users and items \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. This approach is particularly effective in addressing the cold-start problem, which remains a challenge for traditional CF methods \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. Additionally, BernNet introduces a novel framework for learning arbitrary graph spectral filters using Bernstein polynomial approximations, enabling more flexible and accurate modeling of graph signals \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\].

Efforts to improve the scalability and efficiency of graph-based recommendation systems have also led to the development of simplified models. UltraGCN, for example, skips infinite layers of message passing and instead approximates the limit of infinite-layer graph convolutions via a constraint loss, achieving significant speedups while maintaining performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\]. These advancements underscore the ongoing efforts to balance model complexity with computational efficiency.

In addition to these approaches, recent work has focused on self-supervised learning for recommendation, aiming to improve the robustness and generalization of GCNs. Furthermore, the PPNP model improves upon existing GCN-based methods by utilizing personalized PageRank for propagation, allowing for a larger adjustable neighborhood and efficient training \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05997)\]. These developments collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance the efficiency, scalability, and effectiveness of graph convolution mechanisms for real-world applications.

### <a id='category-limitations-of-existing-gcn-based-models'></a>Limitations of Existing GCN-Based Models: 4 papers found.
The related works section focuses on the limitations of existing GCN-based models, which are critical to understanding the innovations in the proposed ChebyCF framework. Current models, such as those based on collaborative filtering, often rely on user and item embeddings learned from sparse interaction data, which limits their ability to capture nuanced preference patterns \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10322)\]. Additionally, neighborhood aggregation mechanisms in GCNs are constrained in their capacity to leverage diverse and fine-grained preference patterns, as they typically use linear operations that restrict the model's expressiveness \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)\]. These limitations are rooted in the spectral graph theory underpinning GCNs, where graph filtering is limited by a cut-off in the frequency spectrum and a restricted linear form, leading to over-smoothing and poor representation of complex graph structures \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\]. Furthermore, existing models often require significant labeled data for training and validation, which is impractical in many real-world scenarios \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07293)\]. These challenges highlight the need for more flexible and expressive graph filtering approaches that can better capture the underlying patterns in user-item interactions. The proposed ChebyCF framework addresses these issues by utilizing raw interaction history instead of learned embeddings, allowing for the full spectrum of signals to be utilized, and by employing Chebyshev interpolation to approximate a flexible non-linear graph filter \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

[Snippet]: «Item-based Collaborative Filtering (ICF) has been widely adopted in recommender systems in industry, owing to its strength in user interest modeling and ease in online personalization. By constructing a user’s profile with the items that the user has consumed, ICF recommends items that are similar to the user’s profile. With the prevalence of machine learning in recent years, significant processes have been made for ICF by learning item similarity (or representation) from data. Nevertheless, we argue that most existing works have only considered linear and shallow relationships between items, which are insufficient to capture the complicated decision-making process of users. In this article, we propose a more expressive ICF solution by accounting for the nonlinear and higher-order relationships among items. Going beyond modeling only the second-order interaction (e.g., similarity) between two items, we additionally consider the interaction among all interacted item pairs by using nonlinear neural networks. By doing this, we can effectively model the higher-order relationship among items, capturing more complicated effects in user decision-making. For example, it can differentiate which historical itemsets in a user’s profile are more important in affecting the user to make a purchase decision on an item. We treat this solution as a deep variant of ICF, thus term it as DeepICF. To justify our proposal, we perform empirical studies on two public datasets from MovieLens and Pinterest. Extensive experiments verify the highly positive effect of higher-order item interaction modeling with nonlinear neural networks. Moreover, we demonstrate that by more fine-grained second-order interaction modeling with attention network, the performance of our DeepICF method can be further improved.»
