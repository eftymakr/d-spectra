## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced the naturalness of synthesized speech, enabling high-quality speech generation \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07654)\]. However, the ability to control prosody and correct mispronunciations remains a critical challenge, especially in low-resource settings \[[26](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their flexibility for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Similarly, mispronunciation correction typically depends on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are impractical in scenarios where such resources are unavailable \[[26](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\]. 

Several works have explored the use of prosody modeling in TTS systems. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\] highlights the difficulty of prosody control in neural TTS systems, noting that prosody is implicitly encoded in acoustic features and cannot be easily modified. This limitation has motivated the development of methods that explicitly model and control prosody. \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\] introduces a hierarchical prosody modeling approach for non-autoregressive TTS, enabling fine-grained control over speaking style and word-wise emphasis. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] proposes a hierarchical architecture for non-autoregressive TTS, where phoneme-level prosody is conditioned on word-level features, improving the naturalness of synthesized speech. 

The use of prosody embeddings has also gained traction as a means to control the style of synthesized speech. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\] introduces prosody embeddings for emotional and expressive speech synthesis, enabling fine-grained control over pitch and amplitude at both the frame and phoneme levels. These embeddings are trained without additional supervision, relying only on target speech for synthesis. \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01410)\] further explores the use of global style tokens (GSTs) within Tacotron, allowing for the modeling of expressive speaking styles without explicit labels. These GSTs can be used to control synthesis in novel ways, such as varying speed and speaking style independently of the text content.

In addition to prosody control, the correction of mispronunciations in TTS systems has been addressed through various approaches. \[[26](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\] proposes the use of pre-trained text embeddings, such as BERT, to encode input phrases and improve the naturalness of TTS output by incorporating semantic information. This approach enhances the system's ability to produce accurate pronunciation and prosody. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.07217)\] explores a hierarchical generative model for controllable TTS, enabling the control of latent attributes such as speaking style, accent, and background noise, even when these are not explicitly annotated in the training data.

Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, but precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction remain challenging \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Existing methods often rely on specialized modules or additional training, limiting their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. For example, some approaches condition on acoustic speech features to learn a latent prosody space, enabling control over pitch, duration, and energy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. Non-Attentive Tacotron replaces the attention mechanism with a duration predictor, enabling per-phoneme control of duration and improving robustness \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. Furthermore, models such as CopyCat2 achieve multi-speaker TTS with contextually appropriate prosody through two-stage training, where the first stage learns speaker-independent prosody representations and the second stage predicts them from text \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13443)\]. These methods highlight the growing interest in improving TTS systems with flexible and interpretable prosody control mechanisms. However, they often require additional training or supervision, making post-hoc adjustments difficult \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. The proposed Counterfactual Activation Editing method addresses these limitations by enabling post-hoc manipulation of internal representations in pre-trained TTS models, allowing for inference-time refinement without retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. This approach bridges the gap between pre-trained models and editable speech synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\].

### <a id='category-non-autoregressive-tts-architectures'></a>Non-Autoregressive TTS Architectures: 4 papers found.
Non-autoregressive text-to-speech (NAR-TTS) models such as FastSpeech 2 and Glow-TTS have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional autoregressive models, offering faster inference and parallel generation capabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]. FastSpeech 2 improves upon earlier models by directly using ground-truth targets during training and incorporating more speech variation information, such as pitch, energy, and duration, as conditional inputs, leading to better voice quality and a simplified training pipeline \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Similarly, Glow-TTS introduces a flow-based generative model that eliminates the need for external aligners, enabling robust and efficient speech synthesis with monotonic alignment \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]. These models, along with others like PortaSpeech, which combines lightweight VAEs with flow-based post-nets for high-quality and efficient synthesis \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.15166)\], highlight the growing trend toward parallel and model-agnostic approaches in TTS. These advancements address key limitations of autoregressive models, such as slow inference and reliance on external alignment tools, while maintaining or improving speech quality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.15166)\].

### <a id='category-hierarchical-prosody-modeling-approaches'></a>Hierarchical Prosody Modeling Approaches: 4 papers found.
Recent advances in text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis have emphasized the importance of prosody control for generating natural and expressive speech \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Hierarchical prosody modeling approaches have emerged as a promising direction, enabling more nuanced and interpretable control over speech attributes such as pitch, duration, and energy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. These models often employ a hierarchical architecture where phoneme-level prosody features are conditioned on word-level features, allowing for more natural and controllable speech synthesis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Additionally, some approaches leverage latent variable models, such as conditional variational autoencoders (VAEs), to disentangle and manipulate prosody attributes at multiple levels \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\]. This enables fine-grained control over speaking style, accent, and other attributes that are rarely explicitly annotated in training data \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.07217)\]. By conditioning finer-level representations on coarser-level ones, these models achieve multi-resolution modeling of prosody while maintaining high synthesis quality \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\]. Such methods are particularly valuable for post-hoc adjustments, as they allow for inference-time modifications without requiring retraining \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. These hierarchical approaches address the limitations of traditional methods that rely on specialized modules or grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are less effective in low-resource settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Overall, hierarchical prosody modeling represents a significant step toward more flexible and controllable TTS systems.
