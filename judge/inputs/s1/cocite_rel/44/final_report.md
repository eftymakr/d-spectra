## Related Works

Vectorized embeddings play a crucial role in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient user-item matching by capturing latent relationships in a compact form \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. However, the computational and memory costs of maintaining dense embeddings have motivated research into representation binarization, which converts embeddings into compact binary sequences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. This approach is particularly appealing for online inference, as it reduces both memory usage and computational overhead \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Despite these benefits, existing methods often focus on numerical quantization, which can lead to significant information loss and performance degradation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of graph-based methods for recommendation tasks, leveraging the structural relationships between users and items \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04396)\]. For instance, GraphSAGE provides an inductive framework for generating embeddings of unseen nodes by aggregating features from a node's local neighborhood \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\]. Similarly, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings through the bipartite graph structure \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of capturing collaborative signals in the embedding process, which is essential for accurate recommendations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\].

In the context of recommendation systems, collaborative filtering has been extended with deep learning techniques, such as Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF), which uses a multi-layer perceptron to model user-item interactions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]. Additionally, methods like BPR (Bayesian Personalized Ranking) have been developed to optimize personalized ranking directly, rather than relying on indirect optimization criteria \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]. These models have demonstrated significant improvements over traditional matrix factorization approaches, especially when dealing with implicit feedback \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.00363)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency of recommendation models by leveraging knowledge graphs and attention mechanisms. For example, Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) explicitly models high-order relations in knowledge graphs to enhance recommendation accuracy \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781)\]. Similarly, models like DeepWalk and Variational Autoencoders have been applied to learn latent representations of nodes in large graphs, capturing both structural and semantic information \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07428)\]. These methods have shown promising results in various recommendation scenarios, including cold start and sparse data settings \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05027)\].

In addition to improving model performance, there has been growing interest in model compression and knowledge distillation techniques to enhance deployment efficiency. For instance, ranking distillation (RD) trains a smaller student model to mimic the behavior of a larger teacher model, achieving similar performance with reduced computational cost \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to recommendation systems, demonstrating that compact models can maintain high ranking accuracy \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1510.01784)\].

### <a id='category-deep-learning-for-recommendations'></a>Deep Learning for Recommendations: 6 papers found.
The category "Deep Learning for Recommendations" encompasses a range of techniques that leverage neural networks to enhance collaborative filtering and user-item interaction modeling. Traditional methods often rely on matrix factorization to capture user-item interactions, but recent advances have introduced deep learning models that can learn more complex and non-linear relationships \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. For instance, the Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) framework replaces the inner product used in matrix factorization with a multi-layer perceptron, enabling the model to capture intricate patterns in user-item interactions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. Similarly, the Collaborative Deep Learning (CDL) model integrates deep representation learning with collaborative filtering, allowing for joint modeling of content information and rating matrices \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]. Additionally, models like the Neural Factorization Machine (NFM) combine the strengths of factorization machines and deep neural networks, effectively capturing both second-order and higher-order feature interactions \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05027)\]. These approaches address the limitations of traditional methods, particularly in handling sparse data and capturing non-linear patterns. Furthermore, the integration of visual signals into recommendation systems, such as using pre-trained deep networks to extract visual features from product images, has shown significant improvements in personalized ranking and cold start mitigation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1510.01784)\]. Overall, these works highlight the growing importance of deep learning in recommendation systems, offering more expressive and effective models for user-item matching \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05027)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1510.01784)\].

We propose a KD technique for learning to rank problems, called ranking distillation (RD).
