## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency compared to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach involves predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads, which can enhance the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies and improve generative capabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in effectively leveraging the MTP objective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. To address this, we propose a curriculum learning strategy for MTP training, which includes two variants: a forward curriculum that gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, and a reverse curriculum that does the opposite \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The forward curriculum enables SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training, leading to improved downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach aligns with findings that gradual training strategies can help models adapt to more complex tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. Additionally, speculative decoding methods, such as those used in Medusa and SpecInfer, have been shown to accelerate inference by allowing parallel prediction of multiple tokens \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09781)\]. These methods are particularly beneficial when combined with MTP, as they enable faster generation without compromising model accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\].

In contrast, the reverse curriculum achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This suggests that the reverse curriculum may not be as effective in improving inference efficiency, which is a critical factor in practical applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The effectiveness of curriculum learning in MTP training is further supported by studies on transfer learning, which highlight the importance of pre-training objectives in shaping downstream performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. For instance, transfer learning techniques that convert language problems into text-to-text formats have shown significant improvements in various NLP tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\].

Furthermore, the concept of multi-token prediction has been explored in the context of sequence-to-sequence pre-training, where models are trained to predict future n-grams based on previous context tokens \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\]. This approach encourages the model to plan for future tokens and prevents overfitting on strong local correlations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\]. The success of such methods has been demonstrated in tasks such as summarization and question generation, where models trained with multi-token prediction outperform traditional NTP-based models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\].

In summary, the related works highlight the potential of MTP in improving language model performance and efficiency

### <a id='category-multi-token-prediction-(mtp)-methodology'></a>Multi-token Prediction (MTP) Methodology: 5 papers found.
Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) in training large language models, offering benefits in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike NTP, which predicts one token at a time, MTP trains models to predict multiple future tokens simultaneously using independent output heads on a shared model trunk \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has demonstrated significant gains, particularly for large models, with 13B parameter models solving 12% more problems on HumanEval and 17% more on MBPP compared to comparable next-token models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Additionally, MTP enables faster inference, with models trained using 4-token prediction achieving up to 3 times speed improvements even with large batch sizes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, challenges remain, especially for smaller language models (SLMs), which struggle with the MTP objective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Prior work has identified issues with teacher-forcing, where models may fail to learn accurate next-token predictors, and has proposed teacherless training as a potential solution \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. Furthermore, integrating MTP heads into frozen LLMs is non-trivial due to the strong specialization of hidden layers for NTP \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)\]. While joint training of MTP heads with the backbone improves performance, it cannot fully overcome these barriers, highlighting the need for further research \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)\]. Efforts to address these limitations include curriculum learning strategies, such as forward and reverse curricula, which aim to gradually adjust the complexity of the pre-training objective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. These strategies have shown promise in enabling SLMs to better leverage MTP during pre-training while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

### <a id='category-self-speculative-decoding-techniques'></a>Self-speculative Decoding Techniques: 3 papers found.
Speculative decoding techniques have emerged as a promising approach to accelerate the inference process of large language models (LLMs) by enabling parallel token generation and verification. These methods leverage smaller, more efficient models to draft potential token sequences, which are then validated by the larger, more accurate LLM in parallel, reducing the overall computational burden \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09781)\]. This approach contrasts with traditional autoregressive decoding, which generates one token at a time, leading to high latency \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07851)\]. By utilizing speculative execution, these techniques can generate multiple tokens per step, significantly improving inference speed without compromising output quality \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. The key idea involves organizing candidate token sequences into a tree structure, where each node represents a potential sequence, and the LLM verifies the correctness of these sequences in parallel \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09781)\]. This method has been shown to reduce end-to-end latency and computational requirements while maintaining model performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09781)\]. Additionally, the use of drafter models allows for the efficient exploration of multiple token possibilities, enhancing the scalability of LLM inference \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07851)\]. These techniques have been demonstrated to achieve up to 3X speed improvements compared to standard implementations, making them a valuable addition to the toolkit for efficient LLM deployment \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\].
