## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency compared to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach involves predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads, which can enhance the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies and improve generative capabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in effectively leveraging the MTP objective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. To address this, we propose a curriculum learning strategy for MTP training, which includes two variants: a forward curriculum that gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, and a reverse curriculum that does the opposite \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The forward curriculum enables SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training, leading to improved downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach aligns with findings that gradual training strategies can help models adapt to more complex tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. Additionally, speculative decoding methods, such as those used in Medusa and SpecInfer, have been shown to accelerate inference by allowing parallel prediction of multiple tokens \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09781)\]. These methods are particularly beneficial when combined with MTP, as they enable faster generation without compromising model accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\].

In contrast, the reverse curriculum achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This suggests that the reverse curriculum may not be as effective in improving inference efficiency, which is a critical factor in practical applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The effectiveness of curriculum learning in MTP training is further supported by studies on transfer learning, which highlight the importance of pre-training objectives in shaping downstream performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. For instance, transfer learning techniques that convert language problems into text-to-text formats have shown significant improvements in various NLP tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\].

Furthermore, the concept of multi-token prediction has been explored in the context of sequence-to-sequence pre-training, where models are trained to predict future n-grams based on previous context tokens \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\]. This approach encourages the model to plan for future tokens and prevents overfitting on strong local correlations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\]. The success of such methods has been demonstrated in tasks such as summarization and question generation, where models trained with multi-token prediction outperform traditional NTP-based models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\].

In summary, the related works highlight the potential of MTP in improving language model performance and efficiency
