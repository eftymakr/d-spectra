## Related Works

Personalized outfit generation has been a focal point in recent research, with a growing emphasis on generating compatible and personalized fashion items. Early approaches primarily focused on outfit compatibility, often using rule-based systems or simple similarity metrics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03133)\]. However, these methods lacked the flexibility and adaptability required to capture the nuanced preferences of individual users. More recent works have leveraged deep learning to model the complex relationships between fashion items and user preferences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03016)\].

Several studies have explored the use of neural networks for outfit generation and compatibility prediction. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\] introduced a bidirectional LSTM model to learn compatibility relationships between fashion items by treating an outfit as a sequence. This approach enabled the model to predict the next item in a sequence, thereby improving compatibility. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09196)\] proposed a Siamese network-based approach to learn compatibility metrics by comparing pairs of items, which was later extended to handle cross-category compatibility \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02231)\].

The importance of user preferences in outfit generation has also been emphasized. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02662)\] introduced a system that scores fashion outfit candidates based on appearance and metadata, leveraging popularity on fashion websites to supervise the scoring component. This work laid the foundation for more sophisticated personalization techniques. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05691)\] further advanced this idea by proposing a Personalized Outfit Generation (POG) model that connects user preferences with a Transformer architecture, achieving significant improvements in both compatibility and personalization metrics.

Another key contribution is the development of models that can generate new fashion items based on user preferences. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.00313)\] introduced an Attribute-GAN model that generates clothing-match pairs by incorporating semantic attributes, while \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.07473)\] proposed a framework that uses a pyramid style extractor to generate realistic outfit images. These models demonstrated the potential of generative adversarial networks (GANs) in creating new fashion items that align with user preferences.

The challenge of generating outfits from incomplete information has also been addressed. \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b5f488dd55024f0db2cabb73b055002b5d995be9)\] proposed a model that fills in missing items in an outfit by learning compatibility relationships from a large-scale dataset. This work was later extended to include both visual and textual modalities \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.04757)\]. Additionally, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00741)\] introduced a multilayer neural network that learns embeddings of items in a latent style space, enabling the generation of compatible outfits.

Recent advancements have also focused on the integration of user feedback and multi-expert evaluations to improve the personalization of outfit generation. \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/762ec45448e915142f355848265d10dd55ab470f)\] introduced a framework that uses a two-level attention mechanism to capture user preferences, while \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04014)\] proposed a system that generates new items based on user queries and style preferences

Recent advancements in generative models have significantly impacted fashion recommendation and outfit generation, with a growing focus on integrating personalization and compatibility into the design process. Researchers have explored the use of generative adversarial networks (GANs) to synthesize new fashion items that align with user preferences and maintain visual coherence \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ca3ad34ff2652cd922c3476514a758674bc7e76b)\]. For instance, one study proposes a personalized fashion design framework that jointly considers compatibility and personalization by using a generator network guided by two discriminators—one for real/fake distinction and another for modeling item compatibility and user preferences \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ca3ad34ff2652cd922c3476514a758674bc7e76b)\]. Similarly, another work introduces an Attribute-GAN that generates clothing-match pairs by training a generator with adversarial supervision from both a collocation and attribute discriminator, leveraging a manually annotated large-scale outfit dataset \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/33eea4fc5622f05ab30c46c9bcf476d8d2fe811c)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of semantic attributes and compatibility rules in generating realistic and stylistically consistent outfits. Additionally, there is a trend toward joint learning of image representations and recommendation systems, where models are trained from the pixel level to capture fashion-aware features, leading to improved performance over traditional methods like BPR \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02231)\]. Furthermore, frameworks such as COutfitGAN have been developed to generate photo-realistic, complementary fashion items by incorporating a pyramid style extractor, a UNet-based discriminator, and a collocation discriminator, all trained on a large-scale dataset of over 200K outfits \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08674)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the potential of GANs in addressing the challenges of personalization, compatibility, and diversity in fashion generation, setting the stage for more sophisticated and user-centric systems.

[Snippet]: «Making language models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent. For example, large language models can generate outputs that are untruthful, toxic, or simply not helpful to the user. In other words, these models are not aligned with their users. In this paper, we show an avenue for aligning language models with user intent on a wide range of tasks by fine-tuning with human feedback. Starting with a set of labeler-written prompts and prompts submitted through the OpenAI API, we collect a dataset of labeler demonstrations of the desired model behavior, which we use to fine-tune GPT-3 using supervised learning. We then collect a dataset of rankings of model outputs, which we use to further fine-tune this supervised model using reinforcement learning from human feedback. We call the resulting models InstructGPT. In human evaluations on our prompt distribution, outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters. Moreover, InstructGPT models show improvements in truthfulness and reductions in toxic output generation while having minimal performance regressions on public NLP datasets. Even though InstructGPT still makes simple mistakes, our results show that fine-tuning with human feedback is a promising direction for aligning language models with human intent.»
