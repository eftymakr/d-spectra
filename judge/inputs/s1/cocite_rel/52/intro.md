## Related Works

Dense retrievers have become a cornerstone in information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) to encode text into dense vector representations that can be compared via simple similarity operations such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. These models typically employ contrastive learning during fine-tuning to improve retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. However, recent research has questioned the relative contributions of pre-training versus fine-tuning in acquiring retrieval knowledge, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily gained during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may only adjust neuron activations rather than reorganize knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This claim was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative dense retriever \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different pre-training and fine-tuning strategies for dense retrieval. For instance, BERT has been widely used for dense retrieval tasks, and its performance has been further improved through various optimizations such as RoBERTa \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\], which re-examines hyperparameter choices and training data size to achieve better results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Additionally, the Condenser architecture was introduced to pre-train dense encoders by learning to condense information into dense vectors \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. This approach has shown significant improvements over standard language models in text retrieval and similarity tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\].

The role of fine-tuning in dense retrievers has also been investigated. For example, the study by \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\] highlights that fine-tuning can significantly enhance retrieval performance, particularly when combined with effective negative sampling strategies. Similarly, \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\] discusses the importance of negative examples in training dense retrievers, noting that the quality of negatives directly impacts retrieval accuracy. The paper also introduces the ANCE method, which constructs negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index to improve retrieval performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)\].

Another important consideration is the choice of representation methods. For example, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15466)\] compares the use of CLS tokens with mean pooling in dense retrievers, finding that mean pooling can lead to better performance in certain scenarios. Additionally, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\] introduces the DeCLUTR model, which uses contrastive learning to generate high-quality sentence embeddings without requiring labeled data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\].

The study by \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00300)\] extends the analysis of pre-training and fine-tuning in dense retrievers by testing different representation approaches, backbone architectures, and datasets.
