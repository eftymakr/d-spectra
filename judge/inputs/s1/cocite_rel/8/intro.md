## Related Works

The growing use of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in how they handle model uncertainty and its implications for biased decision-making \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations and fail to capture the implicit impact of model confidence across different groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more nuanced fairness measures that account for uncertainty in predictions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. 

Several studies have explored fairness in machine learning, emphasizing the importance of addressing bias in classification tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. For instance, the concept of fairness through awareness introduces a framework where similar individuals are treated similarly, ensuring that the classifier maintains utility while adhering to fairness constraints \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This aligns with the broader goal of developing fairness-aware learning algorithms that consider demographic factors \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9dae5041ed97ab21f637fb282d758fd9cb771476)\]. However, these approaches often overlook the role of model confidence in decision-making, which is critical for evaluating fairness in LLMs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

The challenge of defining and measuring fairness has been extensively discussed in the literature, with varying definitions and measures proposed \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. Some studies have highlighted the tension between minimizing error disparity across groups and maintaining calibrated probability estimates \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. This tension is particularly relevant when evaluating LLMs, as fairness metrics must account for both accuracy and confidence in predictions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. 

Recent work has also emphasized the importance of diverse and representative datasets for evaluating fairness in machine learning systems \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725)\]. For example, the MEDFAIR framework introduces a benchmark for medical imaging, highlighting the need for datasets that reflect real-world diversity and ethical considerations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725)\]. Similarly, the introduction of a new gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset with 31,756 samples addresses the limitations of existing datasets in terms of size, diversity, and clarity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. This dataset is particularly useful for evaluating modern LLMs, as it enables more fine-grained analysis of fairness and uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

The legal and ethical implications of algorithmic bias have also been explored, with studies drawing on political philosophy to formalize definitions of fairness and discrimination \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. These philosophical perspectives provide a foundation for operationalizing fairness in machine learning, ensuring that technical interventions align with broader social and legal principles \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. 

In addition, the concept of counterfactual fairness has been introduced to address the issue of biased historical data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This framework ensures that decisions are fair by considering how they would change in a counterfactual scenario where the individual belonged to a different demographic group \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\].
