## Related Works

Event cameras, also known as dynamic vision sensors (DVS), have gained significant attention in recent years due to their ability to capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis, enabling extremely high temporal resolution \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/47cd947b70d5c6d90a5ecf0a76a6199fd2541175)\]. This capability makes them particularly suitable for applications such as autonomous driving, robotics, and high-speed vision tasks where traditional frame-based cameras struggle with motion blur and dynamic lighting conditions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06310)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces challenges in processing and interpretation, as it requires specialized algorithms tailored to the unique characteristics of event streams \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\].

Prior work has explored methods to reduce the computational and bandwidth demands of event cameras, primarily through event downsampling techniques. Most existing approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit their adaptability to different applications and environments \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/47cd947b70d5c6d90a5ecf0a76a6199fd2541175)\]. For instance, some methods use simple intensity thresholds to filter out less significant events, while others apply spatial or temporal filters \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. These approaches, however, often fail to account for the structural importance of certain events, leading to performance degradation in tasks such as object classification and detection \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/47cd947b70d5c6d90a5ecf0a76a6199fd2541175)\].

To address these limitations, recent research has proposed more sophisticated methods for event processing. For example, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\] introduces a framework for unsupervised learning of optical flow, depth, and egomotion from event streams, leveraging a discretized volume representation of events to predict motion information. This work demonstrates that direct learning from raw events can yield competitive performance compared to frame-based methods, even in challenging scenarios \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. Similarly, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07165)\] proposes a recurrent network to reconstruct intensity images from event streams, showing that learned representations can significantly improve the quality of reconstructed images and enable the use of off-the-shelf computer vision algorithms \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07165)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving the efficiency and adaptability of event-based systems. \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07640)\] introduces EDS, a direct monocular visual odometry method that combines events and frames to estimate camera motion. This approach leverages the event generation model to track motion between frames, enabling robust performance in low-light and high-speed scenarios \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.07640)\]. Additionally, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10180)\] proposes the Event-based Double Integral (EDI) model to reconstruct high frame-rate, sharp videos from a single blurry frame and its event data, demonstrating the potential of event cameras in high-speed video synthesis \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10180)\].
