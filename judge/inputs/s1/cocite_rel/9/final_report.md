## Related Works

The detection and segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions, particularly paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), have been extensively studied in recent years. These lesions, characterized by a paramagnetic rim due to iron deposition, are considered a key biomarker for disease progression and severity \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. The identification of PRLs is challenging due to their rarity and the class imbalance between PRLs and non-rim lesions \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Several studies have explored automated methods for lesion detection and segmentation using deep learning and traditional machine learning techniques \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2dfca37050afe466e81fbb8cc8ae9da3e368fb0c)\]\[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]\[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/11d9a8fa4b2e87bde4b9a3ff5d103dcca43767b8)\].

Deep learning approaches have shown promise in improving the accuracy of lesion detection, especially when dealing with imbalanced data. For instance, QSMRim-Net is a deep neural network that fuses lesion-level radiomic and convolutional image features for automated identification of rim+ lesions on quantitative susceptibility maps (QSM) \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. This method employs a two-branch feature extraction network and a synthetic minority oversampling technique to enhance classification performance \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. Similarly, the study by Zhang et al. presents a fully automated technique for detecting PRLs using T2*-phase images and radiomic features, achieving a high area under the curve (AUC) of 0.80 \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\].

Another approach, the use of synthetic data generation, has been proposed to address the class imbalance issue. In the context of MS lesion segmentation, synthetic lesion generation using generative adversarial networks (GANs) has been explored to improve classifier performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. This technique involves generating synthetic lesions to augment the training dataset, thereby improving the model's ability to detect rare classes \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. The proposed method in this paper extends this idea by generating synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps (QSMs) of PRLs and demonstrating that the inclusion of such synthetic data significantly improves classifier performance \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\].

Additionally, the use of domain adaptation techniques has been investigated to improve the generalizability of lesion segmentation models across different MRI scanners and protocols \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. One-shot domain adaptation methods have shown promising results, where models trained on a single case can achieve performance comparable to fully trained models \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. This is particularly relevant for clinical settings where annotated data is limited \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\].

Furthermore, the integration of anatomical information into the segmentation process has been explored to improve the detection of small lesions. ### <a id='category-deep-learning-for-quantitative-susceptibility-maps'></a>Deep Learning for Quantitative Susceptibility Maps: 3 papers found.
The category "Deep Learning for Quantitative Susceptibility Maps" focuses on the application of deep learning techniques to analyze quantitative susceptibility maps (QSM) in the context of multiple sclerosis (MS). This includes the development of deep neural networks for automated identification of specific lesion types, such as rim+ lesions, which are associated with chronic active MS lesions and increased disability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. These lesions are characterized by a paramagnetic rim, detectable via QSM, and are linked to iron deposition in chronically activated microglia \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2dfca37050afe466e81fbb8cc8ae9da3e368fb0c)\]. The challenge of class imbalance between rim+ and rim-negative lesions is addressed through methods like synthetic minority oversampling, where synthetic QSM data is generated to improve classifier performance \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. Additionally, the physical principles underlying QSM, including magnetic susceptibility and its relation to tissue composition, are explored, highlighting the importance of accurate QSM in studying pathophysiological changes such as iron distribution and demyelination \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/455e181fe505a36406af032700fea2ca87a2e9b9)\]. The integration of deep learning with QSM enables more precise detection and segmentation of lesions, supporting longitudinal monitoring and improving diagnostic accuracy.

### <a id='category-lesion-detection-techniques-in-ms'></a>Lesion Detection Techniques in MS: 18 papers found.
The detection and segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions, particularly paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), have been the focus of numerous studies due to their clinical significance in prognosis and disease monitoring \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2dfca37050afe466e81fbb8cc8ae9da3e368fb0c)\]. Automated methods, such as deep learning-based approaches, have been proposed to address the challenges of manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to variability \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e9df6d7fa6b380c5db0ad4f29b9c32219aca05fe)\]. For instance, a supervised approach using features from image intensities, subtraction values, and deformation fields has shown promising results in detecting new T2-weighted lesions with a mean Dice similarity coefficient of 0.77 \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a6c3b459dcaed58347ae0d270c0cfd93ee8aed6f)\]. Similarly, a cascaded convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture has been developed to improve the accuracy of white matter (WM) lesion segmentation by reducing misclassified voxels \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\]. Another method, MSmetrix, utilizes a probabilistic model based on 3D T1-weighted and FLAIR MR images to detect WM lesions without requiring training data, achieving a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.67 with expert reference segmentations \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/11d9a8fa4b2e87bde4b9a3ff5d103dcca43767b8)\]. The challenge of class imbalance, particularly in rare PRLs, has also been addressed through synthetic data generation and denoising techniques, which enhance classifier performance by increasing the minority class representation \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2dfca37050afe466e81fbb8cc8ae9da3e368fb0c)\]. Additionally, the use of fully convolutional deep neural networks with asymmetric similarity loss functions has demonstrated superior performance in MS lesion segmentation, achieving average Dice similarity coefficients of 69.9% and 65.74% on benchmark datasets \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.11078)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the accuracy and reliability of automated lesion detection and segmentation in MS, which are critical for clinical applications and longitudinal studies \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.06780)\].

[Snippet]: «In recent years, several convolutional neural network (CNN) methods have been proposed for the automated white matter lesion segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) patient images, due to their superior performance compared with those of other state-of-the-art methods. However, the accuracies of CNN methods tend to decrease significantly when evaluated on different image domains compared with those used for training, which demonstrates the lack of adaptability of CNNs to unseen imaging data. In this study, we analyzed the effect of intensity domain adaptation on our recently proposed CNN-based MS lesion segmentation method. Given a source model trained on two public MS datasets, we investigated the transferability of the CNN model when applied to other MRI scanners and protocols, evaluating the minimum number of annotated images needed from the new domain and the minimum number of layers needed to re-train to obtain comparable accuracy. Our analysis comprised MS patient data from both a clinical center and the public ISBI2015 challenge database, which permitted us to compare the domain adaptation capability of our model to that of other state-of-the-art methods. In both datasets, our results showed the effectiveness of the proposed model in adapting previously acquired knowledge to new image domains, even when a reduced number of training samples was available in the target dataset. For the ISBI2015 challenge, our one-shot domain adaptation model trained using only a single case showed a performance similar to that of other CNN methods that were fully trained using the entire available training set, yielding a comparable human expert rater performance. We believe that our experiments will encourage the MS community to incorporate its use in different clinical settings with reduced amounts of annotated data. This approach could be meaningful not only in terms of the accuracy in delineating MS lesions but also in the related reductions in time and economic costs derived from manual lesion labeling.»

ABSTRACT We propose a dual pathway, 11‐layers deep, three‐dimensional Convolutional Neural Network for the challenging task of brain lesion segmentation. The devised architecture is the result of an in‐depth analysis of the limitations of current networks proposed for similar applications. To overcome the computational burden of processing 3D medical scans, we have devised an efficient and effective dense training scheme which joins the processing of adjacent image patches into one pass through the network while automatically adapting to the inherent class imbalance present in the data. Further, we analyze the development of deeper, thus more discriminative 3D CNNs. In order to incorporate both local and larger contextual information, we employ a dual pathway architecture that processes the input images at multiple scales simultaneously. For post‐processing of the network's soft segmentation, we use a 3D fully connected Conditional Random Field which effectively removes false positives. Our pipeline is extensively evaluated on three challenging tasks of lesion segmentation in multi‐channel MRI patient data with traumatic brain injuries, brain tumours, and ischemic stroke. We improve on the state‐of‐the‐art for all three applications, with top ranking performance on the public benchmarks BRATS 2015 and ISLES 2015.
