## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural network models that directly generate document identifiers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach leverages large language models to encode and retrieve information without the need for explicit index structures \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Earlier works, such as the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), demonstrated that a single Transformer model can encode the entire corpus and answer queries by generating relevant document IDs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. However, these models often face challenges related to data distribution mismatch between indexing and retrieval phases \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

Several studies have explored ways to improve the effectiveness of generative retrieval. For instance, SEAL proposed using all ngrams in a passage as possible identifiers, allowing autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams, which are then mapped to full passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This method outperformed prior autoregressive approaches and achieved significant improvements over established retrieval solutions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, GLEN introduced a novel method for generative retrieval via lexical index learning, addressing the discrepancy between pre-trained language models and identifiers, as well as the gap between training and inference \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03057)\].

The problem of defining document identifiers has also been addressed by GenRet, which learns to tokenize documents into short discrete representations (docids) using a discrete auto-encoding approach \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. This method enables fully end-to-end modeling of document retrieval tasks and achieves state-of-the-art performance on several benchmark datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. Furthermore, TOME proposed a two-stage approach for model-based retrieval, utilizing tokenized URLs as identifiers and designing a two-stage generation architecture to improve performance and scalability \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\].

Scaling generative retrieval to large corpora remains a significant challenge. Studies such as \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\] and \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\] have examined the performance of generative retrieval on datasets with millions of passages, highlighting the importance of synthetic queries during indexing and the limitations of naively scaling model parameters. ### <a id='category-document-identifier-learning-methods'></a>Document Identifier Learning Methods: 6 papers found.
Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm that replaces traditional search index structures with a single large-scale neural network, enabling end-to-end document retrieval by directly generating document identifiers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach leverages autoregressive models to produce identifiers based on corpus-specific constraints, allowing for seamless integration with generative large language models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\]. However, existing methods face challenges in defining meaningful document identifiers and maintaining performance across dynamic and out-of-distribution corpora \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Several studies have explored alternative strategies for generating identifiers, such as using ngrams from passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\], dynamic lexical identifiers \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03057)\], and discrete auto-encoding representations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These methods aim to capture semantic information more effectively than fixed rule-based identifiers. Additionally, continual learning remains a critical challenge, as document collections are often dynamic, requiring models to incrementally index new documents while preserving retrieval accuracy \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Recent work has introduced techniques like Incremental Product Quantization and memory-augmented learning to address these issues \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Furthermore, the generalization capabilities of generative retrieval have been extensively studied, with models like Differentiable Search Index (DSI) demonstrating strong performance across varying corpus sizes and query types \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. These advancements highlight the evolving landscape of generative retrieval, emphasizing the need for robust theoretical understanding and practical improvements to enhance generalization and efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\].

### <a id='category-large-scale-retrieval-challenges'></a>Large-Scale Retrieval Challenges: 4 papers found.
The category "Large-Scale Retrieval Challenges" highlights the difficulties of scaling generative retrieval techniques to large document corpora. While generative retrieval models, such as those based on differentiable search indexes, offer promising alternatives to traditional search systems by encoding entire document collections within a single Transformer \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\], their effectiveness is limited when applied to large-scale datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. Existing approaches often struggle with compute efficiency and performance degradation as corpus size increases, with some studies showing that naive scaling of model parameters does not necessarily improve retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Recent work has attempted to address these challenges by introducing novel optimization frameworks, such as RIPOR, which incorporates prefix-oriented ranking optimization and relevance-based document ID construction to enhance retrieval accuracy on benchmarks like MS MARCO \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. Additionally, other research has focused on improving training mechanisms for dense retrieval models, such as ANCE, which leverages approximate nearest neighbor indices to generate more representative negative training samples, thereby improving generalization \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These efforts underscore the ongoing challenges in developing robust, scalable generative retrieval systems that can effectively handle large-scale, real-world document collections.

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-paradigm-overview'></a>Generative Retrieval Paradigm Overview: 2 papers found.
Generative retrieval represents a paradigm shift in text retrieval, moving away from traditional sparse or dense retrieval methods by leveraging powerful generative language models to directly generate identifiers of relevant passages \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. This approach enables seamless integration with large language models and offers potential improvements in efficiency and adaptability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. However, the learning objective of autoregressive models often diverges from the goal of passage ranking, leading to a learning gap \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. To address this, LTRGR introduces a learning-to-rank framework that optimizes the autoregressive model toward the final passage ranking target through a rank loss, enhancing current generative retrieval systems without increasing inference complexity \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. Additionally, generative retrieval has been applied to entity retrieval tasks, where systems like GENRE generate entity names token-by-token, conditioned on context, thereby improving accuracy and reducing memory footprint compared to dense representation methods \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00904)\]. These advancements highlight the evolving landscape of generative retrieval, emphasizing both theoretical challenges and practical improvements in the field.
