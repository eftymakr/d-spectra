## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural network models that directly generate document identifiers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach leverages large language models to encode and retrieve information without the need for explicit index structures \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Earlier works, such as the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), demonstrated that a single Transformer model can encode the entire corpus and answer queries by generating relevant document IDs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. However, these models often face challenges related to data distribution mismatch between indexing and retrieval phases \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

Several studies have explored ways to improve the effectiveness of generative retrieval. For instance, SEAL proposed using all ngrams in a passage as possible identifiers, allowing autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams, which are then mapped to full passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This method outperformed prior autoregressive approaches and achieved significant improvements over established retrieval solutions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, GLEN introduced a novel method for generative retrieval via lexical index learning, addressing the discrepancy between pre-trained language models and identifiers, as well as the gap between training and inference \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03057)\].

The problem of defining document identifiers has also been addressed by GenRet, which learns to tokenize documents into short discrete representations (docids) using a discrete auto-encoding approach \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. This method enables fully end-to-end modeling of document retrieval tasks and achieves state-of-the-art performance on several benchmark datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. Furthermore, TOME proposed a two-stage approach for model-based retrieval, utilizing tokenized URLs as identifiers and designing a two-stage generation architecture to improve performance and scalability \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\].

Scaling generative retrieval to large corpora remains a significant challenge. Studies such as \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\] and \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\] have examined the performance of generative retrieval on datasets with millions of passages, highlighting the importance of synthetic queries during indexing and the limitations of naively scaling model parameters. The work on RIPOR introduced an optimization framework for generative retrieval, focusing on prefix-oriented ranking optimization and constructing document IDs based on relevance associations between queries and documents \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. This approach significantly improved retrieval performance on large-scale benchmarks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\].

Additionally, the use of query generation during indexing has been explored to mitigate data distribution mismatches. DSI-QG, for example, represents documents with a number of potentially relevant queries generated by a query generation model, allowing the DSI model to connect document identifiers to a set of queries \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].
