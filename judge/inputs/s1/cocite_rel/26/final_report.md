## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied and implemented in various contexts, particularly in distributed systems where data is collected from multiple users. Early work on DP focused on the central model, where a trusted server collects and processes data while ensuring privacy \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4ce10063cd25447dcde75c2d9ce327446ced952)\]. However, the local model, where each user perturbs their data before sending it to the server, has gained popularity due to its inherent privacy guarantees without requiring trust in the data collector \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Despite these advantages, local DP protocols are vulnerable to manipulation attacks, where malicious users can alter their outputs to skew the aggregated results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. 

Several studies have explored the vulnerabilities of local DP protocols. For instance, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\] highlights that attackers can inject fake users and send crafted data to manipulate frequency estimates or identify heavy hitters. This type of attack, known as data poisoning, has prompted the development of countermeasures, though their effectiveness varies \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Similarly, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] shows that LDP is susceptible to output-manipulation attacks, where attackers can control the estimations by exploiting the normalization process in the LDP protocol. To mitigate these issues, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] proposes a verifiable randomization mechanism that allows the data collector to verify the correctness of the randomization process, thereby preventing malicious data providers from compromising the protocol.

The shuffled model of differential privacy has also emerged as a promising approach that combines the benefits of both local and central models. By introducing an anonymous channel that permutes user messages, the shuffled model achieves higher accuracy than the local model while avoiding the need for a trusted server \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model has been further analyzed to establish its theoretical limits and practical feasibility \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. Additionally, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.11839)\] provides a comprehensive survey of shuffle protocols and their separation from the local and central models, highlighting the potential for improved accuracy and privacy.

To address the limitations of traditional DP mechanisms, several works have introduced novel techniques. For example, \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.06387)\] proposes the distributed discrete Gaussian mechanism for federated learning, which allows for accurate model training while preserving privacy through secure aggregation. This approach discretizes data and adds noise before aggregation, balancing communication, privacy, and accuracy. Similarly, \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\] introduces interactive proofs for DP, enabling verifiable DP counting queries that ensure the correctness of the results without revealing the underlying randomness. This concept is closely related to the idea of verifiable distributed differential privacy (VDDP) proposed in this work, which incorporates verification mechanisms to ensure the faithful execution of DP protocols \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\].

### <a id='category-data-poisoning-and-manipulation-attacks'></a>Data Poisoning and Manipulation Attacks: 3 papers found.
Local differential privacy (LDP) protocols, which enable privacy-preserving data analytics by allowing users to perturb their data before sending it to an untrusted collector, have been widely studied and deployed in practice \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. However, recent research highlights significant vulnerabilities in these protocols, particularly regarding adversarial manipulation and data poisoning attacks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Attackers can exploit the inherent randomness in LDP mechanisms to inject fake data or manipulate outputs, leading to skewed statistical results \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. For example, malicious data providers can exploit the plausible deniability provided by randomization to alter the estimated background data distribution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This manipulation is further exacerbated when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large, as a small fraction of users can obscure the true distribution of honest users' inputs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, attackers can craft data to influence frequency estimates or identify heavy hitters, undermining the integrity of the analytics \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. While some countermeasures have been proposed, such as verifiable randomization mechanisms and robust protocols, they often have limited effectiveness or introduce significant overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. These findings underscore the need for more secure and robust LDP protocols that can withstand adversarial manipulation while maintaining utility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\].

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-models-(central-vs.-local)'></a>Differential Privacy Models (Central vs. Local): 5 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy Models (Central vs. Local)" explores the distinctions and implications of privacy guarantees in different models of differential privacy. In the local differential privacy (LDP) model, users add noise to their data before sending it to a server, ensuring that the data remains private even if the server is untrusted \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. This model is particularly relevant for scenarios where repeated data collection occurs, such as monitoring user preferences or software usage over time \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. However, LDP protocols often introduce high noise and overhead due to their stringent privacy guarantees \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. In contrast, the central differential privacy (CDP) model assumes a trusted server that aggregates data and adds noise at the end, which can lead to better accuracy and lower noise \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Recent research has shown that certain LDP algorithms can achieve stronger privacy guarantees in the CDP model, especially when reports are anonymized, suggesting that the privacy cost of LDP may be lower than previously thought \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. This has important implications for real-world deployments, such as Microsoft's use of LDP mechanisms for telemetry collection across millions of devices \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. Additionally, the challenges of repeated data collection under LDP have led to the development of new mechanisms that maintain privacy guarantees over long periods while improving accuracy for tasks like mean estimation and histogram estimation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of differential privacy models and their practical applications in preserving user privacy while enabling useful data analysis.

### <a id='category-efficiency-improvements-in-privacy-mechanisms'></a>Efficiency Improvements in Privacy Mechanisms: 5 papers found.
Efficiency improvements in privacy mechanisms have been a focal point of recent research, with a particular emphasis on reducing computational overhead while maintaining strong privacy guarantees. Several studies have addressed the limitations of traditional methods, such as the use of continuous Gaussian noise, which can introduce interpretability issues when applied to discrete data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. To mitigate these challenges, researchers have proposed the use of discrete Gaussian noise, which offers comparable privacy and accuracy guarantees while being more suitable for discrete data types \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\]. Additionally, the introduction of truncated concentrated differential privacy (tCDP) has enabled more efficient composition and better statistical analysis, supporting advanced techniques like privacy amplification via sub-sampling \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2f0ab8040034e128175d424616b504d22cac485c)\]. In the context of local differential privacy (LDP), frequency oracle (FO) protocols have been enhanced through post-processing steps that leverage non-negativity constraints, leading to improved accuracy for various query types \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. Furthermore, the Gaussian mechanism has been re-evaluated, revealing that its variance formula is not optimal in certain regimes, prompting the development of an optimal Gaussian mechanism with tighter variance calibration and post-processing steps for improved accuracy \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.06530)\]. These advancements collectively highlight a trend toward more efficient and accurate privacy-preserving mechanisms that balance practical implementation with rigorous theoretical foundations.

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-of-differential-privacy'></a>Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy: 3 papers found.
The shuffled model of differential privacy offers a middle ground between the local and central models, combining the privacy guarantees of the local model with the accuracy benefits of the central model \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.11839)\]. In this model, users randomize their data as in the local model but send their outputs through an anonymous channel that permutes the messages, preventing the server from learning individual contributions \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This approach allows for more accurate computations while maintaining user privacy without requiring full trust in a central server \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.11839)\]. Recent work has demonstrated that shuffled protocols can achieve significantly better accuracy than local protocols for certain tasks, such as histogram estimation, with error independent of the domain size \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. However, the shuffled model is not without limitations; for example, under certain constraints like pure differential privacy and single-message randomizers, it becomes equivalent to the local model \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. Additionally, some studies have shown that for specific problems, such as the selection problem, shuffled protocols may require exponentially higher sample complexity compared to central-model protocols \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. These findings highlight the trade-offs and potential of the shuffled model in achieving both privacy and utility in distributed settings.
