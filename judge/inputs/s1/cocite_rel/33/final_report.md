## Related Works

Catastrophic forgetting remains a central challenge in continual learning (CL), where models must learn sequentially without losing previously acquired knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Early approaches to address this issue relied on experience replay, where past data is stored and retrained alongside new data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. This strategy has been widely adopted due to its simplicity and effectiveness in mitigating forgetting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. However, recent work has questioned whether concurrent rehearsal is always optimal, suggesting that sequential rehearsal—where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data—may offer additional benefits \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. 

Several studies have explored the role of rehearsal in CL. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08654)\] introduced a method that leverages a small episodic memory to enable continual learning, demonstrating that joint training on current and stored examples can outperform state-of-the-art approaches. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] proposed a framework that uses a generative model to replay past experiences, enabling the model to maintain knowledge of previous tasks without storing all data explicitly. This approach is inspired by the brain’s memory replay mechanisms, which are thought to play a key role in preserving memories \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. 

Rehearsal-based methods often rely on the assumption that storing past data is feasible. However, in real-world applications, memory constraints make this approach impractical \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. To address this, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\] introduced a method that uses a tiny episodic memory, showing that even with minimal storage, joint training on current and stored examples can yield strong performance. This aligns with findings from \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7bfb4ef17eabec1acd266958bdb08622eebfbb05)\], which demonstrated that replaying internal representations, rather than raw data, can effectively prevent catastrophic forgetting. 

The effectiveness of rehearsal strategies depends on the similarity between tasks. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10635)\] found that when tasks are less similar, sequential rehearsal outperforms concurrent rehearsal, as it allows the model to revisit past data after learning new tasks. This insight motivates the development of hybrid rehearsal strategies, which combine the strengths of both approaches \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. For example, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04799)\] proposed a method that trains similar tasks concurrently and revisits dissimilar tasks sequentially, achieving better performance than standard concurrent rehearsal. This aligns with theoretical analyses showing that sequential rehearsal is more effective when tasks are less similar \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. 

Recent work has also explored alternative approaches to mitigate forgetting without relying on explicit rehearsal. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\] introduced a method based on task-conditioned hypernetworks, which generate task-specific weights without storing past data. ### <a id='category-catastrophic-forgetting-in-continual-learning'></a>Catastrophic Forgetting in Continual Learning: 8 papers found.
The concept of catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) has been extensively studied, with various approaches proposed to mitigate its impact. Traditional methods often rely on rehearsal buffers or task identity information to retain knowledge from previous tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08654)\]. However, recent work has shifted toward more efficient strategies, such as Learning without Forgetting, which enables models to preserve existing capabilities while learning new tasks without access to prior training data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. This approach leverages new task data to update the network, maintaining performance on old tasks through careful parameter adjustment. Similarly, the L2P method introduces a dynamic prompting framework that allows pre-trained models to adapt to sequential tasks without explicit task identity, using small, learnable prompts to manage task-specific and invariant knowledge \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08654)\]. Another line of research focuses on structural and algorithmic innovations, such as intelligent synapses that accumulate task-relevant information over time, enabling rapid memory storage without forgetting \[[20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a99d857ecc78316a0d9a774972b775058d5644ca)\]. These methods aim to improve the stability-plasticity trade-off, a key challenge in CL, by either modifying the model architecture or introducing novel training objectives that directly address forgetting \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12588)\]. Additionally, theoretical analyses have explored the effectiveness of different rehearsal strategies, such as concurrent versus sequential rehearsal, revealing that sequential rehearsal can offer advantages when tasks are less similar \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These findings underscore the importance of developing adaptive and efficient mechanisms for continual learning, particularly in scenarios where data distributions change over time and task identities are not available \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\].

Neural networks trained by stochastic gradient descent often degrade on old tasks when trained successively on new tasks with different data distributions. This phenomenon, referred to as catastrophic forgetting, is considered a major hurdle to learning with non-stationary data or sequences of new tasks, and prevents networks from continually accumulating knowledge and skills. We examine this issue in the context of reinforcement learning, in a setting where an agent is exposed to tasks in a sequence. Unlike most other work, we do not provide an explicit indication to the model of task boundaries, which is the most general circumstance for a learning agent exposed to continuous experience. While various methods to counteract catastrophic forfeiting have recently been proposed, we explore a straightforward, general, and seemingly overlooked solution - that of using experience replay buffers for all past events - with a mixture of on- and off-policy learning, leveraging behavioral cloning. ### <a id='category-generative-models-for-experience-replay'></a>Generative Models for Experience Replay: 3 papers found.
The category "Generative Models for Experience Replay" focuses on addressing catastrophic forgetting in continual learning by leveraging generative models to replay past experiences. These models enable the training of a task-solving model ("solver") with data sampled from a deep generative model ("generator"), which mimics the hippocampus's role in short-term memory \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. This approach allows for efficient and feasible replay of previous tasks without requiring access to the full historical dataset, which is often impractical in real-world applications \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Several studies have explored variations of this framework, such as integrating generative models into the main model through feedback mechanisms to reduce computational costs while maintaining performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10635)\]. Additionally, controlled sampling strategies have been proposed to prioritize the most interfered samples for replay, improving performance and reducing forgetting \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. These methods collectively highlight the potential of generative replay as a scalable solution for lifelong learning in artificial intelligence.

This problem remains a hurdle for artificial intelligence systems with sequential learning capabilities.
