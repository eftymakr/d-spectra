## Related Works

Catastrophic forgetting remains a central challenge in continual learning (CL), where models must learn sequentially without losing previously acquired knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Early approaches to address this issue relied on experience replay, where past data is stored and retrained alongside new data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. This strategy has been widely adopted due to its simplicity and effectiveness in mitigating forgetting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. However, recent work has questioned whether concurrent rehearsal is always optimal, suggesting that sequential rehearsal—where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data—may offer additional benefits \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. 

Several studies have explored the role of rehearsal in CL. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08654)\] introduced a method that leverages a small episodic memory to enable continual learning, demonstrating that joint training on current and stored examples can outperform state-of-the-art approaches. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] proposed a framework that uses a generative model to replay past experiences, enabling the model to maintain knowledge of previous tasks without storing all data explicitly. This approach is inspired by the brain’s memory replay mechanisms, which are thought to play a key role in preserving memories \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. 

Rehearsal-based methods often rely on the assumption that storing past data is feasible. However, in real-world applications, memory constraints make this approach impractical \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. To address this, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\] introduced a method that uses a tiny episodic memory, showing that even with minimal storage, joint training on current and stored examples can yield strong performance. This aligns with findings from \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7bfb4ef17eabec1acd266958bdb08622eebfbb05)\], which demonstrated that replaying internal representations, rather than raw data, can effectively prevent catastrophic forgetting. 

The effectiveness of rehearsal strategies depends on the similarity between tasks. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10635)\] found that when tasks are less similar, sequential rehearsal outperforms concurrent rehearsal, as it allows the model to revisit past data after learning new tasks. This insight motivates the development of hybrid rehearsal strategies, which combine the strengths of both approaches \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. For example, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.04799)\] proposed a method that trains similar tasks concurrently and revisits dissimilar tasks sequentially, achieving better performance than standard concurrent rehearsal. This aligns with theoretical analyses showing that sequential rehearsal is more effective when tasks are less similar \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. 

Recent work has also explored alternative approaches to mitigate forgetting without relying on explicit rehearsal. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00695)\] introduced a method based on task-conditioned hypernetworks, which generate task-specific weights without storing past data.
