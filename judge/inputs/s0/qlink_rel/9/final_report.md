## Related Works

The segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been a focal point of research due to its importance in disease monitoring and treatment evaluation. Traditional methods often rely on manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-observer variability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. To address these limitations, automated methods have been proposed, ranging from atlas-based techniques to deep learning approaches. Atlas-based methods, such as those described in \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ecafc65d094c52300ffa2c9640303c584f4d569b)\], use statistical and topological atlases to constrain the segmentation of brain structures, ensuring anatomical consistency. These methods have demonstrated competitive accuracy with state-of-the-art techniques while segmenting the entire brain \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ecafc65d094c52300ffa2c9640303c584f4d569b)\]. However, they may struggle with the high variability of MS lesions across different patients and imaging protocols \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\].

Deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool for MS lesion segmentation, particularly in handling the complexity of lesion appearance and anatomical variability. Early approaches, such as the cascaded 3D convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\], showed significant improvements in accuracy by using two CNNs in sequence to reduce misclassification. These methods were evaluated on public datasets like MICCAI2008 and demonstrated superior performance compared to existing tools \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.04869)\]. Similarly, the fully convolutional neural network (FCN) approach in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\] introduced a dual-pathway architecture to capture multi-contrast MRI data, achieving a Dice score of 90.48 on the ISBI 2015 challenge data, comparable to human raters \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09172)\]. 

The challenge of class imbalance, particularly in distinguishing between rim and non-rim lesions, has also been addressed in recent work. For example, the multi-branch CNN in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\] was designed to handle multi-modal data and improve segmentation accuracy by fusing features from different modalities. This approach was evaluated on both public and private datasets, showing high improvements in segmentation performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02942)\]. Another notable work, \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3fe6e8e47663a38c037813bc35524fe0931b31b2)\], proposed a 2.5D stacked slice method using Tiramisu networks, achieving a top rank in the ISBI challenge with a score of 93.1 \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3fe6e8e47663a38c037813bc35524fe0931b31b2)\]. 

The issue of domain adaptation, where models trained on one dataset may not generalize to new imaging protocols, has also been explored. The one-shot domain adaptation method in \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\] demonstrated that even with minimal annotated data from a new domain, the model could maintain performance comparable to fully trained models \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\]. This is particularly relevant for clinical settings where labeled data is scarce \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12415)\].

### <a id='category-lesion-segmentation-techniques'></a>Lesion Segmentation Techniques: 8 papers found.
The related works section focuses on the challenges and advancements in automated multiple sclerosis (MS) lesion segmentation, particularly in the context of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data. Automated segmentation methods have been extensively explored to address the variability in lesion appearance, location, and size, as well as the limitations of manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-observer variability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Recent approaches have integrated generative models and machine learning techniques to improve segmentation accuracy, especially for white matter lesions and other complex structures \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05135)\]. These methods often rely on multi-contrast MRI data and adaptive models that can generalize across different imaging protocols and scanners \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/691751bfa44017d8041e17409411f9e9ffc3dec6)\]. One promising technique involves the use of non-local means (NLM) with rotation-invariant distance measures to capture lesion spatial distribution and improve segmentation robustness \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1562d113593782a0ce01347b594e05abae02fbfa)\]. Additionally, supervised methods have shown competitive results, particularly when validated on large clinical datasets, with strong correlations between automated and manual segmentation \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8611112d0c105f752e239fe8729e652f8ee52ce3)\]. However, challenges remain in achieving consistent performance across diverse patient populations and imaging conditions, as no single method has been widely adopted in clinical practice \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Some studies have also emphasized the importance of synthetic data generation to address class imbalance, particularly for rare lesion types like paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), which are critical for prognosis and diagnosis \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. These efforts highlight the ongoing need for robust, standardized validation frameworks to advance automated lesion segmentation in MS research and clinical applications.

QSM has emerged as a valuable tool for detecting chronic active lesions, which are characterized by paramagnetic iron deposition and are associated with ongoing inflammation and tissue damage \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. These lesions, identifiable through susceptibility-based MRI, are linked to more aggressive disease progression and earlier disability onset, even in patients on disease-modifying therapies \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. The ability to differentiate rim lesions from non-rim lesions is critical for monitoring disease activity and treatment response \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. The presence of iron in chronically activated microglia within MS lesions further supports the utility of iron detection via MRI as a biomarker for chronic inflammation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. The clinical relevance of QSM is underscored by its potential to inform treatment strategies targeting perilesional inflammation, as demonstrated by longitudinal studies showing that rim lesions are associated with progressive tissue damage and greater disability \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Overall, the integration of QSM into clinical practice represents a significant step forward in the non-invasive assessment of MS pathology \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\].

### <a id='category-probabilistic-segmentation-methods'></a>Probabilistic Segmentation Methods: 2 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «The location and extent of white matter lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are important criteria for diagnosis, follow-up and prognosis of multiple sclerosis (MS). Clinical trials have shown that quantitative values, such as lesion volumes, are meaningful in MS prognosis. Manual lesion delineation for the segmentation of lesions is, however, time-consuming and suffers from observer variability. In this paper, we propose MSmetrix, an accurate and reliable automatic method for lesion segmentation based on MRI, independent of scanner or acquisition protocol and without requiring any training data. In MSmetrix, 3D T1-weighted and FLAIR MR images are used in a probabilistic model to detect white matter (WM) lesions as an outlier to normal brain while segmenting the brain tissue into grey matter, WM and cerebrospinal fluid. The actual lesion segmentation is performed based on prior knowledge about the location (within WM) and the appearance (hyperintense on FLAIR) of lesions. The accuracy of MSmetrix is evaluated by comparing its output with expert reference segmentations of 20 MRI datasets of MS patients. Spatial overlap (Dice) between the MSmetrix and the expert lesion segmentation is 0.67 ± 0.11. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) equals 0.8 indicating a good volumetric agreement between the MSmetrix and expert labelling. The reproducibility of MSmetrix' lesion volumes is evaluated based on 10 MS patients, scanned twice with a short interval on three different scanners. The agreement between the first and the second scan on each scanner is evaluated through the spatial overlap and absolute lesion volume difference between them. The spatial overlap was 0.69 ± 0.14 and absolute total lesion volume difference between the two scans was 0.54 ± 0.58 ml. Finally, the accuracy and reproducibility of MSmetrix compare favourably with other publicly available MS lesion segmentation algorithms, applied on the same data using default parameter settings.»
