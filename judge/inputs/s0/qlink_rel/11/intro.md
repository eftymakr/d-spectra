## Related Works

3D human pose estimation has attracted significant research interest due to its applications in action recognition, human-computer interaction, and augmented reality. Early methods primarily relied on 2D pose estimation followed by a mapping to 3D space, often using regression or optimization techniques \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08585)\]. However, these approaches struggled with depth ambiguity and self-occlusion, leading to temporally inconsistent results \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03098)\]. Recent advances have focused on leveraging spatial and temporal dependencies to improve accuracy and robustness \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/50167a9e6bdc1f64a58fe8a3c990105fec0bd2bc)\].

Several works have explored the use of graph-based models to capture the structural relationships between body joints. For example, \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/50167a9e6bdc1f64a58fe8a3c990105fec0bd2bc)\] proposed a graph convolutional network (GCN) to model spatial dependencies in 3D pose estimation, incorporating domain knowledge about human body configurations. Similarly, \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0646857f867d9a624f2b53d1ffb4de99e225b2d3)\] introduced a modulated GCN that learns different feature transformations for each node, improving performance by up to 10%. These methods, however, often suffer from limited receptive fields and suboptimal graph structures \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0646857f867d9a624f2b53d1ffb4de99e225b2d3)\].

Transformers have also been applied to 3D human pose estimation, offering a powerful alternative to convolutional architectures. \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10455)\] introduced PoseFormer, a purely transformer-based approach that models spatial-temporal relationships across frames. This method outperformed existing convolutional models on standard benchmarks. \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14304)\] further improved transformer-based architectures by introducing a strided transformer encoder, which reduces computational cost while maintaining long-range dependency modeling. These transformer-based methods have shown promising results, particularly in capturing cross-frame correlations \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14304)\].

Another line of research focuses on anatomical constraints to improve pose estimation. \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/555a23a05777a0b03614b195c7c72f7c626a97e9)\] proposed an anatomy-aware approach that decomposes the task into bone direction and length prediction, leveraging the consistency of bone lengths over time. This method introduced a novel joint shift loss to bridge the training of bone length and direction prediction, leading to improved accuracy on benchmark datasets. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.00159)\] also explored anatomical constraints by using a structure-aware regression approach, which outperformed previous methods on Human3.6M and MPII benchmarks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.00159)\].
