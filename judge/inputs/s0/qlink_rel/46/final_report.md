## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in traditional sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges, often leveraging domain-specific components or graph-based models to capture cross-domain interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. For instance, the DA-GCN model introduces a domain-aware graph convolution network to learn user-specific node representations, incorporating attention mechanisms to selectively guide message passing across domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Similarly, the π-Net model addresses the shared-account challenge by introducing a shared account filter unit and a cross-domain transfer unit to generate recommendations for multiple domains \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. These models highlight the importance of capturing both intra-domain and inter-domain relationships to improve recommendation accuracy.

Another line of research focuses on contrastive learning and self-attention mechanisms to enhance sequential recommendation. The CL4SRec model leverages contrastive learning to derive self-supervision signals from user behavior sequences, improving the quality of user representations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. The BERT4Rec model, inspired by the Transformer architecture, employs bidirectional self-attention to capture both left and right context in user behavior sequences, leading to more accurate recommendations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. The TiSASRec model further improves upon these approaches by explicitly modeling time intervals between interactions, enhancing the temporal understanding of user behavior \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of self-attention mechanisms in capturing complex sequential patterns.

In addition to self-attention, some works have explored graph-based approaches for cross-domain recommendation. The CoNet model introduces collaborative cross networks to enable dual knowledge transfer across domains, improving the performance of recommendation systems \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. The DDTCDR model proposes a deep dual transfer approach that iteratively transfers information between domains, improving recommendation accuracy in both source and target domains \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. The DDGHM model combines dual dynamic graph modeling with hybrid metric training to address data sparsity and improve prediction accuracy \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of integrating graph-based and attention-based mechanisms to capture cross-domain interactions effectively.

Recent works have also focused on addressing the open-world assumption in CDSR, where data distribution shifts are common in real-world scenarios \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. The AMID framework introduces an adaptive multi-interest debiasing approach to handle these shifts, improving recommendation performance under open-world conditions \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. The DREAM model proposes a decoupled representation approach that simultaneously learns single- and cross-domain user preferences, leveraging supervised contrastive learning to address data sparsity and class imbalance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\].

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Sequential recommender systems seek to exploit the order of users' interactions, in order to predict their next action based on the context of what they have done recently. Traditionally, Markov Chains(MCs), and more recently Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Self Attention (SA) have proliferated due to their ability to capture the dynamics of sequential patterns. However a simplifying assumption made by most of these models is to regard interaction histories as ordered sequences, without regard for the time intervals between each interaction (i.e., they model the time-order but not the actual timestamp). In this paper, we seek to explicitly model the timestamps of interactions within a sequential modeling framework to explore the influence of different time intervals on next item prediction. We propose TiSASRec (Time Interval aware Self-attention based sequential recommendation), which models both the absolute positions of items as well as the time intervals between them in a sequence. Extensive empirical studies show the features of TiSASRec under different settings and compare the performance of self-attention with different positional encodings. Furthermore, experimental results show that our method outperforms various state-of-the-art sequential models on both sparse and dense datasets and different evaluation metrics.»
