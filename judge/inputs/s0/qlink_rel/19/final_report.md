## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match speech content and emotion. Early approaches relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, but recent advances have shifted toward data-driven methods using deep learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. These methods typically encode audio signals into latent representations and map them to facial animations, often leveraging pre-trained self-supervised audio models as encoders \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. However, these models face challenges due to the complex interplay between phonetic content and facial motion.

A significant body of work has focused on improving lip synchronization and facial expression accuracy. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\] introduced a Transformer-based autoregressive model, Face-Former, which encodes long-term audio context and autoregressively predicts 3D face meshes. This approach addresses the issue of limited context in prior works by integrating self-supervised speech representations. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\] proposed EmoTalk, which disentangles emotional and content aspects of speech to generate more expressive facial animations. This method uses an emotion disentangling encoder and an emotion-guided decoder to enhance emotional realism.

Another line of research has explored the use of cross-modal disentanglement to separate speech content from emotional expressions. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\] introduced MeshTalk, which disentangles audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information using a novel cross-modality loss. This enables the generation of realistic facial animations, including eye blinks and eyebrow movements, beyond just lip motion. Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00023)\] proposed SelfTalk, a self-supervised framework that learns 3D talking faces by leveraging a commutative training diagram that exchanges features across audio, text, and lip shape modalities. This approach reduces dependency on labeled data and improves visual accuracy.

The issue of phonetically similar syllables causing averaging effects in lip motion generation has also been addressed. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\] introduced Imitator, which learns identity-specific speaking styles from a short reference video to produce more realistic facial expressions.
