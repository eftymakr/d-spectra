## Related Works

Video super-resolution (VSR) has been a focal point in computer vision, with recent advances leveraging deep learning to address the challenges of temporal coherence and detail preservation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c6a8ff4d9dcdf9e5d82310b847af999ed6655f65)\]. Early methods often relied on explicit motion estimation and compensation, which were computationally intensive and prone to errors \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c6a8ff4d9dcdf9e5d82310b847af999ed6655f65)\]. For instance, the Real-Time Video Super-Resolution with Spatio-Temporal Networks and Motion Compensation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.05250)\] introduced a method that combined early fusion, slow fusion, and 3D convolutions to exploit temporal redundancies while maintaining real-time performance. However, these approaches often struggled with computational efficiency and temporal consistency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.05250)\].

More recently, recurrent architectures have emerged as a promising solution for VSR, enabling the model to assimilate information from multiple frames without increasing computational demands \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04590)\]. The Frame-Recurrent Video Super-Resolution \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04590)\] proposed an end-to-end trainable framework that uses previously inferred high-resolution (HR) estimates to super-resolve subsequent frames, naturally encouraging temporal consistency. This approach significantly reduced computational costs compared to sliding window methods \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04590)\]. Similarly, the Video Super-Resolution via Bidirectional Recurrent Convolutional Networks \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a2c50f93f6d0892918e0ff653750d198ccab48d0)\] introduced a fully convolutional RNN that reduced the number of parameters and modeled temporal dependencies at a finer level, achieving faster performance than traditional multi-frame SR methods \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a2c50f93f6d0892918e0ff653750d198ccab48d0)\].

Transformers have also been explored for VSR, although their direct application faced challenges due to the lack of spatial-temporal locality exploitation and feature alignment \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06847)\]. The Video Super-Resolution Transformer \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06847)\] addressed these issues by introducing a spatial-temporal convolutional self-attention layer and a bidirectional optical flow-based feed-forward layer, enhancing feature alignment and correlation discovery across video frames \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06847)\]. This work demonstrated the effectiveness of Transformers in VSR, achieving competitive results on benchmark datasets \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06847)\].

Diffusion models have gained traction for image and video generation due to their ability to produce high-quality outputs with controllable guidance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. However, applying diffusion models to video generation posed challenges in maintaining temporal coherence and reducing computational costs \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\]. The Align Your Latents: High-Resolution Video Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\] proposed a method to train diffusion models in a compressed latent space, enabling efficient video generation. This approach was further extended to handle high-resolution video synthesis, achieving state-of-the-art performance on real-world datasets \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\].
