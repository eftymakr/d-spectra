## Related Works

Generative retrieval (GR) has recently emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval (IR), shifting the focus from traditional index-retrieve pipelines to end-to-end document retrieval via sequence-to-sequence models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Unlike conventional methods that rely on precomputed dense vectors or inverted indexes, GR models directly generate document identifiers (docids) for a given query \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\]. However, most existing GR models are primarily evaluated on static document collections, with limited exploration of their performance in dynamic corpora where documents are continuously added \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This section reviews related works in generative retrieval, particularly focusing on docid design, scalability, and continual learning.

Docid design is a critical factor in GR performance. Current approaches typically use either numeric-based or text-based docids. Numeric-based docids are efficient but often lead to poor generalization in dynamic settings due to their strong dependence on the initial training set \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This overfitting issue is exacerbated in dynamic corpora, where new documents are frequently added \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. In contrast, text-based docids, which are more semantically aligned with language models' pretrained knowledge, demonstrate superior performance in dynamic settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This is attributed to their fine-grained design and high lexical diversity, which enable better adaptation to new documents \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Several works have explored the benefits of text-based docids, such as in GenRet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\], which learns semantic docids via a discrete auto-encoding approach, and in NOVO \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6682a3679a17d090830635f1c420126d20eae54a)\], which proposes learnable and interpretable docids optimized for retrieval tasks.

Scalability remains a significant challenge for generative retrieval models, especially when dealing with large-scale corpora. Early works like the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\] demonstrated the potential of using a single Transformer to encode the entire corpus, but scalability to millions of passages was not thoroughly investigated until more recent studies \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. These studies revealed that synthetic queries are essential during indexing, and model parameter scaling does not always translate to improved retrieval performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. To address these challenges, works such as DSI++ \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\] and Ultron \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\] have proposed continual learning strategies to incrementally update models with new documents while maintaining retrieval performance over previously indexed content.

### <a id='category-docid-design-approaches'></a>Docid Design Approaches: 6 papers found.
Document retrieval methods have evolved significantly, particularly with the rise of generative retrieval (GR) paradigms that aim to directly generate document identifiers (docids) for retrieval tasks. Traditional index-retrieve frameworks, while effective, are limited in their ability to optimize retrieval quality in an end-to-end manner \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. In contrast, GR approaches represent documents as discrete identifiers and use sequence-to-sequence models to generate these identifiers based on queries, enabling more flexible and semantically rich retrieval \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. However, the design of docids remains a critical challenge. Early approaches relied on fixed rule-based docids, such as document titles or BERT clustering results, which often fail to capture full semantic information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. More recent methods, such as GenRet, propose learning docids through discrete auto-encoding, allowing for semantic-rich representations that are optimized for retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. Similarly, NOVO introduces learnable docids based on n-grams, which can be optimized during training to better capture document semantics and relationships \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. Other works, like Ultron, focus on model-based indexers that encode document knowledge directly into the model, using semantically meaningful docids to improve retrieval performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) further advances this by parameterizing the entire index-retrieve pipeline, allowing for direct query-to-docid mapping \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of docid design in generative retrieval, with text-based docids showing superior generalization to unseen documents compared to numeric-based ones \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Overall, the trend toward learning docids rather than relying on static or rule-based identifiers reflects a growing emphasis on semantic alignment, fine-grained representation, and adaptability to dynamic corpora \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\].

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-(gr)-overview'></a>Generative Retrieval (GR) Overview: 6 papers found.
Generative retrieval (GR) has been increasingly explored as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to sequence-to-sequence modeling that directly generates document identifiers \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Early studies demonstrated the potential of GR models on small-scale corpora, but their scalability to larger datasets remained unexplored until recent work systematically evaluated their performance across varying corpus sizes, including the MS MARCO passage ranking task with 8.8M passages \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This research highlighted key challenges, such as the importance of synthetic queries during indexing and the limitations of naive parameter scaling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Subsequent work, such as RIPOR, introduced optimization frameworks that improved GR performance on large-scale benchmarks, achieving significant improvements in metrics like MRR \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. Similarly, CorpusBrain demonstrated the effectiveness of end-to-end generative retrieval models for knowledge-intensive tasks, eliminating the need for external indexes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. In fact, generative retrieval has also been applied to fact verification, where GERE outperformed traditional methods by generating document titles and evidence sentences directly \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05511)\]. These advancements underscore the growing interest in GR for dynamic and large-scale retrieval tasks, although challenges remain in balancing efficiency with performance, particularly when dealing with numeric-based docids that tend to overfit to initial document sets \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\].
