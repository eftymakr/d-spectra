## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm that replaces traditional search index data structures with a single large-scale neural network, enabling improved efficiency and seamless integration with generative large language models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This approach directly generates document identifiers through corpus-specific constrained decoding, offering a more end-to-end solution compared to conventional index-retrieve pipelines \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. The generalization capabilities of generative retrieval on out-of-distribution corpora have attracted significant attention, as evidenced by recent advancements in both theoretical and practical implementations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\].

Prior work has explored various methods to enhance generative retrieval. For instance, SEAL proposes using all ngrams in a passage as potential identifiers, allowing autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams that are then mapped to full passages through an efficient data structure \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This approach outperforms prior autoregressive methods and achieves an average improvement of at least 10 points over established retrieval solutions on the KILT benchmark \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, GenRet introduces a document tokenization learning method to define semantic docids via a discrete auto-encoding approach, enabling fully end-to-end modeling of document retrieval tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. GenRet also demonstrates significant improvements on unseen documents and outperforms comparable baselines on multiple datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\].

The use of dense representations for retrieval has also been extensively studied. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) shows that dense embeddings, learned from a dual-encoder framework, can outperform traditional sparse methods like BM25, achieving a 9%-19% absolute improvement in top-20 passage retrieval accuracy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. This work laid the foundation for subsequent research into dense retrieval techniques, including Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Learning (ANCE), which constructs negatives from an ANN index to improve training effectiveness \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. ANCE significantly boosts dense retrieval models, nearly matching the accuracy of sparse-retrieval-and-BERT-reranking approaches while providing a 100x speed-up \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has also been explored as a way to integrate retrieval with generation, allowing models to access external knowledge bases \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Blended RAG further improves RAG accuracy by combining semantic search techniques with hybrid query-based retrievers, achieving new benchmarks on IR datasets like NQ and TREC-COVID \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07220)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of both retrieval quality and generation capabilities in knowledge-intensive tasks.
