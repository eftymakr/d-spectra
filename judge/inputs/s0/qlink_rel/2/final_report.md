## Related Works

Nonverbal communication plays a crucial role in human interaction, encompassing gestures, facial expressions, and body language that convey intent and emotion \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4814337c5145dbea034b9c646163dc2e71e8ebff)\]. However, existing large language models (LLMs) have limited capacity to incorporate these nonverbal elements, hindering their ability to create immersive conversational experiences \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/86f81c8a46a84cd969ed80e88395ba5de709794f)\]. This limitation has motivated recent efforts to develop multimodal models that integrate both verbal and nonverbal cues \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02490)\]. 

Several works have explored the integration of vision and language in large models. Flamingo, for instance, is a Visual Language Model (VLM) that enables few-shot learning by bridging pretrained vision-only and language-only models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. It handles sequences of interleaved visual and textual data, allowing models to be trained on large-scale multimodal corpora \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. Similarly, LLaVA is an end-to-end trained large multimodal model that connects a vision encoder with a large language model (LLM) for general-purpose visual and language understanding \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of combining vision and language for tasks such as visual question answering and image captioning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\].

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have also gained attention for their ability to perform a wide range of tasks, including language understanding, generation, and vision tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. Kosmos-1, for example, is a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) that can perceive general modalities, learn in context, and follow instructions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. It is trained on web-scale multimodal corpora, including arbitrarily interleaved text and images, image-caption pairs, and text data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. This training strategy enables the model to achieve impressive performance on tasks such as image recognition with descriptions and visual question answering \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\].

The importance of nonverbal communication has also been emphasized in the context of multimodal language analysis. The CMU-MOSEI dataset, for instance, focuses on sentiment analysis and emotion recognition, capturing the asynchronous nature of multimodal language \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/006fdeff6e1a81c404317ee4056d6cc72f9c0e50)\]. The dataset includes video, audio, and text modalities, allowing for in-depth studies of how these modalities interact \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/006fdeff6e1a81c404317ee4056d6cc72f9c0e50)\]. A novel multimodal fusion technique called the Dynamic Fusion Graph (DFG) has been introduced to improve the interpretability and performance of such models \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/006fdeff6e1a81c404317ee4056d6cc72f9c0e50)\].

Recent benchmarks have also highlighted the need for comprehensive evaluation of MLLMs. The MME benchmark, for example, evaluates both perception and cognition abilities across 14 subtasks, avoiding data leakage by manually designing instruction-answer pairs
