## Related Works

Recent advancements in information retrieval have led to the development of various paradigms for document and table retrieval. Traditional methods rely on the "representation-index-search" pipeline, where tables are encoded into dense vectors, indexed, and then retrieved based on semantic similarity with queries \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06159)\]. While these methods achieve high accuracy, they suffer from error accumulation during representation and indexing, which negatively impacts search performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. Additionally, limited query-table interaction hinders effective semantic alignment, further limiting accuracy improvements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

To address these limitations, differentiable search index (DSI) models have emerged as a promising alternative. DSI unifies indexing and retrieval into a single model, eliminating the need for separate indexing and retrieval stages \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach enables direct query-to-document mapping, improving efficiency and reducing error propagation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. However, DSI models face challenges in continual learning, as retraining is required when new documents are added, leading to catastrophic forgetting \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. To mitigate this, DSI++ was introduced, which employs continual learning strategies to incrementally update the model while preserving previously learned information \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\].

Another line of research focuses on neural corpus indexers that generate document identifiers directly from queries. The Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) leverages a prefix-aware decoder and semantic document identifiers to improve recall performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Similarly, the ColBERT model introduces a late interaction architecture that independently encodes queries and documents, then computes their similarity through a lightweight interaction step, achieving significant speed improvements \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of end-to-end neural retrieval systems, though they often require extensive computational resources \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\].

For table retrieval, the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) model has shown effectiveness by using dense representations to retrieve relevant passages \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. This approach has been further extended to table retrieval, where semantic similarity between queries and tables is leveraged for more accurate matching \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06159)\]. The Birdie framework builds upon these ideas by using a differentiable search index to unify indexing and retrieval, reducing error accumulation and improving query-table interaction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. It introduces a prefix-aware identifier and synthetic query generation to enhance semantic alignment, achieving a 16.8% accuracy improvement over dense methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

The Semantic-Enhanced Differentiable Search Index (SE-DSI) further improves DSI by incorporating elaboration and rehearsal strategies from cognitive psychology, leading to better document memorization and retrieval performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\].
