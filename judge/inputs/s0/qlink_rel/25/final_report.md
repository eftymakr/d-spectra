## Related Works

The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) with large language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning capabilities in claim verification has gained significant attention in recent research. While LLMs have demonstrated strong reasoning abilities, their performance on complex, multi-step reasoning tasks remains limited, particularly when compared to structured knowledge sources such as KGs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. KGs, on the other hand, provide rich, semantically grounded representations that are well-suited for reasoning but are often underutilized in verification tasks due to the reliance on unstructured text corpora \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of KGs to improve LLM reasoning. For instance, the QA-GNN model introduces a joint reasoning framework that combines LLMs and KGs by estimating the relevance of KG nodes relative to a question and performing graph-based message passing to update representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06378)\]. Similarly, the KGP method constructs a knowledge graph over multiple documents to guide LLMs in multi-document question answering, leveraging semantic and structural relationships to improve reasoning \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11730)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of integrating KGs with LLMs but often focus on specific tasks or limited reasoning pipelines \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06378)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11730)\].

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has also been proposed as a way to enhance LLMs with external knowledge. RAG models combine pre-trained LLMs with non-parametric memory, such as Wikipedia, to improve accuracy and reduce hallucinations in knowledge-intensive tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. The LLM-Augmenter system further builds on this idea by incorporating external databases and iterative feedback mechanisms to refine LLM outputs, reducing hallucinations without sacrificing fluency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12813)\]. These methods demonstrate that external knowledge can significantly improve the reliability of LLM outputs, especially in domains requiring factual accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12813)\].

The ability of LLMs to perform zero-shot reasoning has also been explored. Zero-shot-CoT prompting, for example, enables LLMs to generate step-by-step reasoning without the need for task-specific examples, achieving notable improvements on arithmetic and symbolic reasoning tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. However, LLMs still struggle with multi-step modular reasoning and reasoning over KGs without adaptation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. This limitation underscores the need for frameworks that can effectively bridge the gap between LLMs and structured knowledge sources \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\].

### <a id='category-integration-of-knowledge-graphs-with-llms-for-reasoning'></a>Integration of Knowledge Graphs with LLMs for Reasoning: 3 papers found.
The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) with large language models (LLMs) for reasoning has gained significant attention, aiming to enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs by leveraging the structured and semantically rich information in KGs \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\]. Existing approaches often struggle with effectively incorporating KGs into the reasoning pipeline, either due to the limitations of LLMs in handling structured data or the lack of seamless integration between the two \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.02151)\]. To address these challenges, recent works have proposed frameworks that combine LLMs with KGs through joint reasoning and message passing mechanisms, enabling more accurate and interpretable inferences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06378)\]. For instance, QA-GNN introduces a relevance scoring mechanism to identify important KG nodes relative to a given QA context and employs joint reasoning through graph-based message passing to enhance the model's understanding \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06378)\]. Similarly, KagNet utilizes a hierarchical attention mechanism to ground questions into schema graphs derived from external knowledge graphs, improving the transparency and explainability of the reasoning process \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.02151)\]. These methods highlight the potential of integrating KGs with LMs to achieve better performance in commonsense reasoning tasks, as demonstrated by state-of-the-art results on datasets such as CommonsenseQA \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.02151)\]. Additionally, efforts to improve the interpretability and robustness of these models have led to the development of prompting pipelines that enable LLMs to incorporate KG inputs and generate more reliable reasoning pathways \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\]. Overall, these studies underscore the importance of combining structured knowledge from KGs with the strong reasoning abilities of LLMs to address the limitations of both individual components \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06378)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.02151)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\].

### <a id='category-retrieval-augmented-generation-for-llms'></a>Retrieval-Augmented Generation for LLMs: 4 papers found.
The category "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for LLMs" explores methods to enhance large language models (LLMs) by integrating external knowledge sources, addressing limitations such as hallucinations, outdated knowledge, and poor performance on knowledge-intensive tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Researchers have proposed frameworks like RAG, where pre-trained models combine parametric memory (e.g., seq2seq models) with non-parametric memory (e.g., dense vector indexes of Wikipedia) accessed via neural retrievers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. This approach enables models to generate more specific, diverse, and factual responses compared to parametric-only baselines \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Additionally, systems like LLM-Augmenter introduce plug-and-play modules to ground responses in external databases and iteratively refine prompts using utility functions to improve factuality without sacrificing fluency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12813)\]. A comprehensive review highlights the evolution of RAG paradigms, including Naive, Advanced, and Modular RAG, emphasizing the tripartite foundation of retrieval, generation, and augmentation techniques \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\]. These works also identify challenges such as the need for efficient retrieval mechanisms and the integration of dynamic knowledge updates \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Furthermore, studies have shown that retrieval augmentation significantly improves performance on less popular factual knowledge, while scaling primarily enhances memorization of popular knowledge \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511)\]. Overall, these contributions underscore the potential of retrieval-augmented generation to bridge the gap between LLMs and structured knowledge sources, enabling more accurate and reliable reasoning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12813)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\].

### <a id='category-zero-shot-reasoning-and-prompting-techniques'></a>Zero-Shot Reasoning and Prompting Techniques: 3 papers found.
The category "Zero-Shot Reasoning and Prompting Techniques" explores how large language models (LLMs) can perform reasoning tasks without explicit training on specific examples, leveraging simple prompting strategies. Researchers have demonstrated that LLMs can achieve significant improvements in reasoning tasks by using techniques such as chain of thought (CoT) prompting, where models are guided to generate step-by-step reasoning processes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. This approach has shown remarkable success in arithmetic and symbolic reasoning benchmarks, such as MultiArith, GSM8K, and AQUA-RAT, with models like InstructGPT and PaLM achieving accuracy improvements from 17.7% to 78.7% and 10.4% to 40.7%, respectively \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\]. Additionally, GPT-3, a 175 billion parameter model, has exhibited strong few-shot learning capabilities across a wide range of NLP tasks, including translation, question-answering, and reasoning, without any fine-tuning \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. These findings highlight the potential of zero-shot and few-shot learning in LLMs, suggesting that structured prompting can unlock broader cognitive capabilities without the need for extensive training data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. The versatility of these techniques across diverse reasoning tasks further underscores the untapped potential of LLMs in general-purpose reasoning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08302)\].
