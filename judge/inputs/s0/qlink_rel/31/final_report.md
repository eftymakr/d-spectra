## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm in machine learning, particularly in natural language processing and computer vision, where it enables models to learn rich representations from unlabeled data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. However, its application to DNA pre-training has been limited, primarily due to the lack of sufficient information in pure DNA sequences. Unlike images or text, DNA sequences alone do not capture the complex regulatory mechanisms that govern gene function, such as chromatin accessibility and other genomic profiles \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. This limitation has led researchers to explore alternative approaches, including supervised training for genomic profile prediction, which can better capture the functional context of DNA sequences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\].

Several studies have investigated unsupervised learning methods for visual and textual representations, such as Contrastive Predictive Coding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\], which learns representations by predicting future samples in latent space. While these methods have shown promise, they often rely on large amounts of unlabeled data and may not fully capture the biological complexity of DNA sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Similarly, approaches like SimCLR \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\] and MoCo \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\] have demonstrated the effectiveness of contrastive learning in vision tasks, but their direct application to genomic data remains unexplored.

In the field of natural language processing, BERT \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] and RoBERTa \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\] have set new benchmarks for pre-training models using large-scale unlabeled text. These models use bidirectional transformers to learn contextual representations, which could be adapted for DNA sequence analysis. However, the regulatory context of DNA sequences requires a different set of features, such as chromatin accessibility and DNA methylation, which are not captured by traditional language models \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4f7d1e04c9d30ce12df3e085d10d70997e6ea1a5)\].

Recent work on self-supervised learning has also explored the use of predictive tasks, such as predicting image rotations \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07728)\], to learn semantic features without explicit labels. This approach has been successful in computer vision, but its applicability to genomic data is yet to be validated. ### <a id='category-self-supervised-learning-approaches'></a>Self-Supervised Learning Approaches: 15 papers found.
In the realm of self-supervised learning, several approaches have been proposed to learn effective representations from unlabeled data, often leveraging contextual or predictive tasks. For instance, Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) has been introduced as a universal unsupervised learning approach that learns representations by predicting future samples in latent space, using a probabilistic contrastive loss to ensure the latent space captures maximally useful information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Similarly, spatial context has been used as a supervisory signal, where models are trained to predict the relative position of image patches, enabling the learning of rich visual representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. In the domain of satellite imagery, self-supervised contrastive pre-training methods have been employed to leverage the large availability of unlabeled satellite images for improving land cover classification tasks, achieving significant improvements in performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. Additionally, methods such as noise-contrastive estimation have been used to learn instance-level discriminative features, surpassing state-of-the-art results on ImageNet classification \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\]. Transfer learning, where models are pre-trained on data-rich tasks and fine-tuned on downstream tasks, has also been explored, with a unified framework converting language problems into text-to-text formats to achieve state-of-the-art results on multiple benchmarks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. Furthermore, improvements in data-efficient recognition have been achieved by revisiting and enhancing CPC, leading to state-of-the-art linear classification accuracy on ImageNet and improved transfer learning performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the potential of self-supervised learning to capture meaningful representations across diverse domains, often outperforming traditional supervised methods when labeled data is scarce \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-methods'></a>Contrastive Learning Methods: 3 papers found.
Contrastive learning methods have gained significant traction in unsupervised visual representation learning, with frameworks like SimCLR and MoCo demonstrating strong performance. SimCLR introduces a simple yet effective approach by emphasizing the importance of data augmentation and learnable nonlinear transformations, leading to competitive results on ImageNet \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. MoCo, on the other hand, leverages a dynamic dictionary and a moving-averaged encoder to maintain consistency in contrastive learning, achieving results that rival supervised pre-training \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. Additionally, improvements such as using an MLP projection head and enhanced data augmentation in MoCo have further strengthened these methods, making them more accessible without requiring large training batches \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\]. These developments highlight the evolving landscape of contrastive learning, where key components like data augmentation, model architecture, and training strategies play crucial roles in improving representation quality.
