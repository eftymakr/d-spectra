## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Similarly, conventional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making them less practical in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. This section reviews related work on TTS, prosody modeling, and controllability, highlighting key contributions and limitations.

Early TTS systems were based on concatenative synthesis, where pre-recorded speech segments were stitched together \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. However, these systems lacked flexibility in prosody control and were limited by the quality of the speech corpus \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. With the advent of neural TTS, models like Tacotron \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\] and FastSpeech \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] have enabled end-to-end synthesis from text, producing high-quality speech. These models typically use recurrent or transformer-based architectures to generate mel-spectrograms, which are then decoded into audio using vocoders like WaveNet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\].

Prosody control in TTS has been a major focus of recent research. Several works have introduced methods for fine-grained prosody control, such as prosody embeddings \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\] and style tokens \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. These methods allow for the modulation of pitch, duration, and energy, enabling expressive speech synthesis. However, many of these approaches require additional training or explicit prosody annotations, which limits their applicability in real-world scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. For instance, the work by \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\] introduces a hierarchical prosody modeling framework that conditions the TTS model on both coarse and fine-grained acoustic features, enabling more intuitive control over prosody dimensions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\].

Non-autoregressive TTS models, such as FastSpeech \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] and Parallel Tacotron \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\], have also gained popularity due to their parallelizable architecture and faster inference speeds. These models often incorporate duration prediction and knowledge distillation to improve the quality of synthesized speech \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. However, they still face challenges in achieving diverse and natural prosody variations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\].

For example, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\] proposes a hierarchical generative model that allows for disentangled control over attributes such as speaking style, accent, and background noise.
