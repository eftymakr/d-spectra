## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Similarly, conventional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making them less practical in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. This section reviews related work on TTS, prosody modeling, and controllability, highlighting key contributions and limitations.

Early TTS systems were based on concatenative synthesis, where pre-recorded speech segments were stitched together \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. However, these systems lacked flexibility in prosody control and were limited by the quality of the speech corpus \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. With the advent of neural TTS, models like Tacotron \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\] and FastSpeech \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] have enabled end-to-end synthesis from text, producing high-quality speech. These models typically use recurrent or transformer-based architectures to generate mel-spectrograms, which are then decoded into audio using vocoders like WaveNet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\].

Prosody control in TTS has been a major focus of recent research. Several works have introduced methods for fine-grained prosody control, such as prosody embeddings \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\] and style tokens \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. These methods allow for the modulation of pitch, duration, and energy, enabling expressive speech synthesis. However, many of these approaches require additional training or explicit prosody annotations, which limits their applicability in real-world scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. For instance, the work by \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\] introduces a hierarchical prosody modeling framework that conditions the TTS model on both coarse and fine-grained acoustic features, enabling more intuitive control over prosody dimensions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\].

Non-autoregressive TTS models, such as FastSpeech \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] and Parallel Tacotron \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\], have also gained popularity due to their parallelizable architecture and faster inference speeds. These models often incorporate duration prediction and knowledge distillation to improve the quality of synthesized speech \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. However, they still face challenges in achieving diverse and natural prosody variations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\].

### <a id='category-prosody-control-techniques'></a>Prosody Control Techniques: 9 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Many existing approaches rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. For instance, some models condition on acoustic speech features to learn a latent prosody space, enabling control over dimensions like pitch, duration, and energy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Others introduce prosody embeddings that allow for fine-grained control of speaking style, either through temporal structures on the speech or text side \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. These embeddings can be trained without explicit supervision, allowing for adjustments in pitch and amplitude at both frame and phoneme levels \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. Additionally, some studies focus on improving prosody for multi-sentence inputs by leveraging long context, powerful text features, and multi-speaker data, which enhance prosody realization and coherence \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. Others propose fully automatic methods to make TTS systems aware of prosody, enabling sentence-wise control over speaking pace and expressiveness \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. These techniques often incorporate novel attention mechanisms or perceptual losses to improve sensitivity and convergence during training \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Furthermore, some approaches decouple reference signal alignment from the overall system, using phoneme-level time stamps and variational auto-encoders to enhance latent prosody representations \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02479)\]. These methods collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve prosody control and expressiveness in TTS systems without requiring extensive retraining or explicit prosody annotations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02479)\].

### <a id='category-neural-tts-architectures'></a>Neural TTS Architectures: 18 papers found.
Recent advances in text-to-speech (TTS) have significantly improved speech naturalness, with models like FastSpeech and its variants addressing the one-to-many mapping problem by introducing parallel training and more accurate prosody modeling \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. These models rely on conditioning on prosody features such as pitch, energy, and duration to enhance the expressiveness and quality of synthesized speech \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. However, challenges remain in accurately capturing and modeling these prosody attributes, particularly due to errors in extracted pitch and the interdependence of prosody attributes like pitch, duration, and energy \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\]. To tackle these issues, researchers have proposed methods such as ProsoSpeech, which uses quantized latent vectors pre-trained on large-scale data to enhance prosody modeling \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07816)\], and hierarchical architectures that condition phoneme-level prosody on word-level features to improve naturalness and audio quality \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Additionally, models like FastPitch and Glow-TTS have been developed to generate speech in parallel, improving efficiency and allowing for more natural and diverse speech synthesis \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to improve prosody modeling and control in TTS systems, enabling more expressive and controllable speech synthesis.
