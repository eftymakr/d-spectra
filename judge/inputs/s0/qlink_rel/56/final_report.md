## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Early approaches primarily focused on transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain using matrix factorization or neural networks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. However, these methods often assume fully overlapping users and fail to account for the complexities of real-world scenarios where user interactions are sparse and non-overlapping \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. 

Recent works have attempted to address these limitations by incorporating more sophisticated modeling techniques. For instance, C2DSR \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\] proposes a framework that simultaneously captures intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to learn both single- and cross-domain user preferences. This approach improves upon traditional methods by explicitly modeling the interactions between items across different domains. Similarly, DASL \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\] introduces a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between two domains, enhancing cross-domain CTR prediction performance. 

Another line of research focuses on modeling the latent relationships between users and items across domains. DDTCDR \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] employs a dual transfer mechanism to extract user preferences while preserving the relationships between users across different latent spaces. This model is particularly effective in scenarios with limited overlapping users. Additionally, DisenCDR \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa36b8d483b06e0bce366970fde747761151f411)\] disentangles domain-shared and domain-specific information to improve the accuracy of cross-domain recommendations. This approach addresses the issue of domain-specific preferences potentially harming the target domain performance.

The problem of data sparsity and cold-start users has also been addressed through semi-supervised learning techniques. SSCDR \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f3b7672aaab5b65e1eb66aee6f3a5b95fb0c304f)\] introduces a framework that leverages both overlapping users and item information to infer latent representations for cold-start users. This method is particularly useful in scenarios where labeled data is scarce. 

Furthermore, the use of graph-based models has shown promise in capturing complex interactions between users and items. DA-GCN \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\] proposes a domain-aware graph convolution network to learn user-specific node representations, allowing for more accurate cross-domain recommendations. This model effectively captures the relationships between users and items across different domains.

In addition to these approaches, some works have focused on modeling the flow of information across domains. Mixed information flow networks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\] consider both behavioral and knowledge transfer to enrich sequence representations, improving recommendation performance across domains. Similarly, DDGHM \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\] introduces a dual dynamic graph modeling approach to capture intra- and inter-domain sequential transitions, addressing the challenges of data sparsity and model adaptability.

### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation-frameworks'></a>Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation Frameworks: 7 papers found.
Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research direction to address data sparsity and cold-start issues by leveraging user preferences across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing methods often rely on overlapping users to propagate cross-domain information, but they typically assume fully overlapping users and unchanged data distribution, which limits their performance in real-world scenarios where data distribution shifts are common \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. To overcome these limitations, recent approaches have focused on open-world assumptions, designing adaptive frameworks that can handle non-overlapping users and dynamic data distributions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. For example, the Adaptive Multi-Interest Debiasing (AMID) framework introduces a multi-interest information module and a doubly robust estimator to improve cross-domain recommendation performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Similarly, the Dual Attentive Sequential Learning (DASL) model employs a dual learning mechanism to iteratively transfer information between domains, enhancing cross-domain click-through rate (CTR) predictions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Another approach, DDGHM, addresses the challenges of CDSR by incorporating dual dynamic graph modeling and hybrid metric training to capture intra- and inter-domain sequential transitions while alleviating data sparsity \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Additionally, the Shared-account Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (SCSR) task has been explored, where models like π-Net and PSJNet address the challenges of shared accounts and cross-domain behavior by using parallel architectures to generate recommendations across domains \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. These works highlight the ongoing efforts to improve cross-domain sequential recommendation by integrating advanced modeling techniques and addressing practical limitations such as data distribution shifts and shared account scenarios.

### <a id='category-domain-transfer-and-knowledge-sharing-methods'></a>Domain Transfer and Knowledge Sharing Methods: 6 papers found.
Cross-domain recommendation (CDR) has been widely studied to address data sparsity and improve recommendation accuracy by leveraging information from multiple domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Early approaches primarily focused on transferring knowledge between domains using matrix factorization or simple transfer learning techniques, but they often failed to capture complex user-item interactions or bidirectional latent relations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. More recent methods, such as Deep Dual Transfer Cross Domain Recommendation (DDTCDR), have introduced deep learning frameworks that enable dual knowledge transfer and preserve user preferences across domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These models often incorporate autoencoders or neural networks to extract latent features and improve the representation of user and item interactions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Additionally, methods like Collaborative Cross Networks (CoNet) have been proposed to enable dual knowledge transfer by introducing cross connections between base networks, allowing for more efficient training and better performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Another approach, Dual-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation (DTCDR), extends the idea by improving recommendation performance on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously by utilizing multi-source content information and multi-task learning strategies \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in leveraging deep learning and transfer learning techniques to enhance cross-domain recommendation systems.

	Document 2: [Snippet]: «Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated their significant potential to be applied for addressing various application tasks. However, traditional recommender systems continue to face great challenges such as poor interactivity and explainability, which actually also hinder their broad deployment in real-world systems. To address these limitations, this paper proposes a novel paradigm called Chat-Rec (ChatGPT Augmented Recommender System) that innovatively augments LLMs for building conversational recommender systems by converting user profiles and historical interactions into prompts. Chat-Rec is demonstrated to be effective in learning user preferences and establishing connections between users and products through in-context learning, which also makes the recommendation process more interactive and explainable. What's more, within the Chat-Rec framework, user's preferences can transfer to different products for cross-domain recommendations, and prompt-based injection of information into LLMs can also handle the cold-start scenarios with new items. In our experiments, Chat-Rec effectively improve the results of top-k recommendations and performs better in zero-shot rating prediction task.
