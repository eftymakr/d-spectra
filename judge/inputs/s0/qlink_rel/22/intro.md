## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has attracted significant attention due to the high cost of pixel-level annotations in real-world scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Traditional UDA methods assume that the category settings between source and target domains are known, which is often not the case in practical applications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes exist in the target domain \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. To address this limitation, several approaches have been proposed to improve domain adaptation without prior knowledge of category settings.

One line of research focuses on improving the alignment between source and target domains by leveraging adversarial learning. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] introduces a method that aligns the marginal distribution in the feature space through adversarial learning. However, this global alignment strategy does not consider the local category-level feature distribution, which can lead to misalignment of well-aligned categories \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. To address this, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\] introduces a category-level adversarial network that enforces local semantic consistency during global alignment. This approach improves the alignment of categories by adjusting the adversarial loss based on category-level alignment \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\].

Another approach, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\], proposes a curriculum-style learning method to minimize the domain gap by first solving easy tasks and then inferring properties about the target domain. This method is particularly effective for urban scenes, where strong idiosyncrasies exist in the spatial layout of objects \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\]. Similarly, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10349)\] introduces a two-step self-supervised domain adaptation approach that minimizes both inter-domain and intra-domain gaps. This method separates the target domain into easy and hard splits and then uses self-supervised adaptation from the easy to the hard subdomain \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\].

In addition to adversarial learning, some works focus on improving pseudo-label quality. \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\] proposes a method that estimates prediction uncertainty during training to rectify pseudo label learning, which helps improve the robustness of the adaptation process. This approach dynamically sets confidence thresholds based on prediction variance and rectifies learning from noisy pseudo labels \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02649)\]. Similarly, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03773)\] introduces a framework that uses self-ensembling with GAN-based data augmentation to enhance the performance of the segmentation network on the target domain \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\].

Recent methods also explore the use of depth information to improve domain adaptation. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08040)\] proposes a depth-aware domain adaptation framework that leverages dense depth in the source domain to improve the performance of the target domain. This method achieves state-of-the-art results on synthetic-to-real benchmarks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.03213)\].
