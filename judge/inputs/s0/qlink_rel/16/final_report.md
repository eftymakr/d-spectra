## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling detectors to recognize objects from a vocabulary specified at test time, often in natural language \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. This capability is particularly valuable for applications requiring flexible and dynamic object recognition, such as robotics and augmented reality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]. However, the performance of these detectors can be significantly impacted by the quality and relevance of the user-defined vocabulary \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\]. Prior work has explored various approaches to improve detection accuracy by leveraging pre-trained models, weak supervision, and language-visual alignment \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\].

Several studies have focused on improving open-vocabulary detection by using vision-language pre-training models like CLIP and ALIGN \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14940)\]. These models enable zero-shot transfer by aligning visual and textual representations, allowing detectors to recognize objects without explicit bounding box annotations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11876)\]. For instance, ViLD leverages vision-language knowledge distillation to train a two-stage detector, achieving competitive performance on benchmark datasets like LVIS and COCO \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\]. Similarly, RegionCLIP extends CLIP to learn region-level visual representations, enabling fine-grained alignment between image regions and textual concepts \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\].

Another line of research has explored the use of pseudo-labeling and self-training frameworks to generate bounding box annotations from large-scale image-caption pairs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. This approach reduces the reliance on manual annotations and enables the detection of novel object categories \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. For example, the PB-OVD method generates pseudo bounding-box labels from image-caption pairs, leading to improved performance on datasets like COCO and LVIS \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16513)\]. Similarly, the Detic method trains detectors on image classification data, expanding their vocabulary to tens of thousands of concepts without requiring complex assignment schemes \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency and scalability of open-vocabulary detection by simplifying training pipelines and reducing the need for extensive annotations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12763)\]. For instance, F-VLM demonstrates that frozen vision-language models can be used directly for detection, achieving significant improvements in mask AP on novel categories of the LVIS benchmark \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\]. ### <a id='category-open-vocabulary-object-detection-overview'></a>Open-vocabulary object detection overview: 2 papers found.
Open-vocabulary object detection has emerged as a critical area of research to address the limitations of traditional supervised methods, which are constrained by the vocabulary size of detection datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. Unlike image classifiers that can handle large vocabularies due to the ease of collecting labeled data, object detectors have been limited to smaller sets of categories, often requiring extensive bounding box annotations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. To overcome this, researchers have explored weakly supervised and zero-shot learning techniques, which aim to reduce the need for manual annotations but have not achieved the same level of performance as fully supervised models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. More recently, methods like Detic have been proposed to expand the vocabulary of detectors by training classifiers on image classification data, enabling detection of tens of thousands of object categories without requiring box annotations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. This approach simplifies implementation and improves performance for both seen and novel classes, as demonstrated by significant gains in mean average precision (mAP) on benchmarks like LVIS \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in open-vocabulary object detection as a more practical and scalable solution for real-world applications.

### <a id='category-zero-shot-transfer-and-language-visual-alignment'></a>Zero-shot transfer and language-visual alignment: 10 papers found.
The category "Zero-shot transfer and language-visual alignment" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at enabling open-vocabulary object detection by leveraging language-visual alignment and zero-shot transfer capabilities. These methods typically rely on pre-trained vision-language models to align textual descriptions with visual features, allowing detectors to generalize to unseen categories without explicit training on those classes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\]. For instance, ViLD uses vision-language knowledge distillation to align region embeddings of detected boxes with text and image embeddings from a teacher model, achieving strong performance on benchmarks like LVIS and COCO \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]. Similarly, OV-DETR extends DETR to support open-vocabulary detection by formulating the learning objective as a binary matching task between input queries and objects, enabling detection based on class names or exemplar images \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11876)\]. CLIP-based pre-training has also been used to learn robust image representations that enable zero-shot transfer to various downstream tasks, such as object detection, by aligning natural language with visual concepts \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]. More recently, methods like PromptDet and VLDet have further advanced this field by introducing techniques such as regional prompt learning and set matching to improve alignment between image regions and language embeddings \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16513)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. Additionally, HierKD proposes a hierarchical knowledge distillation approach to enhance one-stage detectors by transferring knowledge from pre-trained visual-language models to unseen categories \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10593)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the importance of language-visual alignment and zero-shot transfer in expanding the capabilities of object detectors to handle novel and diverse visual concepts.

These methods aim to reduce the reliance on extensive human-annotated data by leveraging pre-trained vision-language models to generate pseudo bounding-box or segmentation masks from large-scale image-caption pairs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. This approach allows models to generalize to novel object categories without requiring explicit annotations for those categories during training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. For instance, one method uses vision-language models to categorize regions in images into object classes, effectively generating pseudo labels for both open-vocabulary detection and semi-supervised learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. Another framework aligns word semantics from captions with visual features to create pseudo masks for instance segmentation, improving performance on datasets like MS-COCO and Open Images \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12698)\]. These pseudo-labeling strategies are crucial for expanding the base categories used in training, thereby improving the generalization ability of object detectors to unseen classes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. The effectiveness of these methods is demonstrated through significant improvements in metrics such as AP on benchmark datasets like COCO, PASCAL VOC, and LVIS \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. The generated pseudo labels are often refined through robust student models that mitigate noise and enhance the quality of self-training \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12698)\]. Overall, these methods represent a promising direction for reducing annotation costs while maintaining detection accuracy in complex and diverse visual tasks.
