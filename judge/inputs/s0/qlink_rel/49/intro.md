## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have shown significant promise in web search tasks, particularly due to their ability to capture complex semantic relationships between queries and documents \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. However, training these models typically requires large amounts of labeled query-document pairs, which are expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Recent advances have leveraged large language models to generate synthetic queries, offering a scalable alternative to manual labeling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal performance in downstream retrieval tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Existing approaches typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This method, while effective, introduces additional complexity and computational overhead. In contrast, our work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO is a novel parameterization of the reward model in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach eliminates the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning and simplifies the training process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Several studies have explored the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align language models with human preferences \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. For example, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\] applies preference modeling and RLHF to fine-tune language models to act as helpful and harmless assistants, improving performance across a range of NLP tasks. Similarly, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741)\] explores the use of human preferences to define goals for reinforcement learning systems, demonstrating that this approach can solve complex tasks without explicit reward functions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741)\]. These works highlight the potential of RLHF to improve model behavior by aligning it with human preferences \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\].

In the context of retrieval, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\] introduces Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Learning (ANCE), which constructs negative training instances from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index of the corpus. This approach improves the alignment between training and testing data distributions, leading to better performance in dense retrieval tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Similarly, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)\] presents a novel design for $k$-selection that enables efficient similarity search on large-scale datasets, demonstrating significant improvements in speed and accuracy \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734)\].

The use of deep neural networks for ad-hoc retrieval has also been explored in several works. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08611)\] proposes a novel deep relevance matching model (DRMM) that addresses the challenges of relevance matching in ad-hoc retrieval by incorporating query term importance and exact matching signals \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08611)\].
