## Related Works

The growing use of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in how they handle biases and uncertainties \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, which may not fully capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. This limitation has led to the development of more nuanced fairness measures that account for model confidence and decision-making bias \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. 

Several studies have explored fairness in machine learning, particularly in contexts where decisions have legal or ethical implications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. For instance, the concept of counterfactual fairness has been introduced to ensure that decisions remain consistent across different demographic groups \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This idea aligns with the broader goal of preventing discriminatory outcomes in automated decision systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. 

The importance of fairness in automated systems has also been highlighted in the context of hiring, lending, and predictive policing, where biased data can perpetuate existing social inequities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed frameworks that integrate causal inference to model fairness more accurately \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. These approaches aim to ensure that decisions are not only accurate but also equitable across different subgroups \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. 

A key challenge in fairness evaluation is the reliance on datasets that may lack diversity or clarity, which can limit the generalizability of fairness assessments \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. To address this, the paper introduces a new gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset with 31,756 samples, designed to better reflect real-world scenarios and improve the evaluation of modern LLMs \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. This dataset is crucial for benchmarking fairness metrics that consider both accuracy and model confidence \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\].

The proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metric, UCerF, builds upon earlier work that emphasized the need for fairness measures to account for model confidence and decision uncertainty \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. This approach is similar to methods that have been used in other domains, such as recidivism prediction, where fairness criteria have been shown to be incompatible in certain cases \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\]. 

In addition to fairness, the paper also highlights the importance of transparency and accountability in AI systems \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. This aligns with broader discussions on the ethical implications of algorithmic decision-making, which have drawn on philosophical concepts of justice and equality \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. 

The work also touches on the broader challenge of balancing fairness with utility, a tension that has been extensively studied in the context of classification and decision-making systems \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\].
