## Related Works

Neural retrieval methods using transformer-based pre-trained language models have significantly advanced multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. However, their effectiveness for low-resource, morphologically rich languages such as Amharic remains underexplored due to data scarcity and suboptimal tokenization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This challenge is not unique to Amharic; similar issues have been reported for other low-resource languages, where the lack of sufficient training data and inadequate language-specific tokenization strategies hinder model performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04085)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of multilingual pre-trained models for cross-lingual retrieval. For instance, XLM-R has demonstrated superior performance over multilingual BERT on a variety of cross-lingual benchmarks, including +14.6% average accuracy on XNLI \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. Similarly, ColBERT has been shown to be effective for passage retrieval, leveraging late interaction between query and document representations to improve retrieval accuracy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. These models have been evaluated on datasets such as MS MARCO and TREC-CAR, achieving state-of-the-art results \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04085)\]. 

The importance of language-specific adaptation has also been emphasized in the literature. For example, CamemBERT, a French language model, has shown that training on web-crawled data can lead to better performance than using Wikipedia data, even with smaller datasets \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.03894)\]. This aligns with the findings of the current work, which highlights the necessity of language-specific adaptation for low-resource languages like Amharic \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. 

Efforts to improve cross-lingual retrieval for low-resource languages have also included the development of specialized models. For example, ColBERT-X uses the XLM-RoBERTa encoder to support cross-language information retrieval, demonstrating significant improvements over traditional lexical CLIR baselines \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\]. Additionally, the use of dense retrieval models has been shown to outperform sparse vector space models in open-domain question answering tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. 

The current work builds on these advancements by introducing Amharic-specific dense retrieval models based on pre-trained Amharic BERT and RoBERTa backbones. These models achieve notable improvements over existing multilingual baselines, such as Arctic Embed 2.0, demonstrating the potential of language-specific adaptation in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Furthermore, the work highlights the importance of compact model variants, such as RoBERTa-Medium-Amharic-Embed, which remain competitive while being significantly smaller in size \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\].

The category "Cross Lingual Information Retrieval" encompasses a range of efforts aimed at improving information retrieval across multiple languages, particularly focusing on low-resource languages. Several studies have explored the use of multilingual pre-trained models like XLM-R \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\] and multilingual BERT \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00052)\] to enable cross-lingual transfer, with XLM-R showing significant improvements over mBERT in cross-lingual benchmarks such as XNLI and MLQA \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. Additionally, models like ColBERT-X \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\] have been developed to support cross-language information retrieval by leveraging XLM-R encoders, demonstrating substantial improvements in ad hoc document ranking tasks across multiple languages. The challenge of data scarcity in low-resource languages has also been addressed through datasets like mMARCO \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13897)\], which provides a multilingual version of the MS MARCO dataset, and AfriCLIRMatrix \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf387294994faf86b3c8332ba72fc17b1442651c)\], which includes 15 African languages. Furthermore, methods such as OPTICAL \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12566)\] have been introduced to mitigate language bias in cross-lingual retrieval by using optimal transport distillation to transfer knowledge from high-resource to low-resource languages. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve cross-lingual retrieval, particularly for underrepresented languages, through better data, model architectures, and training strategies.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Recent progress in Natural Language Understanding (NLU) is driving fast-paced advances in Information Retrieval (IR), largely owed to fine-tuning deep language models (LMs) for document ranking. While remarkably effective, the ranking models based on these LMs increase computational cost by orders of magnitude over prior approaches, particularly as they must feed each query-document pair through a massive neural network to compute a single relevance score. To tackle this, we present ColBERT, a novel ranking model that adapts deep LMs (in particular, BERT) for efficient retrieval. ColBERT introduces a late interaction architecture that independently encodes the query and the document using BERT and then employs a cheap yet powerful interaction step that models their fine-grained similarity. By delaying and yet retaining this fine-granular interaction, ColBERT can leverage the expressiveness of deep LMs while simultaneously gaining the ability to pre-compute document representations offline, considerably speeding up query processing. Crucially, ColBERT's pruning-friendly interaction mechanism enables leveraging vector-similarity indexes for end-to-end retrieval directly from millions of documents. We extensively evaluate ColBERT using two recent passage search datasets. Results show that ColBERT's effectiveness is competitive with existing BERT-based models (and outperforms every non-BERT baseline), while executing two orders-of-magnitude faster and requiring up to four orders-of-magnitude fewer FLOPs per query.»
