## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit behaviors such as refusal, alignment, and steering, often through mechanisms embedded in their activation space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Traditional approaches to identifying refusal behavior rely on predefined templates detectable in output tokens or require manual analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. However, these methods are limited in their generalizability and often depend on assumptions about the presence of specific refusal tokens \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Recent work has focused on understanding the internal mechanisms of LLMs, particularly the one-dimensional subspace that mediates refusal behavior across models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. This subspace is critical for understanding how models decide to refuse or comply with instructions, and it has been shown to be a key factor in the brittleness of current safety fine-tuning methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\].

In addition to refusal, researchers have explored methods to steer LLMs toward desired behaviors. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) is an innovative approach that modifies model activations during inference to steer behavior, such as increasing factual accuracy or reducing hallucination \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. CAA computes steering vectors by averaging the difference in residual stream activations between positive and negative examples of a behavior, and then adds these vectors during inference to alter model outputs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. This method has been shown to be effective across a range of tasks and is minimally invasive, with little impact on model capabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\].

Another approach to improving truthfulness in LLMs is Inference-Time Intervention (ITI), which shifts model activations during inference to enhance truthfulness. ITI has been shown to significantly improve the performance of LLaMA models on the TruthfulQA benchmark, increasing truthfulness from 32.5% to 65.1% \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\]. This technique operates by modifying a limited number of attention heads during inference, demonstrating that even small interventions can have a substantial impact on model behavior \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\].

Adversarial attacks on aligned LLMs have also been a focus of recent research. The Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks paper introduces a method that automatically generates adversarial suffixes to induce objectionable content in aligned models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. These attacks are transferable across different models and even to black-box systems, highlighting the vulnerabilities in current alignment techniques \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\].

Furthermore, the development of tools like Toolformer has enabled LLMs to use external APIs to perform complex tasks, improving their zero-shot performance without sacrificing core language modeling abilities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. ### <a id='category-adversarial-attacks-and-interpretability'></a>Adversarial Attacks And Interpretability: 3 papers found.
The category "Adversarial Attacks And Interpretability" encompasses research focused on both manipulating language models to produce undesirable outputs and understanding the internal mechanisms that govern their behavior. Adversarial attacks, such as those that generate prompts to induce objectionable content, highlight vulnerabilities in aligned models and challenge the robustness of alignment techniques \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. These attacks often rely on automated methods, such as greedy or gradient-based search, to construct adversarial suffixes that can transfer across different models and even black-box systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. On the interpretability side, researchers have explored the phenomenon of polysemanticity, where neurons activate across multiple semantically distinct contexts, complicating efforts to understand model behavior \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08600)\]. Techniques like sparse autoencoders have been proposed to decompose activation spaces into more interpretable, monosemantic features, offering insights into how models represent and process information \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08600)\]. These approaches underscore the importance of understanding activation spaces to improve model steerability and transparency, as demonstrated by methods like Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA), which modifies activations during inference to steer model behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. Together, these works illustrate the growing interest in both attacking and interpreting large language models, with implications for safety, alignment, and model design.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Language models (LMs) exhibit remarkable abilities to solve new tasks from just a few examples or textual instructions, especially at scale. They also, paradoxically, struggle with basic functionality, such as arithmetic or factual lookup, where much simpler and smaller models excel. In this paper, we show that LMs can teach themselves to use external tools via simple APIs and achieve the best of both worlds. We introduce Toolformer, a model trained to decide which APIs to call, when to call them, what arguments to pass, and how to best incorporate the results into future token prediction. This is done in a self-supervised way, requiring nothing more than a handful of demonstrations for each API. We incorporate a range of tools, including a calculator, a Q\&A system, two different search engines, a translation system, and a calendar. Toolformer achieves substantially improved zero-shot performance across a variety of downstream tasks, often competitive with much larger models, without sacrificing its core language modeling abilities.»

### <a id='category-language-model-ethics-and-capabilities'></a>Language Model Ethics and Capabilities: 2 papers found.
The category "Language Model Ethics and Capabilities" encompasses a broad range of research focused on both the ethical implications and functional capabilities of large language models (LLMs). This includes studies on the risks associated with LMs, such as discrimination, misinformation, and environmental harm, as well as advancements in their ability to perform complex reasoning and decision-making tasks. Research highlights the need for responsible innovation, emphasizing the importance of understanding and mitigating risks like toxic language, unfair bias, and the potential for malicious use \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04359)\]. Concurrently, there is growing interest in enhancing LMs' capabilities through techniques like interleaved reasoning and action generation, which improve task-solving efficiency and human interpretability \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. These two strands of research—ethical risks and functional capabilities—underscore the dual challenge of developing LMs that are both powerful and socially responsible.
