## Related Works

Deep learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for solving inverse problems in imaging, with two prominent approaches being deep-unrolling and plug-and-play (PnP) methods. PnP approaches, which integrate off-the-shelf deep denoisers into iterative algorithms, offer flexibility in handling varying compression ratios (CRs) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. However, they often suffer from limitations in reconstruction accuracy and speed \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. On the other hand, unrolling methods transform truncated iterative optimization processes into end-to-end trainable networks, typically achieving better accuracy and faster inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. Nevertheless, these methods often require retraining when CR changes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. 

The integration of PnP and unrolling approaches has been explored in several works. For instance, the use of denoising networks as implicit image priors in variational methods has been studied, enabling the replacement of proximal operators with neural networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10557)\]. This approach allows for the use of fixed denoisers across different inverse problems, reducing the need for problem-specific training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10557)\]. Similarly, algorithm unrolling has been used to connect iterative algorithms like sparse coding to neural network architectures, offering interpretable and efficient deep learning models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. This technique has been further extended to image deblurring, where an iterative algorithm is unrolled to construct a neural network \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05399)\].

The concept of unrolling has also been applied to other imaging tasks, such as image super-resolution and compressive sensing. For example, the Learned Primal-Dual algorithm unrolls a proximal primal-dual optimization method, replacing proximal operators with convolutional neural networks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03264)\]. This approach is trained end-to-end and does not rely on any initial reconstruction, making it suitable for low-dose computed tomography \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03264)\]. Similarly, ISTA-Net, inspired by the Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA), has been proposed for compressive sensing (CS) reconstruction, combining the structure insights of traditional optimization-based methods with the speed of network-based approaches \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02862)\].

In addition to unrolling, the use of deep denoisers as priors in model-based optimization methods has been investigated. For example, the TNRD (Trainable Nonlinear Reaction Diffusion) framework learns all parameters, including filters and influence functions, from training data, leading to state-of-the-art performance in image restoration tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03679)\]. ### <a id='category-image-restoration-techniques'></a>Image Restoration Techniques: 4 papers found.
Image restoration techniques have seen significant advancements with the integration of deep learning methods, particularly through approaches like deep-unrolling and plug-and-play (PnP) frameworks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. These methods leverage neural networks to address inverse problems in imaging, such as denoising, super-resolution, and deblocking, by either implicitly incorporating regularization via pre-trained denoisers or explicitly unrolling iterative optimization processes into neural network architectures \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. While PnP methods offer flexibility across varying compression ratios, they often suffer from limited accuracy and speed \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. In contrast, unrolling approaches, which transform truncated iterative algorithms into end-to-end trainable networks, generally achieve higher accuracy and faster inference but require retraining when the compression ratio changes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. Recent work has focused on bridging these gaps by designing efficient deep image restorers that can handle varying conditions without retraining, such as the proposed proximal unrolling networks that approximate proximal operators of explicit restoration regularizers \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. Additionally, neural network architectures inspired by iterative algorithms, such as those based on total-variation regularization and half-quadratic splitting, have been developed to improve interpretability and performance \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05399)\]. These approaches often incorporate multi-resolution decomposition and residual learning to enhance reconstruction quality while preserving structural details \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03679)\]. The integration of physical models with deep learning, such as through direct inversion followed by convolutional neural networks, has also shown promise in solving ill-posed inverse problems with reduced computational costs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03679)\]. Overall, the field continues to evolve with a focus on balancing flexibility, accuracy, and efficiency in image restoration tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05399)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03679)\].
