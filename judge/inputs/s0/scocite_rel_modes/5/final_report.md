## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding is a critical area of research in natural language processing, aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions. This task has attracted significant attention due to its applicability in various downstream tasks such as semantic similarity, information retrieval, and question answering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06726)\]. However, existing approaches often suffer from significant computational overhead, as they require re-encoding the entire corpus for each new instruction \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more efficient methods that can adapt pre-computed embeddings in real time \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\].

Several prior works have explored the use of pre-trained language models for generating text embeddings. For instance, BERT \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] and RoBERTa \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\] have demonstrated the effectiveness of deep bidirectional transformers in capturing contextual information. These models are pre-trained on large corpora and can be fine-tuned for specific tasks. However, they typically require retraining for each new instruction, which is computationally expensive \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.08877)\].

The concept of instruction-aware embeddings has also been explored in recent studies. For example, SimCSE \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07280)\] introduces a contrastive learning framework that generates sentence embeddings by contrasting positive and negative examples. This method has shown promising results in semantic textual similarity tasks. Similarly, INSTRUCTOR \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\] proposes a method for computing text embeddings given task instructions, enabling a single embedder to generate embeddings tailored to different downstream tasks without additional training. This approach is particularly useful for tasks that require domain-specific knowledge \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving the efficiency of text embedding generation. For example, the E5 model \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\] is trained using weakly-supervised contrastive pre-training and achieves strong performance on a wide range of tasks, including retrieval and classification. This model demonstrates the potential of contrastive learning in generating high-quality embeddings with minimal computational overhead \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4546)\].

Additionally, the use of transformer-based models for text embedding has gained traction. For instance, Sentence-T5 \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02724)\] explores the use of pre-trained text-to-text models for generating sentence embeddings, achieving state-of-the-art results on semantic textual similarity tasks. This work highlights the potential of transformer-based models in generating high-quality embeddings while maintaining efficiency \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\].

In summary, while existing methods have made significant strides in generating instruction-aware text embeddings, they often suffer from computational inefficiencies. The proposed GSTransform framework addresses these limitations by introducing a lightweight transformation mechanism that adapts pre-computed embeddings in real time, guided by a small amount of instruction-focused label annotation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03533)\].

### <a id='category-transformer-based-language-models'></a>Transformer Based Language Models: 7 papers found.
The category "Transformer Based Language Models" encompasses a range of advancements in natural language processing, focusing on models that leverage attention mechanisms for improved performance and efficiency. The Transformer architecture, introduced as a replacement for recurrent and convolutional neural networks, has demonstrated superior results in machine translation tasks, achieving high BLEU scores on benchmarks like WMT 2014 English-to-German and English-to-French \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06726)\]. This foundational work laid the groundwork for subsequent models that build upon the Transformer's capabilities. Transfer learning, where models are pre-trained on large datasets and then fine-tuned for specific tasks, has further enhanced performance, with frameworks like the unified text-to-text approach enabling state-of-the-art results across multiple NLP tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. The BERT model, a bidirectional Transformer-based approach, has been pivotal in pre-training deep bidirectional representations, achieving new benchmarks in tasks such as question answering and language inference \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Additionally, models like SimCSE have advanced sentence embeddings through contrastive learning, achieving strong results with both unsupervised and supervised approaches \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\]. The LXMERT framework extends the Transformer's utility to vision-and-language tasks, demonstrating cross-modal understanding through pre-training on diverse tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. Furthermore, studies have highlighted the importance of careful hyperparameter tuning and training data size in achieving optimal results with models like BERT, showing that undertraining can be a significant limitation \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. These developments collectively underscore the transformative impact of Transformer-based models on the landscape of language processing, emphasizing their adaptability and effectiveness across a wide array of tasks.

### <a id='category-vision-and-language-navigation'></a>Vision and Language Navigation: 3 papers found.
The category "Vision and Language Navigation" explores the challenges and advancements in enabling robots and agents to interpret and follow natural-language navigation instructions using vision and language understanding. Recent research has focused on developing systems that can perform visually grounded sequence-to-sequence translation, where a robot interprets a natural-language instruction based on its visual input \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07280)\]. This task is closely related to Visual Question Answering, as both involve understanding and reasoning about visual scenes using language \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07280)\]. One key challenge is the lack of detailed low-level motor instructions in natural language, requiring the system to infer missing information from perceptual context \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02724)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed approaches that incorporate embedded speaker models to synthesize new instructions for data augmentation and implement pragmatic reasoning to evaluate candidate action sequences \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02724)\]. Additionally, systems have been developed that learn to transform natural-language navigation instructions into executable formal plans by observing how humans follow instructions, using learned lexicons and semantic parsers to refine inferred plans \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4b17662f21c6313907a022af20f88616d11620eb)\]. These methods highlight the importance of combining vision, language, and reinforcement learning to improve the accuracy and efficiency of vision-and-language navigation tasks. The development of large-scale simulation environments, such as the Matter-port3D Simulator, has also played a crucial role in enabling benchmarking and evaluation of these approaches \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07280)\].
