## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, owing to their ability to model complex interactions between users and items \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Early GCN implementations, such as those in \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\], focused on simplifying the model by removing nonlinearities and collapsing weight matrices, leading to linear models that are easier to train and interpret. However, these simplifications often come at the cost of reduced expressiveness, as demonstrated by the need for deeper layers to capture non-linear interactions in recommendation tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. 

The integration of user-item interactions into the embedding process has been a key advancement. Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] was one of the first to explicitly model the bipartite graph structure by propagating embeddings through the graph, thereby capturing high-order connectivity and collaborative signals. This approach was further refined in LightGCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which simplified the model by removing feature transformations and nonlinear activations, resulting in a more efficient and effective framework. However, LightGCN still faces challenges in handling large-scale graphs due to training difficulties and over-smoothing effects \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\]. 

To address these issues, UltraGCN \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\] was proposed, which further simplifies GCNs by skipping infinite layers of message passing and instead approximating the limit of infinite-layer graph convolutions via a constraint loss. This approach not only improves efficiency but also allows for more flexible edge weight assignments and relationship modeling. 

In addition to GCN-based methods, other approaches have explored the use of graph attention mechanisms to better capture fine-grained interactions. Graph Attention Networks (GATs) \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\] introduced masked self-attentional layers that allow nodes to weigh their neighbors differently without requiring costly matrix operations. This has been particularly useful in social recommendation, where heterogeneous relationships and user-item interactions need to be modeled effectively \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07243)\]. 

The use of knowledge graphs (KGs) has also gained traction in recommendation systems. Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\] explicitly models high-order relations in KGs, which are crucial for capturing complex user-item interactions. This approach has shown significant improvements over traditional methods like Neural FM and RippleNet \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. 

Furthermore, self-supervised learning has been explored to improve the robustness and accuracy of GCNs in recommendation tasks. Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL) \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\] introduces auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning by generating multiple views of a node and maximizing agreement between different views.

### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Based Collaborative Filtering: 10 papers found.
Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have been increasingly adopted in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, with models like LightGCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], NGCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\], and UltraGCN \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\] demonstrating significant improvements over traditional methods. These models leverage the user-item interaction graph to propagate embeddings and capture high-order connectivity, enhancing the modeling of collaborative signals. However, existing GCN-based approaches often incorporate non-linear activation functions and feature transformations, which can complicate training and degrade performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. For instance, LightGCN simplifies GCN by retaining only neighborhood aggregation, leading to better performance and easier training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, UltraGCN further simplifies the model by approximating infinite-layer graph convolutions through a constraint loss, achieving faster training and better performance \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\]. Meanwhile, NGCF explicitly propagates embeddings on the user-item graph to inject collaborative signals, showing improvements over earlier models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Despite these advancements, challenges remain, such as the over-smoothing problem and the difficulty of training with non-linear activations on large-scale graphs \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Recent work has also explored integrating user intents and social influence into the embedding process, such as DGCF \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\] and the deep influence propagation model \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10322)\], which aim to disentangle user intents and model recursive social diffusion. These developments highlight ongoing efforts to refine GCN-based CF models by addressing limitations in embedding capacity, neighborhood aggregation, and training efficiency.

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-for-recommendations'></a>Graph Neural Networks for Recommendations: 6 papers found.
Recent advancements in graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly impacted recommendation systems, particularly in modeling complex user-item interactions and leveraging graph structures for better personalization \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]. Traditional approaches, such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), have been widely adopted for their ability to capture local graph structures and node features, yet they often face challenges in scalability and efficiency \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\]. For instance, GCNs typically require all nodes to be present during training, limiting their ability to generalize to unseen data \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed variants like GraphSAGE, which introduces an inductive framework that generates embeddings for previously unseen nodes by aggregating features from a node's local neighborhood \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Additionally, attention mechanisms have been integrated into GNNs, leading to the development of Graph Attention Networks (GATs), which enable models to assign different weights to different nodes in a neighborhood without requiring costly matrix operations \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]. These innovations highlight the evolving landscape of GNNs in recommendation systems, where models are increasingly designed to handle large-scale data, improve generalization, and incorporate diverse forms of side information \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\].

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 10 papers found.

To address these limitations, researchers have proposed alternative approaches, such as spectral-based methods that leverage the full spectrum of signals in user interaction data \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. ### <a id='category-recommender-systems-research'></a>Recommender Systems Research: 4 papers found.
Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommender systems, but its performance is often hindered by data sparsity and the cold start problem \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have explored the integration of auxiliary information, such as item content or social networks, to enhance recommendation accuracy \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\]. For instance, Collaborative Topic Regression (CTR) couples CF with content-based learning, but its effectiveness diminishes when auxiliary data is sparse \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]. Similarly, knowledge graphs have been leveraged as a source of side information, with frameworks like RippleNet incorporating them into recommendation systems through ripple propagation mechanisms \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\]. Additionally, geometric deep learning on graphs has emerged as a promising approach, combining graph convolutional neural networks with recurrent networks to model complex user-item interactions \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\]. These methods aim to capture non-linear diffusion patterns and reduce parameter dependency on the scale of the dataset. The proposed ChebyCF framework further advances this field by utilizing spectral graph theory to overcome limitations in traditional graph-based CF, such as restricted linear filters and limited latent space capacity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. By employing Chebyshev interpolation and ideal pass filters, ChebyCF effectively captures the full spectrum of user interaction signals, leading to improved performance across benchmarks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].
