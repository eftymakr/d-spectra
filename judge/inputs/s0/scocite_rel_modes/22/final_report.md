## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has attracted significant research interest due to its ability to transfer knowledge from labeled source data to unlabeled target data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. Traditional UDA methods often assume that the category settings between source and target domains are known \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\], which is not always the case in real-world scenarios. This assumption can lead to performance degradation when private classes—categories present in one domain but not the other—exist \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. To address this limitation, several approaches have been proposed to handle domain adaptation without prior knowledge of category settings.

One line of research focuses on improving feature alignment between domains. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] introduces a category anchor-guided approach that explicitly enforces category-aware feature alignment to learn shared discriminative features and classifiers simultaneously. This method uses category-wise centroids as guided anchors to identify active features in the target domain and assign pseudo-labels \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. Similarly, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\] proposes a two-step self-supervised domain adaptation approach to minimize both inter-domain and intra-domain gaps. The first step involves inter-domain adaptation, while the second step focuses on reducing the intra-domain gap by leveraging self-supervised learning from an easy to hard subdomain \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\].

Another approach leverages adversarial learning to align the marginal distributions between source and target domains. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\] introduces a method that swaps the low-frequency spectrum of one domain with the other to reduce domain discrepancy. This method is simple and achieves state-of-the-art performance without requiring adversarial optimization \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\]. In contrast, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\] proposes a category-level adversarial network that enforces local semantic consistency during global alignment. This approach reduces the weight of adversarial loss for well-aligned categories while increasing the adversarial force for poorly aligned ones \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\].

Recent works have also focused on pseudo-label refinement and uncertainty estimation. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\] introduces a method that estimates prediction uncertainty during training to rectify pseudo label learning, dynamically setting confidence thresholds based on prediction variance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\]. \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\] further improves pseudo-label learning by using prototypical pseudo label denoising and target structure learning, aligning prototypical assignments based on relative feature distances \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\]. These methods aim to improve the reliability of pseudo labels, which are crucial for self-training approaches in UDA.

### <a id='category-semantic-segmentation-domain-adaptation'></a>Semantic Segmentation Domain Adaptation: 21 papers found.
Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation aims to transfer knowledge from labeled source data to unlabeled target data, addressing the challenge of domain shift \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. Traditional methods often assume known category settings between domains, which is impractical in real-world scenarios where private classes may exist, leading to performance degradation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. Recent works have focused on improving adaptation by leveraging pseudo-labels, adversarial learning, and consistency regularization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. For instance, some methods use entropy minimization to balance gradients and avoid bias toward easy-to-transfer samples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\], while others employ self-supervised learning to reduce intra-domain gaps \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. Additionally, depth-aware frameworks have been proposed to exploit privileged information like depth maps for better alignment \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. A two-step approach has also been introduced to minimize both inter-domain and intra-domain gaps by first adapting the model and then refining it within subdomains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. Furthermore, methods such as DACS mix images and labels from both domains to improve training \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\], while others focus on fine-grained adversarial learning to align class-level features \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, with a growing emphasis on robustness, efficiency, and adaptability to unseen domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\].

A popular strategy is to align the marginal distribution in the feature space through adversarial learning. Recent advancements in domain adaptation for semantic segmentation have focused on addressing the challenges posed by domain shifts, particularly in unsupervised settings. Prior work, such as the introduction of domain adversarial networks, has aimed to align source and target domains by leveraging adversarial learning to improve generalization \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02649)\]. However, these methods often assume known category settings between domains, which is not always valid in real-world applications \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11556)\]. To overcome this limitation, researchers have proposed techniques that enable adaptation without prior knowledge of category configurations, such as the Universal Domain Adaptation for Semantic Segmentation (UniDA-SS) framework \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11556)\]. This approach introduces novel components like Domain-Specific Prototype-based Distinction (DSPD) and Target-based Image Matching (TIM) to enhance the separation of common classes and improve adaptation performance \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11556)\]. Additionally, methods that utilize synthetic data for training have been explored, with approaches like target-guided distillation and spatial-aware adaptation schemes aiming to bridge the domain gap between synthetic and real-world data \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11556)\]. These strategies highlight the ongoing efforts to improve domain adaptation in semantic segmentation by addressing both distribution shifts and category configuration uncertainties \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02649)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11556)\].
