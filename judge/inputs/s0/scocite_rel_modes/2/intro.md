## Related Works

Nonverbal communication plays a crucial role in human interaction, encompassing gestures, facial expressions, and body language that convey intent and emotion \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/86f81c8a46a84cd969ed80e88395ba5de709794f)\]. Existing large language models (LLMs) have primarily focused on textual inputs, often neglecting the integration of nonverbal cues, which limits their ability to create immersive conversational experiences \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/86f81c8a46a84cd969ed80e88395ba5de709794f)\]. This gap has motivated recent research into multimodal models that incorporate visual and linguistic information for more comprehensive understanding \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of multimodal data to enhance language models. For instance, Kosmos-1 is a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) that can perceive general modalities, learn in context, and follow instructions without requiring fine-tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. It is trained on web-scale multimodal corpora, including images, text, and image-caption pairs, enabling it to perform tasks such as image recognition, visual question answering, and even OCR-free NLP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. Similarly, the Flamingo model is designed for few-shot learning and can handle sequences of interleaved visual and textual data, demonstrating strong performance on tasks like visual question-answering and captioning \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. 

The importance of nonverbal cues in dialogue has also been addressed in the context of video-based conversation models. Video-ChatGPT, for example, merges a video-adapted visual encoder with a large language model to understand and generate detailed conversations about videos \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\]. This model is trained on a dataset of 100,000 video-instruction pairs, enabling it to generate contextually relevant responses based on visual input \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\]. 

Multimodal datasets have been instrumental in advancing research in this area. The CMU-MOSEI dataset, for instance, is a large-scale dataset for sentiment analysis and emotion recognition, containing asynchronous sequences of text, visual, and acoustic modalities \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/006fdeff6e1a81c404317ee4056d6cc72f9c0e50)\]. It has been used to develop interpretable fusion techniques, such as the Dynamic Fusion Graph (DFG), which allows for effective interaction between modalities \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/006fdeff6e1a81c404317ee4056d6cc72f9c0e50)\]. Similarly, the Visual Genome dataset provides dense annotations of objects, attributes, and relationships within images, enabling models to reason about complex visual scenes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07332)\]. 

Recent benchmarks have also been introduced to evaluate the performance of multimodal models. The MME benchmark evaluates MLLMs on 14 subtasks, measuring both perception and cognition abilities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\].
