## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match speech content and emotional tone. Early approaches often relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, but recent advances have shifted toward data-driven deep learning models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. These models typically use neural networks to map audio signals to facial animations, often leveraging pre-trained self-supervised audio encoders to extract meaningful features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. However, a critical limitation of these methods is the inability to accurately distinguish between phonetically similar syllables that produce distinct lip shapes, leading to an averaging effect in lip motion generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\].

Several studies have explored the use of self-supervised speech representations to improve the quality of audio-driven facial animation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\] introduced a method that integrates self-supervised pre-trained speech representations to address data scarcity issues, while \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\] proposed a Transformer-based model that captures long-term audio context for more accurate lip synchronization. These works highlight the importance of capturing both phonetic and emotional nuances in speech to generate realistic facial animations. However, they often fail to explicitly decorrelate audio encodings to prevent the averaging effect caused by phonetically similar syllables \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

To address this limitation, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00023)\] proposed a method that disentangles audio representations into phonetic content, emotional tone, and background noise, improving robustness against variations in speech input. Similarly, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\] introduced a framework that learns disentangled audio-visual representations to enable arbitrary-subject talking face generation, demonstrating the potential of disentanglement in enhancing realism and controllability. These approaches, however, do not explicitly target the coupling between phonetically similar syllables and their corresponding lip shapes, which remains a key challenge in achieving natural facial animations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09488)\].

Another line of research focuses on the integration of emotional and linguistic cues for more expressive facial animation. For example, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\] proposed a system that decomposes speech into duration-independent emotion and duration-dependent content spaces, enabling more natural and emotionally expressive facial movements. Additionally, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\] introduced a method that disentangles speech into emotional and content components, allowing for more accurate lip synchronization and expressive facial expressions. These works underscore the importance of incorporating both linguistic and emotional information for more realistic facial animations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\].

### <a id='category-3d-facial-animation-from-speech'></a>3D Facial Animation from Speech: 14 papers found.
In the field of 3D facial animation driven by speech, prior research has primarily focused on improving the accuracy of lip synchronization and the realism of facial expressions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. Many approaches rely on pre-trained self-supervised audio models to encode speech, but these models often struggle with distinguishing between phonetically similar syllables that produce distinct lip shapes, leading to an averaging effect in lip motion generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. To address this limitation, recent works have introduced methods that incorporate semantic information to better capture the nuances of speech \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. For example, some studies have proposed using categorical latent spaces to disentangle audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information, enabling more realistic facial animations that include not only lip movements but also eye blinks and brow motion \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. Others have developed transformer-based models that leverage long-term audio context and autoregressive prediction to generate more expressive and accurate 3D face meshes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Additionally, there has been a growing emphasis on handling identity-specific speaking styles and facial idiosyncrasies, with methods like Imitator learning identity-specific details from short input videos to produce more natural facial expressions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. Furthermore, efforts have been made to generalize across arbitrary subjects and languages, with models such as VOCA capable of animating a wide range of adult faces based on any speech input \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of speech-driven facial animation, with a focus on improving both the technical accuracy and the perceptual realism of generated animations.

### <a id='category-speech-driven-3d-facial-animation'></a>Speech Driven 3D Facial Animation: 10 papers found.
Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point of recent research, aiming to generate realistic and emotionally expressive facial movements from speech signals \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. Existing methods often rely on pre-trained self-supervised audio models as encoders, but they struggle with the coupling of phonetically similar syllables that result in an averaging effect during lip motion generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed various approaches, such as the emotion disentangling encoder (EDE) that separates emotional and content components of speech \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\], and the code query task framework that reduces cross-modal mapping uncertainty by leveraging a learned codebook \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\]. Additionally, methods like SelfTalk introduce self-supervision in cross-modal networks to enhance lip movement accuracy while reducing reliance on labeled data \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. Another approach, FaceXHuBERT, utilizes a self-supervised HuBERT model to incorporate both lexical and non-lexical information from audio, improving emotional expressiveness without requiring extensive labeled data \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05416)\]. The EMOTE model further advances this by explicitly controlling emotional expression while maintaining lip-sync, using decoupled losses for speech and emotion \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. Moreover, the Temporal GAN approach generates videos with synchronized lip movements and natural facial expressions by using multiple discriminators focused on audio-visual synchronization \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\]. These methods collectively highlight the challenges and advancements in disentangling speech content and emotion, improving lip-sync, and generating more natural and expressive facial animations.

### <a id='category-audiodriven-facial-animation'></a>AudioDriven Facial Animation: 2 papers found.
In the field of audio-driven facial animation, recent advancements have focused on improving the accuracy and expressiveness of generated facial movements. Researchers have explored methods that generate 3D facial animations directly from speech, such as Voice2Face, which employs a conditional Variational Autoencoder to produce mesh animations and a separate module to map these to rig controller space \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/435b15745fb279db4ff8daf9d5facb9b21d58f3b)\]. This approach allows for automated speech style control and the animation of the tongue, which is a significant improvement over previous models that often lacked such capabilities \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/435b15745fb279db4ff8daf9d5facb9b21d58f3b)\]. Similarly, 3D-TalkEmo introduces a deep neural network capable of generating 3D talking-head animations with various emotions, supported by a large dataset of synchronized audio and video recordings \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12051)\]. This work also proposes a novel 3D face representation structure, the geometry map, which preserves vertex-to-vertex distances and enables effective convolutional processing of facial surfaces \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12051)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance the realism and emotional expressiveness of audio-driven facial animations, addressing limitations such as the averaging effect of phonetically similar syllables in self-supervised audio feature spaces. The integration of semantic information to decorrelate audio encodings, as seen in the Wav2Sem module, further demonstrates the trend toward more precise and natural facial animation generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\].
