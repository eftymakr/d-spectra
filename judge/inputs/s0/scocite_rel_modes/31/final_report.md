## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning general-purpose representations from raw data. This approach has been widely applied in computer vision and natural language processing, with notable works such as Contrastive Predictive Coding \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\], which leverages autoregressive models to learn latent representations by predicting future samples \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. Similarly, SimCLR \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\] presents a simple framework for contrastive learning of visual representations, demonstrating that composition of data augmentations and learnable nonlinear transformations significantly improve representation quality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. These methods have shown strong performance across various domains, including speech, images, and text \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. However, these approaches often rely on large amounts of unlabeled data and may not capture the complex relationships between different genomic profiles \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\].

In the field of genomics, unsupervised learning has also been explored. For example, the work by \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\] proposes an unsupervised learning approach to build features suitable for object detection and classification by solving jigsaw puzzles of natural images. This method pre-trains a context-free network (CFN) on a large dataset and transfers the features to other tasks through fine-tuning. While this approach is effective for visual tasks, it does not directly address the challenges of learning representations from genomic data, which are regulated by complex genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\].

These findings suggest that self-supervised learning can be effective for visual tasks, but they do not directly address the challenges of learning representations from genomic data, which require capturing the relationships between DNA sequences and genomic profiles \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\].

### <a id='category-self-supervised-learning-methods'></a>Self Supervised Learning Methods: 10 papers found.
In the realm of self-supervised learning methods, several approaches have emerged to learn effective representations from unlabeled data. Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\] introduces a probabilistic contrastive loss to capture maximally useful information for predicting future samples, leveraging autoregressive models in latent space. Similarly, spatial context-based methods, such as those that predict patch positions within images \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\], have shown success in learning rich visual representations by utilizing within-image context. These methods enable unsupervised visual discovery of objects and improve performance in downstream tasks like object detection \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. Another approach, Noise-Contrastive Estimation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\], focuses on instance-level discriminative features, achieving state-of-the-art results on ImageNet classification by learning representations that capture apparent similarity among instances. Furthermore, improvements to contrastive learning, such as those in SimCLR and MoCo \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\]\[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\], have led to more efficient and effective unsupervised learning frameworks, with MoCo demonstrating strong transferability to downstream tasks \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. Additionally, methods like Prototypical Contrastive Learning (PCL) \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\] introduce prototypes to encode semantic structures into the embedding space, achieving significant accuracy improvements in low-resource transfer tasks. These diverse methods highlight the ongoing evolution of self-supervised learning, emphasizing the importance of context, contrastive learning, and efficient representation learning in advancing AI capabilities.

### <a id='category-self-supervised-visual-learning'></a>Self Supervised Visual Learning: 3 papers found.
In the realm of self-supervised visual learning, recent advancements have focused on improving the quality of learned representations without the need for extensive labeled data. SimCLR \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\] introduced a simple framework that leverages contrastive learning, emphasizing the importance of data augmentation and nonlinear transformations in enhancing representation quality. The framework demonstrated significant improvements, achieving 76.5% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet with a linear classifier trained on self-supervised representations, matching the performance of a supervised ResNet-50 \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. Building on these ideas, DINO \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\] further explored the potential of self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs), showing that self-supervised ViT features contain explicit semantic segmentation information and excel as k-NN classifiers, reaching 78.3% top-1 accuracy with a small ViT. Additionally, DINO highlighted the importance of momentum encoder, multi-crop training, and small patches in ViT performance \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\]. Meanwhile, semi-supervised learning techniques, such as those discussed in \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\], have also contributed to the field by utilizing unlabeled data to improve model accuracy when labeled data is limited, employing methods like randomized data augmentation and dropout to enhance generalization. These approaches collectively underscore the evolving landscape of self-supervised learning, emphasizing the role of architectural choices, training strategies, and data augmentation in achieving competitive performance across various visual tasks.
