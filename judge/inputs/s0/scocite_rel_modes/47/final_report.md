## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to improve recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge transfer across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Traditional MDR methods often assume a single structure for transferring domain-shared knowledge, which may not be optimal when the beneficial information varies across domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. This limitation can lead to the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP), where unselectively leveraging information from all domains results in performance degradation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. To address this, recent works have explored more sophisticated mechanisms for domain selection and knowledge transfer.

Several approaches have focused on improving domain alignment and knowledge transfer. For instance, CoNet introduces a deep transfer learning framework that enables dual knowledge transfer across domains by connecting hidden layers of two base networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. This method leverages cross connections to enhance the learning of complex user-item interaction relationships. Similarly, DDTCDR proposes a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains until the learning process stabilizes, thereby preserving bidirectional latent relations between users and items \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods emphasize the importance of modeling domain-specific and shared knowledge effectively.

Another line of research has explored multi-task learning (MTL) for MDR. The DTCDR framework, for example, utilizes a multi-task learning approach to improve recommendation performance on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously by sharing embeddings of common users across domains \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. This approach allows for better adaptation to domain-specific characteristics while maintaining generalization. Similarly, the MAMDR framework introduces a model-agnostic learning approach that enables scalability across thousands of domains by leveraging domain negotiation and regularization strategies \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\]. This work highlights the challenges of domain conflict and overfitting in multi-domain settings.

In addition to these, the PPGN model addresses the sparsity issue by constructing a Cross-Domain Preference Matrix (CDPM) and using a preference propagation mechanism to capture high-order information propagation on the graph \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]. This approach simplifies cross-domain recommendation into a unified multi-task model, improving performance on real-world datasets. Similarly, the BiTGCF model introduces a bi-directional transfer learning method that exploits high-order connectivity in user-item graphs and enables two-way knowledge transfer across domains \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.3535)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of graph-based approaches in capturing complex interactions across domains.

The STAR model, on the other hand, proposes a star topology adaptive recommender system that trains a single model to serve multiple domains by leveraging data from all domains simultaneously \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59)\]. This approach captures domain-specific characteristics while modeling commonalities between domains, leading to improved performance on click-through rate

### <a id='category-cross-domain-recommendation-methods'></a>Cross Domain Recommendation Methods: 11 papers found.
Cross-domain recommendation (CDR) has emerged as a critical approach to address data sparsity in recommender systems by leveraging knowledge from multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Various methods have been proposed to facilitate knowledge transfer between domains, including transfer learning-based approaches that use neural networks to model complex user-item interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. These methods often introduce cross connections between domains to enable dual knowledge transfer, as seen in collaborative cross networks (CoNet) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Additionally, dual learning mechanisms have been employed to iteratively transfer information between domains, enhancing the ability to capture bidirectional latent relations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of cross-domain knowledge transfer by incorporating graph-based models and preference propagation techniques \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. Furthermore, multi-domain recommendation (MDR) has gained attention for its ability to improve recommendations across multiple domains simultaneously, with approaches such as adversarial feature translation and domain-specific masked encoders being proposed to enhance feature interactions and domain adaptation \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]. These methods aim to address the challenges of domain-specific preferences and data distribution differences, ensuring that the transferred knowledge is both relevant and effective \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb919954e93726aff490458bd61d6b3d8155fbd7)\]. The development of scalable and generalizable frameworks, such as MAMDR, has also been a focus, enabling the deployment of multi-domain recommendation systems in large-scale platforms \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb919954e93726aff490458bd61d6b3d8155fbd7)\]. Overall, these approaches highlight the importance of modeling domain interactions and adapting transfer strategies to avoid negative transfer and improve recommendation performance \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16863)\].

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 3 papers found.
The Related Works section for the paper's abstract on Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) highlights existing methods that address cross-domain knowledge transfer, particularly focusing on the challenges of data sparsity and negative transfer. Traditional collaborative filtering approaches, such as probabilistic matrix factorization, often struggle with sparse rating matrices \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.3535)\]. To overcome this, multi-domain collaborative filtering (MCF) has been proposed, which simultaneously models multiple domains and transfers knowledge across them by learning domain correlations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.3535)\]. However, most existing techniques are limited to single-target or dual-target cross-domain recommendation (CDR), making them difficult to generalize to multiple target domains \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11964)\]. Additionally, the negative transfer problem remains prevalent, where knowledge from a source domain may not always improve performance in a target domain, especially when the source domain has sparse data \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11964)\]. Recent work, such as CAT-ART, addresses these limitations by introducing a multi-target CDR method that leverages representation learning and embedding transfer to improve recommendations across all participating domains \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11964)\]. Similarly, the Shared-account Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (SCSR) task introduces new challenges, such as identifying user behaviors under shared accounts and transferring knowledge across domains \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the need for more flexible and adaptive approaches to domain transfer in recommendation systems.
