## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has emerged as a critical area within sentiment analysis, particularly in the context of user-generated content such as app reviews. While traditional sentiment analysis often focuses on broad polarity categories (positive, negative, neutral), recent research has emphasized the need for more nuanced emotion detection to better capture the complexity of human expression \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. This is especially important in domains like app reviews, where users frequently express a wide range of emotions that go beyond simple sentiment labels \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\].

Several studies have explored the development of emotion lexicons and annotated datasets to support fine-grained emotion analysis. For example, the NRC VAD Lexicon provides human-rated valence, arousal, and dominance scores for over 20,000 English words, enabling more precise emotion modeling \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882)\]. Similarly, the GoEmotions dataset, consisting of 58,000 Reddit comments labeled for 27 emotion categories, has been used to train and evaluate emotion classification models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. These datasets highlight the importance of large-scale, high-quality annotations in improving the accuracy of emotion detection systems.

Crowdsourcing has also been leveraged to generate emotion lexicons, as seen in the work of \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\], which demonstrates how collaborative annotation can produce reliable word-emotion associations. This approach is particularly useful for overcoming the limitations of small, manually curated lexicons \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b)\]. However, challenges remain in ensuring annotation consistency and avoiding biases, especially when dealing with complex or mixed emotions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\].

In the context of app reviews, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.01996)\] highlights the need for structured annotation frameworks that align with established emotion taxonomies, such as Plutchik’s wheel of emotions. This work also emphasizes the importance of iterative human annotation processes to refine guidelines and address challenges in emotion classification \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66c8ab0b3b7698cf5333c3f7350a6eaa5302294b)\]. Furthermore, the use of large language models (LLMs) for automating emotion annotation has been explored, with studies showing that while LLMs can reduce manual effort and achieve substantial agreement with human annotators, full automation remains challenging due to the interpretive nature of emotional content \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/723f744455914bf1ead6ad267976a1500b7dee4a)\].

### <a id='category-affect-recognition-in-text'></a>Affect Recognition in Text: 14 papers found.
Affect recognition in text has been a focal point of recent research, with significant efforts directed towards improving the accuracy and granularity of emotion detection. Early studies highlighted the limitations of small emotion lexicons and the need for large-scale annotated datasets to advance the field \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. Researchers have since developed methods to generate high-quality lexicons through crowdsourcing, emphasizing the importance of structured annotation protocols and validation techniques to ensure reliability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. The development of large annotated datasets, such as those used in the SemEval-2018 Task 1, has enabled more robust machine learning models, with some achieving near-human performance in mapping between dimensional and categorical emotion representations \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b)\]. Additionally, the use of deep learning models on fine-grained emotion datasets has led to state-of-the-art results, with models achieving an average accuracy of 87.58% on 24 emotion types and 95.68% on Plutchik’s 8 primary emotion dimensions \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. These advancements have also been applied to specific domains, such as children's fairy tales, where supervised machine learning models have shown promising results in classifying emotional content \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/872352b0a53ab6cbb4420f81df64d215d86c7d9b)\]. The integration of appraisal theories, which consider cognitive evaluations leading to emotions, has further enriched the understanding of affective processes, with studies showing that knowledge of the emotion improves annotator reliability \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12858)\]. Furthermore, the use of fine-grained emotion features has been shown to significantly improve personality detection, demonstrating the value of detailed emotional categorization \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6352)\]. Overall, these contributions highlight the growing interest in and progress towards more nuanced and accurate affect recognition in text.

### <a id='category-emotion-analysis-datasets'></a>Emotion Analysis Datasets: 2 papers found.
There is a growing body of research focused on emotion analysis datasets, particularly in the context of social media and long-form narratives. Existing datasets for sentiment analysis, such as polarity and subjectivity classification, are well-established \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. However, datasets for detecting discrete emotions, especially in the social media domain, remain limited \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. Recent efforts have aimed to address this gap by extending existing datasets with emotion annotations, such as the SemEval 2016 stance and sentiment dataset \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. Additionally, new datasets like the Dataset for Emotions of Narrative Sequences (DENS) have been introduced, which includes narratives from both classic literature and modern online platforms, annotated using crowdsourcing methods \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11769)\]. These datasets provide valuable resources for studying emotion in diverse text types, though challenges remain in aligning emotion annotations with other annotation layers such as stance and sentiment \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. The development of such datasets has also led to the exploration of advanced models, including fine-tuned BERT models, which have shown promising results in emotion classification tasks \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11769)\]. These contributions highlight the ongoing efforts to improve emotion analysis in text, particularly in domains where fine-grained emotion detection is essential.
