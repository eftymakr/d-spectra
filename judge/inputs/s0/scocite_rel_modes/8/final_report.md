## Related Works

Fairness in machine learning has been a growing concern, particularly as algorithms are increasingly used in high-stakes decision-making scenarios. Early work on fairness focused on statistical definitions, such as parity of false positive and false negative rates across different demographic groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. However, these definitions often fail to capture the nuanced ways in which bias can manifest, especially when considering model uncertainty \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. For instance, a model might have equal accuracy across groups but exhibit higher confidence in incorrect predictions for one group, leading to disparate treatment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This issue is particularly relevant in the context of large language models (LLMs), where model confidence can significantly influence the perceived fairness of predictions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\].

Several studies have highlighted the limitations of conventional fairness metrics, which often rely on discrete accuracy-based evaluations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. These metrics fail to account for the implicit impact of model uncertainty, which can lead to biased outcomes even when accuracy is balanced \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed alternative fairness definitions that incorporate uncertainty, such as the concept of counterfactual fairness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. This framework suggests that a decision is fair if it remains consistent across different demographic groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. However, applying such definitions in practice remains challenging, particularly when dealing with complex models like LLMs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

The importance of diverse and representative datasets for evaluating fairness has also been emphasized. Many existing datasets suffer from issues such as limited diversity, lack of clarity, and insufficient size, which can hinder the development of robust fairness benchmarks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. In response, new datasets have been introduced to better reflect the complexities of real-world applications. For example, the UCI Adult dataset has been widely used to study fairness in predictive models, though it has limitations in terms of external validity \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\]. More recently, researchers have proposed new datasets that span multiple domains and include a broader range of demographic groups \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\].

To further improve fairness evaluation, some studies have explored adversarial learning techniques to mitigate unwanted biases in models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08843)\]. These methods aim to reduce the ability of an adversary to infer protected attributes from model predictions, thereby promoting fairness \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. However, achieving fairness often comes at the cost of reduced model performance, highlighting the inherent trade-offs between fairness and accuracy \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. This trade-off is particularly relevant in the context of LLMs, where maintaining high accuracy is crucial for practical applications \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07139)\].

### <a id='category-fairness-in-machine-learning'></a>Fairness in Machine Learning: 15 papers found.
The category of fairness in machine learning encompasses a wide range of approaches aimed at mitigating bias and ensuring equitable treatment across different demographic groups. Researchers have proposed various definitions of fairness, such as statistical parity, equalized odds, and individual fairness, each with its own strengths and limitations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. For instance, statistical definitions often focus on parity of outcomes across predefined groups, but they can fail to account for subgroup disparities and are susceptible to "fairness gerrymandering" \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. In contrast, individual fairness emphasizes treating similar individuals similarly, which can be more aligned with intuitive notions of justice \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. However, achieving both group and individual fairness simultaneously is challenging, as these criteria can be inherently conflicting \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. Some studies have explored causal inference as a means to better understand and model fairness, arguing that observational criteria may not capture the underlying causal relationships that contribute to discrimination \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. Additionally, fairness in machine learning is closely related to other concepts such as privacy, with differential privacy techniques being adapted to ensure fair outcomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. The development of fairness-aware algorithms, such as those that incorporate adversarial learning to reduce bias \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\], represents a significant step toward creating more equitable models. Furthermore, the application of fairness criteria to real-world scenarios, such as recidivism prediction and pretrial risk assessment, highlights the practical implications of these theoretical advancements \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\]. Overall, the field is characterized by ongoing debates about the best ways to operationalize fairness, with a growing recognition of the need for nuanced, context-sensitive approaches \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\].

The related works section focuses on the challenges and solutions in ensuring fairness in machine learning systems, particularly in the context of algorithmic bias and fairness mitigation. Existing research highlights that fairness in deep learning is a critical concern, especially in high-stake applications, where algorithmic discrimination can have significant societal impacts \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. Scholars have emphasized the need for interpretability to diagnose the reasons behind such discrimination, suggesting that fairness mitigation approaches should be integrated throughout the deep learning life-cycle \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. Furthermore, the fairness community has largely relied on limited datasets, such as the UCI Adult dataset, which may not adequately represent real-world complexities \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This has led to the development of new, more comprehensive datasets that span multiple domains and allow for more robust empirical analysis of fairness trade-offs and algorithmic interventions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. In medical imaging, for instance, researchers have identified challenges in comparing bias mitigation algorithms due to inconsistent fairness criteria and varying experimental settings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. To address these issues, frameworks like MEDFAIR have been introduced to benchmark fairness in medical AI, offering a reproducible and standardized approach for evaluating different algorithms \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. These efforts underscore the growing recognition that fairness in machine learning requires not only technical innovations but also a deeper understanding of real-world needs and ethical considerations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. The development of uncertainty-aware fairness metrics, such as UCerF, further illustrates the evolving landscape of fairness evaluation, as researchers seek to capture the nuanced impact of model confidence on fairness outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Overall, these works collectively highlight the importance of aligning fairness research with practical challenges and ethical standards in AI development.
