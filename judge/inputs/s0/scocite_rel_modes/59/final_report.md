## Related Works

Fairness in ranking and recommendation systems has been a growing area of research, with multiple approaches addressing the trade-off between accuracy and fairness. Early work on fairness in rankings focused on group fairness, ensuring that different demographic groups are treated equitably in terms of exposure or representation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\]. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.07281)\] proposed a framework for fairness constraints on rankings based on exposure allocation, which allows for the formulation of fairness goals in terms of how items are presented. This approach was later extended to include both user-side and provider-side fairness, recognizing that recommender systems operate in two-sided markets \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]. 

The concept of fairness through awareness was introduced in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\], where the goal is to ensure that similar individuals are treated similarly during classification, while maintaining utility for the classifier. This idea was later adapted to ranking tasks, where fairness constraints are incorporated into the ranking process to ensure equitable treatment of items or users \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04056)\]. The paper \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06368)\] introduced a fairness-aware ranking algorithm that ensures group fairness in top-k rankings, ensuring that protected groups are represented fairly in the top positions. This work was further extended to consider both user and item fairness in a joint framework \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].

In the context of re-ranking, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] proposed a fairness-aware re-ranking algorithm that balances ranking quality and provider-side fairness, incorporating personalized user tolerance towards diversity. This approach was later refined to ensure fairness for both customers and producers in two-sided platforms \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]. The paper \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06660)\] introduced a max-min fairness re-ranking model that prioritizes the utility of the worst-off providers, ensuring that even those in weak market positions are fairly represented. This model was further generalized to incorporate both consumer and producer fairness in a joint optimization framework \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\].

The fairness-accuracy trade-off has also been studied in the context of learning-to-rank (LTR) algorithms, where fairness constraints are incorporated into the learning objective to ensure equitable treatment of items \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04056)\]. This approach was extended to consider the elasticity of utility between item groups, leading to the development of the ElasticRank algorithm \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]. ### <a id='category-fairness-in-recommendation-systems'></a>Fairness in Recommendation Systems: 8 papers found.
Fairness in recommendation systems has been a growing area of research, with multiple approaches addressing the trade-offs between accuracy and fairness. Prior work has identified that fairness considerations in re-ranking can lead to a decline in user-side accuracy, similar to how commodity taxes on suppliers transfer costs to consumers \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00093)\]. This trade-off is governed by the elasticity of utility between item groups, a concept that has been used to formalize the relationship between item-side fairness and user-side accuracy \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00093)\]. Several studies have proposed fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms to balance these competing objectives. For instance, the Fairness-Aware Re-ranking (FAR) algorithm balances ranking quality and provider-side fairness by incorporating personalized user tolerance towards provider diversification \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Similarly, the Provider Max-min Fairness Re-ranking (P-MMF) model addresses provider fairness by formulating the problem as a resource allocation task, where exposure slots are allocated to providers using max-min fairness as a regularizer \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06660)\]. Additionally, the FairRec algorithm ensures two-sided fairness by guaranteeing exposure for producers while maintaining customer-side fairness \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]. These approaches highlight the need for more comprehensive fairness metrics that account for both user and provider perspectives \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\]. The concept of elasticity has also been used to evaluate fairness-accuracy trade-offs, leading to the introduction of the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) as an evaluation framework \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00093)\]. Overall, these studies underscore the complexity of fairness in recommendation systems and the importance of developing algorithms that can effectively balance multiple fairness objectives \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00093)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06660)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\].
