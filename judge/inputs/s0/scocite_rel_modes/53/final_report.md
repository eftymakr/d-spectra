## Related Works

Sequential recommendation systems aim to predict the next item a user will interact with, based on their historical behavior. These systems have evolved significantly, incorporating advanced neural network architectures to better capture sequential patterns and user preferences. Early approaches, such as Markov Chains (MCs), assumed that the next action depends only on the last few actions, while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) introduced the ability to model longer-term dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, RNNs often suffer from computational inefficiency and difficulty in capturing long-range dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. More recently, self-attention mechanisms have gained popularity for their ability to dynamically weigh the importance of different items in a sequence \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. 

The use of self-attention in sequential recommendation was further refined with models like SASRec, which combines the flexibility of attention with the efficiency of MC-based methods by focusing on relevant items in the history \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Similarly, TiSASRec extends this idea by incorporating time intervals between interactions, allowing for more nuanced modeling of temporal dynamics \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of temporal awareness in capturing user behavior accurately.

Convolutional approaches, such as Caser, have also been introduced to model sequential patterns by treating item sequences as images in the time and latent spaces \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. This enables the extraction of local features, which can be useful for capturing both general preferences and sequential patterns. However, these models may not fully address the challenges of modeling long-range dependencies or capturing diverse interaction patterns \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\].

The introduction of bidirectional models, such as BERT4Rec, has further advanced the field by allowing the model to consider both left and right context of an item, thereby improving the representation of user behavior \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. This bidirectional approach helps in capturing more comprehensive user preferences, but it introduces challenges related to training efficiency and information leakage \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. To mitigate these issues, the Cloze objective is used to train the model by predicting masked items based on their surrounding context \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\].

In addition to attention-based and convolutional models, there has been growing interest in self-supervised learning for sequential recommendation. S3-Rec, for instance, leverages mutual information maximization to enhance the learning of correlations among item attributes and subsequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. This approach is particularly beneficial in scenarios with limited training data, as it enables the model to learn meaningful representations without explicit supervision \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\].

Another notable advancement is the use of graph neural networks (GNNs) in session-based recommendation, where session sequences are modeled as graph-structured data to capture complex item transitions

### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-models'></a>Sequential Recommendation Models: 9 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models aim to predict user actions based on their interaction sequences, with a focus on capturing both short-term and long-term patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Traditional approaches like Markov Chains (MCs) assume that the next action depends only on the last few actions, while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) can model longer-term dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, RNNs often struggle with computational efficiency and handling sparse data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, attention-based models such as SASRec \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\] and BERT4Rec \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] have been introduced, leveraging self-attention mechanisms to capture relevant items from a user's history without relying solely on sequential order. These models allow for more flexible and efficient representation of user behavior by considering both left and right contexts \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Additionally, models like Caser \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\] and TiSASRec \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\] incorporate convolutional filters and time-aware positional encodings, respectively, to better capture local features and temporal dynamics within sequences. Session-based recommendation methods, such as NARM \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\] and SR-GNN \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00855)\], further extend these ideas by focusing on short, anonymous sessions and modeling complex item transitions using graph neural networks. Meanwhile, hierarchical attention networks \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0b42168f22713c48d8b0ce3bc8f680d347184d88)\] and generative models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05163)\] have also been proposed to better handle long-term preferences and long-range dependencies. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the accuracy and efficiency of sequential recommendation systems by integrating diverse modeling techniques.

### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-methods'></a>Sequential Recommendation Methods: 4 papers found.
Recent advancements in sequential recommendation have focused on improving the modeling of user behavior through deep learning techniques. Existing methods often rely on item prediction loss, which can lead to data sparsity issues and poor utilization of contextual information \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed self-supervised learning approaches, such as S3-Rec, which utilize mutual information maximization to capture correlations between attributes, items, subsequences, and sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. Additionally, noise in logged user behavior data has been identified as a critical issue, prompting the development of filtering algorithms inspired by signal processing to improve model robustness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13556)\]. Another line of work emphasizes the importance of feature-level transitions, proposing methods like FDSA that integrate heterogeneous item features to better capture sequential patterns \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/63555b4f363ee72dd8d49d8deca68760542c0cd2)\]. Furthermore, representation degeneration in item embeddings has been observed, leading to the introduction of contrastive regularization techniques in models like DuoRec to enhance embedding quality \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to improve sequential recommendation by addressing data sparsity, noise, and representation quality through advanced neural architectures and self-supervised learning strategies.

Even though there has been a large body of research on generating recommendations based on modeling user-item interaction sequences, few of them attempt to represent and transfer these models for serving downstream tasks where only limited data exists. In this paper, we delve on the task of effectively learning a single user representation that can be applied to a diversity of tasks, from cross-domain recommendations to user profile predictions. Fine-tuning a large pre-trained network and adapting it to downstream tasks is an effective way to solve such tasks. However, fine-tuning is parameter inefficient considering that an entire model needs to be re-trained for every new task. To overcome this issue, we develop a parameter-efficient transfer learning architecture, termed as PeterRec, which can be configured on-the-fly to various downstream tasks. Specifically, PeterRec allows the pre-trained parameters to remain unaltered during fine-tuning by injecting a series of re-learned neural networks, which are small but as expressive as learning the entire network. We perform extensive experimental ablation to show the effectiveness of the learned user representation in five downstream tasks. Moreover, we show that PeterRec performs efficient transfer learning in multiple domains, where it achieves comparable or sometimes better performance relative to fine-tuning the entire model parameters.
