## Related Works

Recent advancements in information retrieval have seen a shift from traditional index-retrieve pipelines to more unified, end-to-end models that integrate indexing and search within a single framework. This trend is driven by the limitations of conventional methods, which often suffer from error accumulation during representation and indexing phases \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Additionally, insufficient query-document interaction hinders semantic alignment, limiting accuracy improvements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Several works have explored differentiable search indexes (DSIs) as a solution, where a single transformer model encodes all corpus information and directly maps queries to document identifiers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. 

The DSI approach eliminates the need for separate indexing and retrieval components, enabling direct query-to-docid mapping \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This paradigm has been further refined with variations such as DSI-QG, which addresses data distribution mismatches by generating relevant queries during indexing \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. Similarly, the Semantic-Enhanced DSI (SE-DSI) model improves document memorization by incorporating semantic features and elaborative descriptions during indexing \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of aligning query and document representations during the indexing phase to improve retrieval performance.

Another line of research focuses on the continual learning of DSI models, as dynamic corpora require incremental updates without catastrophic forgetting \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. DSI++ introduces an index update strategy using parameter isolation to mitigate forgetting during continual indexing \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. This work demonstrates that flatter minima in the loss landscape can stabilize learning and improve memory retention, offering a promising direction for maintaining performance over time.

Generative retrieval models, such as those proposed in the SEAL framework, use autoregressive models to generate and score ngrams as document identifiers, mapping them to full passages through efficient data structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This approach outperforms prior autoregressive methods and established retrieval solutions, achieving significant improvements on benchmark datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) introduces a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder and semantic document identifiers to enhance recall performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of end-to-end neural architectures for document retrieval, reducing reliance on traditional indexing techniques.

The use of large language models (LLMs) for query generation and semantic alignment has also gained traction. For instance, the TOME framework employs tokenized URLs as identifiers and introduces a two-stage generation architecture to improve retrieval accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\]. This approach addresses the discrepancy between pre-training and fine-tuning, as well as training and inference, by leveraging tokenized identifiers and structured generation pipelines \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\].
