## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a prominent approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. This strategy has been widely adopted due to its effectiveness in mitigating forgetting, particularly in scenarios where data is not available for replay \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. However, the optimality of concurrent rehearsal remains an open question. Recent work has explored alternative strategies, such as sequential rehearsal, inspired by human learning, where revisiting tasks helps mitigate forgetting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. 

Theoretical analyses of rehearsal-based CL in overparameterized linear models have shown that sequential rehearsal can outperform concurrent rehearsal when tasks are less similar \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. This finding aligns with empirical studies that demonstrate the benefits of selective experience replay, where experiences are stored in a long-term memory and selectively retrieved for replay \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. For instance, distribution matching has been shown to successfully prevent catastrophic forgetting, and is consistently the best approach on all domains tested \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Similarly, methods like REMIND, which use compressed representations for replay, have demonstrated superior performance in preventing forgetting while maintaining computational efficiency \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\].

The concept of hybrid rehearsal, which combines concurrent and sequential strategies, has also been explored. This approach trains similar tasks concurrently and revisits dissimilar tasks sequentially, aiming to balance the benefits of both strategies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08383)\]. The hybrid approach has been shown to outperform standard concurrent rehearsal in both theoretical analysis and empirical experiments with deep neural networks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\]. This aligns with findings that selective experience replay, when suitable selection algorithms are employed, can prevent catastrophic forgetting \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\].

Several studies have also investigated the role of memory mechanisms in continual learning. For example, the Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) method has been proposed to alleviate forgetting while allowing beneficial transfer of knowledge to previous tasks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6211)\]. Similarly, the Learning without Forgetting method enables the addition of new capabilities to a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) without requiring access to the training data for its existing capabilities \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of memory mechanisms in mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

In addition, the use of generative models for replay has been explored, such as Deep Generative Replay, which leverages a dual model architecture to sample and interleave training data for previous tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11910)\]. ### <a id='category-continual-learning-methods'></a>Continual Learning Methods: 7 papers found.
Continual Learning (CL) methods aim to enable models to learn sequentially from a stream of tasks without catastrophic forgetting, a phenomenon where past knowledge is lost as new tasks are learned \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Recent approaches have focused on leveraging episodic memory to retain past experiences, with methods like reservoir sampling showing effectiveness in maintaining performance across tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. However, challenges remain, particularly in non-stationary environments where data distributions change over time, leading to performance degradation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11910)\]. To address this, novel frameworks such as Variational Continual Learning (VCL) have been introduced, combining online variational inference with Monte Carlo methods to train models on evolving tasks while avoiding catastrophic forgetting \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10628)\]. Additionally, architectures inspired by human memory, such as dual memory systems, have been proposed to emulate the hippocampus and neocortex, using generative replay to maintain long-term memory \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10368)\]. These methods often rely on experience replay, where past data is stored and retrained alongside new data, though the optimal strategy—whether concurrent or sequential rehearsal—remains debated \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\]. Some studies suggest that sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first before revisiting past data, may offer greater benefits in certain scenarios, particularly when tasks are less similar \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\]. This has led to the development of hybrid rehearsal strategies that combine concurrent and sequential approaches based on task similarity \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\]. Overall, the field continues to explore both theoretical and empirical advancements to improve generalization and reduce forgetting in continual learning settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11910)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10628)\]\[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10368)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\].

The related works section focuses on the challenges and solutions for catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL), a critical issue in neural networks that limits their ability to learn multiple tasks sequentially \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. To address this, various approaches have been proposed, including methods that aim to balance the plasticity-stability trade-off, such as task-incremental classification, where tasks are presented in batches and the model must continually adapt without retraining from scratch \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. The concept of rehearsal-based methods has gained traction, as they store and reuse past data to mitigate forgetting \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. However, the optimal strategy for rehearsal remains unclear, with some studies suggesting that sequential rehearsal, where tasks are revisited in order, may offer greater benefits than concurrent rehearsal, especially when tasks are less similar \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. This idea is supported by theoretical analyses in overparameterized linear models, which show that sequential rehearsal can reduce forgetting and improve generalization \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Additionally, research has explored the role of model architecture and training regimes, such as dropout, learning rate decay, and batch size, in influencing the degree of forgetting \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. The use of experience replay, where past experiences are selectively stored and reused, has also been proposed as a promising approach to prevent catastrophic forgetting \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08383)\]. Furthermore, brain-inspired methods like REMIND have been introduced to enable efficient replay with compressed representations, offering a more biologically plausible approach to lifelong learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\]. These studies collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to develop robust continual learning frameworks that can effectively manage the trade-off between stability and plasticity \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\].

### <a id='category-continual-learning-techniques'></a>Continual Learning Techniques: 2 papers found.
The category "Continual Learning Techniques" explores methods to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in deep neural networks when learning new tasks incrementally. Research highlights that traditional models struggle with retaining prior knowledge when exposed to new data, leading to performance degradation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\]. To address this, various mechanisms have been proposed, including regularization, ensembling, rehearsal, dual-memory, and sparse-coding, each aiming to improve incremental learning efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\]. Rehearsal, in particular, has been widely studied, where past data is stored and reused during training to prevent forgetting \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05922)\]. However, full rehearsal is computationally expensive and memory-intensive, prompting the development of more efficient alternatives like ExStream, which reduces memory usage while maintaining effectiveness \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.05922)\]. Additionally, theoretical analyses of rehearsal-based continual learning in overparameterized models suggest that sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first and then past data is revisited, can offer better performance, especially when tasks are dissimilar \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\]. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring rehearsal strategies to task similarity and data characteristics, motivating hybrid approaches that combine concurrent and sequential rehearsal for optimal results \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.02072)\].
