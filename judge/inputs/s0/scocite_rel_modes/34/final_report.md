## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advancements focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as autoregressive models and Gaussian processes have been widely used but often struggle with capturing complex temporal dependencies \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. The introduction of deep learning frameworks has significantly enhanced forecasting capabilities, particularly with the advent of Transformer-based models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\]. These models leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies, making them suitable for both short- and long-term forecasting tasks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. However, the application of Transformers to time series forecasting is not without challenges, including computational inefficiency and the inability to effectively model non-stationary data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of Transformers for time series forecasting, with notable contributions such as PatchTST, which introduces a segmentation strategy to reduce computational complexity while retaining local semantic information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, iTransformer redefines the attention mechanism to better handle multivariate time series by inverting the dimensions of the input \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. These models have demonstrated improved performance on real-world datasets, particularly in scenarios requiring long-term forecasting \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. However, the effectiveness of Transformers in time series analysis is still debated, with some studies suggesting that simple linear models can outperform complex Transformer-based architectures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. This highlights the need for further investigation into the validity of Transformer-based approaches in time series forecasting.

In the financial domain, the integration of forecasting with decision-making processes has gained attention. The Predict-then-Optimize (PnO) approach, which combines forecasting with optimization, has been proposed to address goal mismatch in fund allocation \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07436)\]. This approach, however, introduces additional uncertainty due to the reliance on high-accuracy forecasting models. To mitigate this, the Risk-aware Time-Series Predict-and-Allocate (RTS-PnO) framework was introduced, which incorporates uncertainty calibration and is agnostic to the underlying forecasting model \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07436)\]. This framework aligns with recent efforts to develop models that are robust to forecasting uncertainty, such as the work on Non-stationary Transformers, which addresses the challenge of modeling non-stationary data by introducing a dual-module architecture \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\].

### <a id='category-time-series-forecasting-with-transformers'></a>Time Series Forecasting with Transformers: 12 papers found.
Time series forecasting has seen significant advancements with the integration of Transformer architectures, which excel at capturing long-range dependencies and complex temporal patterns \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. However, traditional Transformers face challenges such as quadratic time complexity, high memory usage, and difficulties in handling non-stationary data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. To address these issues, several models have been proposed, including PatchTST, which introduces a patching design to reduce computational overhead while preserving local semantic information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\], and iTransformer, which restructures the attention mechanism to focus on variate tokens for improved multivariate correlation capture \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. Additionally, the problem of over-stationarization in non-stationary data has been tackled by Non-stationary Transformers, which incorporate modules to retain intrinsic non-stationary information \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. Meanwhile, models like LSTNet combine CNNs and RNNs to extract both short-term and long-term patterns, while TimesNet leverages 2D tensor transformations to model complex temporal variations more effectively \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. The LogSparse Transformer and Autoformer further enhance efficiency and accuracy by introducing memory-efficient attention mechanisms and decomposition strategies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.00235)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to improve Transformer-based forecasting models in terms of computational efficiency, adaptability to non-stationary data, and performance on real-world applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\].

### <a id='category-time-series-forecasting-with-llms'></a>Time Series Forecasting with LLMs: 6 papers found.
In the realm of time series forecasting with large language models (LLMs), recent studies have explored the potential of leveraging LLMs' reasoning and knowledge capabilities to address challenges in financial time series forecasting \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. For instance, one study focused on NASDAQ-100 stocks, utilizing historical stock price data, company metadata, and financial news to develop an explainable forecasting approach \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. This work demonstrated that LLMs, such as GPT-4 and Open LLaMA, can effectively reason over textual and price data, extract insights, and perform cross-sequence reasoning, outperforming traditional models like ARMA-GARCH and gradient-boosting trees \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. Similarly, another study introduced Chronos, a framework that tokenizes time series data and trains transformer-based models on these tokens, achieving significant performance improvements across diverse datasets \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\]. Additionally, research has shown that pre-trained models, when fine-tuned, can generalize well to new forecasting tasks, even in zero-shot scenarios, by leveraging their ability to understand and generate structured outputs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. These developments highlight the growing interest in using LLMs for time series forecasting, emphasizing their potential to overcome limitations of classical methods and improve accuracy and interpretability \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\].

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 3 papers found.
The related works in the area of time series forecasting and financial decision-making highlight several key approaches and challenges. Traditional methods often rely on statistical models, while recent advances have incorporated deep learning architectures to better capture temporal dependencies and complex interactions between variables \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\]. However, many existing models, including those based on deep learning, are often treated as black-boxes, limiting their interpretability and adaptability to real-world scenarios \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. This issue is particularly relevant in financial applications, where the integration of forecasting and optimization is crucial for effective fund allocation \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. Recent studies have explored the use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting, but these models have been found to perform similarly to or worse than simpler attention-based approaches, especially when considering computational efficiency and the ability to capture sequential dependencies \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. Additionally, the introduction of state-of-the-art forecasting models can introduce additional uncertainty, which complicates the optimization process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. To address these challenges, the proposed RTS-PnO framework introduces a risk-aware approach that aligns the forecasting and allocation objectives, calibrates uncertainty adaptively, and remains agnostic to the underlying forecasting model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. This approach builds upon prior work that emphasizes the need for end-to-end training and objective alignment in financial decision-making \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\].
