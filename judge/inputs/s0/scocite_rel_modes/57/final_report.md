## Related Works

Generative retrieval (GR) has gained significant attention as a novel paradigm in information retrieval (IR), offering an end-to-end approach to document retrieval by generating document identifiers (docids) directly from queries \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Unlike traditional index-retrieve frameworks, GR models encode the entire corpus into a single model, enabling direct query-to-docid mapping \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This paradigm has been explored in various works, each proposing different strategies for docid representation and training procedures. 

Early works such as the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) introduced the idea of using a single transformer model to encode all documents and answer queries directly, eliminating the need for separate indexing stages \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. However, DSI models faced challenges in handling data distribution mismatches between indexing and retrieval phases, especially when queries are shorter than indexed documents \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address this, DSI-QG was proposed, which generates potentially relevant queries during indexing, allowing the model to better align with retrieval queries \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

Similarly, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) aimed to unify training and indexing stages by introducing a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder, improving recall performance on academic benchmarks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. The Ultron model further advanced this idea by encoding document knowledge into the model and directly retrieving relevant documents in an end-to-end manner \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\]. However, existing approaches often suffer from semantically deficient docids and limited supervised data, which hinder their effectiveness in dynamic settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\].

The GenRet method was introduced to address the challenge of defining semantic docids for generative retrieval. It learns to tokenize documents into short discrete representations using a discrete auto-encoding approach, enabling end-to-end modeling of retrieval tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. This method was later extended to handle larger corpora, with studies showing that generative retrieval can be competitive with state-of-the-art dual encoders on small datasets but faces scalability challenges when applied to millions of passages \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].

The introduction of NOVO docids further improved the interpretability and effectiveness of model-based IR by allowing docids to be generated in any order and optimized through training \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6682a3679a17d090830635f1c420126d20eae54a)\]. This approach was validated on multiple datasets, showing strong performance in both normal and zero-shot settings \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6682a3679a17d090830635f1c420126d20eae54a)\]. Additionally, the Semantic-Enhanced DSI (SE-DSI) model was proposed, which incorporates learning strategies from cognitive psychology to enhance document memorization and retrieval accuracy \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\].

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-research'></a>Generative Retrieval Research: 15 papers found.
Generative retrieval (GR) has gained significant attention as a novel paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-retrieve frameworks to end-to-end models that directly generate document identifiers (docids) for queries \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Unlike conventional methods that rely on static indexes, GR models encode the entire corpus into a single model, enabling direct query-to-docid mapping \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach has been explored through various architectures, such as the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), which uses a single Transformer to perform both indexing and retrieval \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. However, existing GR models often assume static document collections, limiting their applicability to dynamic corpora where documents are continuously added \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00537)\]. Recent studies have addressed this limitation by proposing continual learning strategies, such as DSI++, which allows incremental indexing of new documents while maintaining retrieval performance over previously indexed items \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. Additionally, models like CLEVER have introduced mechanisms to handle dynamic data, including incremental product quantization for efficient docid encoding and memory-augmented learning to prevent forgetting \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. The effectiveness of GR models in dynamic settings is further supported by findings that text-based docids, which are semantically rich and diverse, outperform numeric-based ones, especially in terms of generalization to unseen documents \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. These advancements highlight the growing focus on improving GR models for real-world applications where document collections evolve over time \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00537)\].

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Web search provides a promising way for people to obtain information and has been extensively studied. With the surgence of deep learning and large-scale pre-training techniques, various neural information retrieval models are proposed and they have demonstrated the power for improving search (especially, the ranking) quality. All these existing search methods follow a common paradigm, i.e. index-retrieve-rerank, where they first build an index of all documents based on document terms (i.e., sparse inverted index) or representation vectors (i.e., dense vector index), then retrieve and rerank retrieved documents based on similarity between the query and documents via ranking models. In this paper, we explore a new paradigm of information retrieval with neither sparse nor dense index but only a model. Specifically, we propose a pre-training model-based IR system called DynamicRetriever. As for this system, the training stage embeds the token-level and document-level information (especially, document identifiers) of the corpus into the model parameters, then the inference stage directly generates document identifiers for a given query. Compared with existing search methods, the model-based IR system has two advantages: i) it parameterizes the traditional static index with a pre-training model, which converts the document semantic mapping into a dynamic and updatable process; ii) with separate document identifiers, it captures both the term-level and document-level information for each document. Extensive experiments conducted on the public search benchmark MS MARCO verify the effectiveness and potential of our proposed new paradigm for information retrieval.»

It assigns each document a unique identifier, known as the DocID, and employs a generative model to directly generate the relevant DocID for the input query. A common choice for the DocID is one or several natural language sequences, e.g. the title, synthetic queries, or n-grams, so that the pre-trained knowledge of the generative model can be effectively utilized. However, a sequence is generated token by token, where only the most likely candidates are kept and the rest are pruned at each decoding step, thus, retrieval fails if any token within the relevant DocID is falsely pruned. What's worse, during decoding, the model can only perceive preceding tokens in the DocID while being blind to subsequent ones, hence is prone to make such errors.
