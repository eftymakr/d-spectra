## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy-preserving data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for data privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to adversarial behaviors in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Specifically, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This issue has been explored in several works, including those that examine the limitations of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols. For instance, manipulation attacks have been shown to be a fundamental limitation of LDP, where a small fraction of users can obscure the distribution of honest users' input, especially when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Similarly, data poisoning attacks have been demonstrated to allow attackers to inject fake users and manipulate the output of LDP protocols to skew estimations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These findings highlight the need for robust verification mechanisms in DP systems.

Several works have addressed the problem of ensuring correctness in DP mechanisms. For example, the shuffled model of differential privacy has been proposed as a middle ground between the local and central models, offering improved accuracy while maintaining user privacy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This model leverages an anonymous channel to shuffle user inputs, allowing for more efficient and accurate computation of statistics \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, verifiable randomization mechanisms have been introduced to prevent malicious data providers from manipulating their output before sending it to the data collector \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. These mechanisms enable the data collector to verify that the randomization process was executed correctly, thereby preventing output-manipulation attacks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\].

In the context of verifiable DP, zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) have been explored as a potential tool for ensuring the correctness of DP protocols. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as alternative approaches can achieve the same goals with lower computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This has led to the development of novel mechanisms such as the Verifiable Distributed Discrete Laplacian Mechanism (VDDLM), which offers significant improvements in proof generation efficiency compared to existing verifiable differentially private mechanisms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Similarly, an improved solution to Verifiable Randomized Response (VRR) under local DP has been proposed, achieving up to a 5000x reduction in communication costs and the verifier's overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\].

### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-techniques'></a>Local Differential Privacy Techniques: 6 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has been extensively studied for its ability to protect user privacy in distributed settings, where data is perturbed locally before being sent to an untrusted aggregator \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. However, LDP protocols are vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can distort the aggregated results significantly, especially under high privacy parameters or large domain sizes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This vulnerability has led to the development of more robust protocols that aim to mitigate such manipulation while maintaining privacy guarantees \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, researchers have explored the relationship between LDP and central differential privacy, showing that under certain conditions, LDP algorithms can achieve stronger privacy guarantees in the central model, which could imply lower actual privacy costs than previously assumed \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Furthermore, the security of LDP protocols has been a growing concern, with attacks such as data poisoning being demonstrated, where adversaries inject fake users to manipulate the output of LDP mechanisms \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. To address these issues, post-processing techniques have been proposed to improve the accuracy of LDP-based frequency estimation by leveraging constraints such as non-negative frequencies and normalization \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance both the utility and security of LDP protocols in real-world applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\].

### <a id='category-privacy-enhancing-techniques'></a>Privacy Enhancing Techniques: 2 papers found.
The category "Privacy Enhancing Techniques" encompasses a range of methods aimed at improving data privacy while maintaining utility in data analysis. Recent studies highlight the vulnerabilities of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols, where malicious data providers can manipulate their outputs to skew estimations, exploiting the normalization process defined in the LDP protocol \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms that allow data collectors to verify the completeness of randomization execution, thereby preventing output-manipulation attacks and mitigating potential damage \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, the local model of differential privacy, while offering increased privacy by not requiring a trusted data collector, suffers from reduced utility and algorithmic expressibility compared to the central model \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. In response, systems like Cryptε have been developed to bridge this gap by enabling the execution of DP programs on encrypted data using two non-colluding untrusted servers, achieving the accuracy and expressibility of the central model without the need for a trusted data collector \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. These advancements reflect ongoing efforts to enhance privacy mechanisms while balancing their practical feasibility and performance.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-techniques'></a>Differential Privacy Techniques: 3 papers found.
The shuffled model of differential privacy offers a middle ground between the local and central models, enabling accurate computation of statistics without requiring trust in a central server \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes user messages, allowing for greater accuracy than the local model while maintaining user privacy \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Research has shown that the shuffled model can achieve the accuracy of the central model while avoiding its trust assumptions, and it provides a strictly greater power than the local model in certain scenarios \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Additionally, recent work has demonstrated that the shuffled model can enable efficient histogram estimation with error independent of domain size, highlighting its potential for practical applications \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. However, the shuffled model is not without limitations, as it is not equivalent to the local model under certain constraints such as pure differential privacy and single-message randomizers \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. These findings underscore the importance of exploring the shuffled model as a promising direction for achieving both privacy and utility in distributed settings \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\].
