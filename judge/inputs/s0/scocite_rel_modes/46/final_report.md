## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in traditional sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges, primarily leveraging self-attention mechanisms and domain-specific components to improve recommendation accuracy. For instance, DA-GCN introduces a domain-aware graph convolution network to capture user-specific node representations and employs attention mechanisms to guide message passing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Similarly, DASL proposes a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains to enhance cross-domain CTR predictions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. These models aim to improve performance by explicitly incorporating domain-specific components, yet they often overlook the inherent power of the self-attention module in transformers \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\].

The self-attention mechanism in transformers has been widely adopted in sequential recommendation models, such as SASRec and BERT4Rec, to capture long-term dependencies and contextual relationships in user behavior sequences \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, these models typically focus on single-domain settings and do not fully exploit cross-domain interactions. To address this, several works have explored cross-domain knowledge transfer, such as CoNet, which uses deep transfer learning to enable dual knowledge transfer across domains \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Additionally, π-Net and PSJNet tackle the shared-account cross-domain setting by introducing mechanisms to distinguish between different user roles and domains \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\].

Recent approaches have also focused on modeling the temporal dynamics of user interactions, such as TiSASRec, which explicitly incorporates time intervals between interactions to improve next-item prediction \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Similarly, the mixed information flow network considers both behavioral and knowledge flows between domains to enhance recommendation performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of integrating multiple sources of information to improve cross-domain recommendations.

Despite these advancements, existing CDSR frameworks often rely on manual design of domain-specific components, which can be computationally expensive and less flexible \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\]. Moreover, they may suffer from negative transfer when transferring knowledge between domains \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. To overcome these limitations, AutoCDSR proposes a novel approach that enhances the self-attention module by formulating cross-domain learning as a multi-objective optimization problem, dynamically minimizing cross-domain attention scores while optimizing the recommendation task \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. This approach enables automated knowledge transfer and encourages complementary knowledge exchange between domains \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. AutoCDSR+ further improves upon this by introducing a more performant variant with minimal additional computational overhead \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\].

### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation'></a>Cross Domain Sequential Recommendation: 21 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research area to address data sparsity and cold-start problems in traditional sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Existing approaches often rely on RNN-based models to mine sequential patterns, but these methods struggle to capture complex relationships among multiple entities and domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. To overcome these limitations, researchers have proposed graph-based solutions such as DA-GCN, which constructs domain-specific graphs and employs attention mechanisms to enhance message passing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Similarly, the Dual Attentive Sequential Learning (DASL) model introduces dual embeddings and attention mechanisms to simultaneously transfer information between domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Another approach, C2DSR, leverages both intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Transfer learning methods, such as Collaborative Cross Networks (CoNet), have also been explored to enable dual knowledge transfer across domains \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Furthermore, the Adaptive Multi-Interest Debiasing framework (AMID) addresses open-world assumptions by incorporating multi-interest information and doubly robust estimators to reduce biases across domains \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. These methods highlight the importance of modeling both behavioral and knowledge flows across domains \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\]. Additionally, the mixed information flow network considers both behavioral and knowledge transfer to improve recommendation performance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. The Parallel Information-sharing Network (π-Net) addresses shared-account and cross-domain challenges by integrating shared account filtering and cross-domain transfer units \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. The RecGURU algorithm generates generalized user representations across domains using adversarial learning \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. The BiTGCF model improves cross-domain recommendation by enabling bidirectional knowledge transfer through a graph collaborative filtering network \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]. These diverse approaches demonstrate the evolving strategies to enhance cross-domain sequential recommendation by addressing domain-specific challenges and improving model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\].

### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-models'></a>Sequential Recommendation Models: 7 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models have evolved significantly, incorporating techniques such as Markov Chains (MCs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), and self-attention mechanisms to better capture user behavior dynamics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Early approaches relied on MCs, which assume that a user's next action depends only on their last few actions, making them suitable for sparse datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. However, RNNs offer greater flexibility by modeling longer-term dependencies, though they often struggle with computational efficiency and sparse data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. More recently, self-attention based models like SASRec have emerged, combining the efficiency of MCs with the expressive power of RNNs by selectively focusing on relevant historical actions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. These models have been further refined with bidirectional architectures, such as BERT4Rec, which leverages deep bidirectional self-attention to capture both left and right context in user behavior sequences, improving recommendation accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Additionally, session-based recommendation models like NARM have been developed to address the challenge of modeling user intent within short sessions, using hybrid encoders and attention mechanisms to capture both sequential behavior and user purpose \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Researchers have also explored self-supervised learning approaches, such as S3-Rec, which utilize intrinsic data correlations to enhance representation learning and mitigate data sparsity issues \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Moreover, models like TiSASRec have been introduced to explicitly account for temporal dynamics by incorporating time intervals between interactions, improving the accuracy of next-item predictions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to balance model complexity, computational efficiency, and the ability to capture meaningful patterns in sequential user behavior.
