## Related Works

Dense retrieval (DR) has emerged as a powerful paradigm in information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT and LLaMA to encode text into dense representations that can be compared using simple similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. These representations are typically fine-tuned via contrastive learning to improve retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Recent research has questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in DR, arguing that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning does not significantly alter this knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. This claim was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) as a representative DR system \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. However, this analysis was limited to a specific model architecture and dataset, prompting further investigation into other representation approaches, backbone architectures, and datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different fine-tuning strategies for DR. For example, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14218)\] introduced coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00635)\] proposed ReContriever, which improves upon the SOTA unsupervised Contriever model by introducing relevance-aware contrastive learning, adaptively weighing the contrastive loss based on estimated relevance. These approaches highlight the importance of contrastive learning in DR and the potential for improving retrieval performance through more sophisticated fine-tuning strategies.

The use of different representation methods, such as CLS token versus mean pooling, has also been explored. For instance, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)\] showed that the CLS token, commonly used in BERT-based models, may not always yield the best retrieval performance, while mean pooling can lead to more discriminative representations. This was further validated in the context of the Contriever model, which uses mean pooling to generate more robust and discriminative representations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. Similarly, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\] demonstrated that the use of a lightweight multi-layer perception (MLP) decoder in autoencoder-based models can enhance the discriminative ability of learned representations, leading to better retrieval performance.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-in-retrieval-models'></a>Contrastive Learning in Retrieval Models: 5 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a critical technique in improving dense retrieval models by enhancing the discriminative power of text representations. Recent studies have shown that pre-trained autoencoder-based language models with weak decoders can significantly boost retrieval performance, but they face challenges such as the bypass effect and limited discriminative ability due to equal token weighting \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed contrastive pre-training approaches that generate word distributions in a non-autoregressive manner, pulling masked versions of the same text closer while pushing away from others, thereby suppressing common words and highlighting representative ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Additionally, contrastive learning has been applied to inject dictionary knowledge into pre-trained language models, improving tasks like NER and CommonsenseQA through tasks such as dictionary entry prediction and entry description discrimination \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00635)\]. Another line of work focuses on refining the embedding space by introducing dual training objectives, such as in the DANCE framework, which enhances query representations by aligning with document embeddings and maintaining a uniform distribution \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. Furthermore, contrastive learning has been adapted to improve typo-robustness in dense retrievers by leveraging multiple positive samples (typoed queries) simultaneously, leading to better performance in handling noisy inputs \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.10939)\]. These approaches collectively demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of contrastive learning in refining dense retrieval models, addressing limitations in representation quality, knowledge injection, and robustness to input variations.

### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-in-dense-retrieval'></a>Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval: 7 papers found.
Recent research has focused on improving dense retrievers by leveraging pre-trained language models (LMs) and addressing their limitations in training and robustness. Studies have shown that dense retrievers, which encode text into dense vectors for efficient retrieval, often require extensive fine-tuning pipelines and are sensitive to training data noise \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. To mitigate these issues, approaches like Condenser and coCondenser have been proposed, where Condenser pre-trains LMs to condense information into dense vectors, and coCondenser adds a contrastive loss to improve passage embeddings without requiring large batches or data engineering \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, SimLM introduces a bottleneck architecture that compresses passage information into dense vectors through self-supervised pre-training, achieving strong performance with minimal labeled data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. Additionally, the challenge of handling misspelled queries has been addressed with ToRoDer, a pre-training strategy that enhances robustness to out-of-distribution inputs while maintaining retrieval effectiveness \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08138)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve dense retrievers through better pre-training strategies, reduced dependency on large batches, and enhanced robustness to real-world query variations.

### <a id='category-fine-tuning-vs.-pre-training-knowledge-acquisition'></a>Fine-tuning vs. Pre-training Knowledge Acquisition: 9 papers found.
Fine-tuning pre-trained language models has been extensively studied for its impact on model performance and knowledge acquisition, with varying outcomes across different architectures and tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. While fine-tuning is commonly used to adapt models to downstream tasks, recent research suggests that it may alter the internal representations of pre-trained models, potentially introducing or removing linguistic knowledge \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. This effect is not uniform across models; for instance, fine-tuning can lead to significant changes in probing accuracy for some models like BERT, RoBERTa, and ALBERT, while other models may show less sensitivity to these changes \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. In cross-lingual settings, fine-tuning can weaken the cross-lingual ability of pre-trained models, but methods like continual learning have been proposed to preserve this ability during fine-tuning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14218)\]. Similarly, in knowledge-intensive tasks, unsupervised fine-tuning often underperforms compared to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which consistently outperforms fine-tuning in both learning existing and new factual knowledge \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)\]. Additionally, the relationship between pre-training and fine-tuning is complex, with continual pre-training improving model performance in a latent way that becomes evident after fine-tuning, and fine-tuning sometimes leading to forgetting of previously learned domain knowledge \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06663)\]. Adversarial training for robustness has also been explored, with methods like Robust Informative Fine-Tuning (RIFT) designed to retain pre-trained linguistic features during fine-tuning, showing improved performance on tasks like sentiment analysis and natural language inference \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.11668)\]. These findings highlight the nuanced effects of fine-tuning on model knowledge and performance, emphasizing the need for careful interpretation of its impacts \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\].
