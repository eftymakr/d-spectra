## Related Works

Economic inequality has been a focal point for researchers and policymakers, with various models and frameworks proposed to address its complexities. Traditional economic models, such as those based on neoclassical theories, often assume rational agents and static behavioral rules, which may not capture the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of real-world economic systems \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. In contrast, Complex Adaptive Systems (CAS) frameworks have gained traction for their ability to model economic agents as boundedly rational and adaptive, leading to more realistic simulations of economic phenomena \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. Agent-Based Models (ABMs) have been particularly useful in this regard, as they allow for the exploration of how micro-level interactions can lead to macro-level outcomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\].

The study of tax evasion has also been a significant area of research, with early models like the Allingham and Sandmo framework incorporating tax rates, penalties, and audit probabilities to evaluate taxpayer behavior \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. However, these models often fail to account for the social and network dynamics that influence tax compliance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. Agent-based models have since been proposed as a more robust alternative, enabling the simulation of interactions among taxpayers, tax advisors, and authorities \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. These models can incorporate features such as backauditing and endogenous penalty rates, which enhance the realism of tax compliance simulations \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\].

In the context of policy design, ABMs have been used to evaluate the impact of different tax policies and redistribution strategies on wealth inequality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08573)\]. For instance, studies have shown that well-designed income redistribution policies and strict control over tax evasion can significantly reduce wealth inequality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08573)\]. These findings align with the broader literature on economic inequality, which emphasizes the need for adaptive and context-sensitive policies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\].

Recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) have also contributed to the development of more sophisticated ABMs. RL models have been integrated into ABMs to improve the accuracy of agent behavior, particularly in policy analysis scenarios \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. These models can learn from past data and adapt to changing environments, making them valuable tools for simulating economic systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. Furthermore, the use of RL in ABMs has enabled the exploration of heterogeneous agent behaviors and the emergence of complex economic patterns \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\].

While LLMs have been primarily used for language generation, recent studies have explored their potential in agent-based systems, particularly in improving reasoning and decision-making capabilities \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. ### <a id='category-complex-adaptive-systems-and-agent-based-models'></a>Complex Adaptive Systems and Agent-Based Models: 6 papers found.
The category "Complex Adaptive Systems and Agent-Based Models" explores the application of agent-based modeling (ABM) to economic and social systems, emphasizing the adaptive and heterogeneous nature of economic agents. Research in this area highlights the limitations of traditional economic models, which often rely on mechanistic and reductionist approaches, failing to capture the dynamic and evolutionary interactions among agents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. In contrast, ABMs are increasingly used to simulate complex economic phenomena, such as inequality and poverty, by incorporating heterogeneous agents with boundedly rational behaviors \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. These models allow for the exploration of how local interactions among agents can lead to macro-level patterns, such as the emergence of economic inequality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. Moreover, studies have demonstrated the potential of ABMs to evaluate and design policies, such as tax credit distribution and poverty reduction strategies, by simulating the impact of different policy interventions on agent behavior and system outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. For instance, one study proposes an innovative government policy for distributing tax credits, leveraging insights from spending patterns to reduce disparities and improve social welfare \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]. Another study presents ABIDES-Economist, an agent-based simulator that integrates reinforcement learning to model heterogeneous agents, including households, firms, and the central bank, thereby enhancing the realism and adaptability of economic simulations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. These contributions underscore the growing importance of ABMs in addressing real-world economic challenges through data-driven and adaptive policy design.

### <a id='category-tax-evasion-and-compliance-models'></a>Tax Evasion and Compliance Models: 3 papers found.
The category "Tax Evasion and Compliance Models" explores various methodologies to understand and model taxpayer behavior, focusing on the interplay between individual decision-making and societal structures. Research in this area often employs agent-based models to simulate the dynamics of tax compliance, incorporating factors such as ethical commitment, social influence, and enforcement mechanisms \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. These models are grounded in economic theories, such as the Allingham and Sandmo framework, which initially attempted to predict tax compliance based on utility maximization but was criticized for underestimating observed compliance levels in real-world settings \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. To address these limitations, scholars have turned to more sophisticated models, including the Ising model from statistical mechanics, which allows for the simulation of complex social interactions and the analysis of large, heterogeneous agent populations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1112.0233)\]. These models incorporate elements like social temperature, external fields, and multi-dimensional interactions to better reflect the nuanced behaviors of taxpayers and the impact of enforcement strategies \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1112.0233)\]. Additionally, the role of backauditing and endogenously determined penalty rates has been emphasized as crucial for enhancing the realism and predictive power of these models \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. By integrating these elements, researchers aim to provide a more accurate representation of tax evasion dynamics and inform policy decisions aimed at improving compliance and reducing inequality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\].

Furthermore, RL behavioral models can learn to outperform the default adaptive behavioral models in the two ABMs examined.»

### <a id='category-integration-of-large-language-models-in-agent-based-systems'></a>Integration of Large Language Models in Agent-Based Systems: 4 papers found.
The integration of large language models (LLMs) in agent-based systems has been explored in various contexts, including tax policy modeling and agent training. Recent research highlights the potential of LLMs to enhance agent performance by improving reasoning capabilities through techniques like chain-of-thought (COT) fine-tuning \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the misalignment between model assessments and actual performance, which can lead to suboptimal reasoning outcomes \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. To address this, Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) has been proposed, incorporating a constraint alignment loss to ensure high-quality COTs are prioritized while maintaining reasonable performance on suboptimal responses \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Additionally, the use of LLMs as agents interacting with environments through tools like search engines has been examined, revealing limitations in their ability to effectively utilize tools during training \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11651)\]. To overcome these challenges, researchers have explored the value of incorporating unsuccessful trajectories into training, using prefixes or suffixes to guide model generation toward successful outcomes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11651)\]. Furthermore, efforts to make LLMs more human-like have focused on enhancing natural language understanding, conversational coherence, and emotional intelligence, which could improve user interactions in agent-based systems \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05032)\]. In the context of tax policy, LLMs have been leveraged to automate the maintenance of tax preparation software by extracting code differentials from IRS publications, offering a more efficient and accurate approach to updating tax regulations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\]. These developments underscore the growing interest in integrating LLMs into agent-based systems to improve adaptability, reasoning, and user interaction.
