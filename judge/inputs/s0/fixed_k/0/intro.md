## Related Works

Economic inequality has been a focal point for researchers and policymakers, with various models and frameworks proposed to address its complexities. Traditional economic models, such as those based on neoclassical theories, often assume rational agents and static behavioral rules, which may not capture the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of real-world economic systems \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. In contrast, Complex Adaptive Systems (CAS) frameworks have gained traction for their ability to model economic agents as boundedly rational and adaptive, leading to more realistic simulations of economic phenomena \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\]. Agent-Based Models (ABMs) have been particularly useful in this regard, as they allow for the exploration of how micro-level interactions can lead to macro-level outcomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\].

The study of tax evasion has also been a significant area of research, with early models like the Allingham and Sandmo framework incorporating tax rates, penalties, and audit probabilities to evaluate taxpayer behavior \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. However, these models often fail to account for the social and network dynamics that influence tax compliance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. Agent-based models have since been proposed as a more robust alternative, enabling the simulation of interactions among taxpayers, tax advisors, and authorities \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\]. These models can incorporate features such as backauditing and endogenous penalty rates, which enhance the realism of tax compliance simulations \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.7000)\].

In the context of policy design, ABMs have been used to evaluate the impact of different tax policies and redistribution strategies on wealth inequality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08573)\]. For instance, studies have shown that well-designed income redistribution policies and strict control over tax evasion can significantly reduce wealth inequality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08573)\]. These findings align with the broader literature on economic inequality, which emphasizes the need for adaptive and context-sensitive policies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\].

Recent advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) have also contributed to the development of more sophisticated ABMs. RL models have been integrated into ABMs to improve the accuracy of agent behavior, particularly in policy analysis scenarios \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. These models can learn from past data and adapt to changing environments, making them valuable tools for simulating economic systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. Furthermore, the use of RL in ABMs has enabled the exploration of heterogeneous agent behaviors and the emergence of complex economic patterns \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\].

The integration of large language models (LLMs) with ABMs represents a novel approach to designing adaptive tax policies. While LLMs have been primarily used for language generation, recent studies have explored their potential in agent-based systems, particularly in improving reasoning and decision-making capabilities \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\].
