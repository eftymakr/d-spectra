## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction from 2D images, leveraging implicit neural representations to model continuous 3D scenes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. These methods typically take multiple views as input and optimize the reconstructed scene by minimizing geometric and appearance uncertainty across views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often constrained by the number of input views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. In complex and dynamic scenes, where certain angles of objects are never captured, this limitation becomes more pronounced \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. To address this, several works have explored the use of video frame interpolation as a data augmentation strategy for neural rendering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\].

Video frame interpolation has been extensively studied, with various approaches focusing on improving the quality and efficiency of intermediate frame synthesis. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation followed by pixel synthesis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. More recent approaches have integrated these steps into a single convolutional process, using spatially adaptive kernels to account for motion and re-sampling simultaneously \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These methods, however, often require large kernels to handle large motion, which increases memory demands and limits scalability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. To address this, adaptive separable convolutions have been proposed, reducing the number of parameters and enabling efficient frame interpolation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\].

Another line of research has focused on leveraging neural fields for video interpolation, enabling continuous scene representation and spatial-temporal consistency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. The CURE method, for instance, represents videos as neural fields parameterized by spatiotemporal coordinates, allowing for high-quality interpolation across multiple videos \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. Additionally, methods such as the Video Frame Interpolation Transformer have introduced attention mechanisms to better capture long-range dependencies and content-aware aggregation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. These approaches have demonstrated significant improvements in both quantitative and qualitative metrics \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\].

Furthermore, the use of depth-aware techniques has been explored to improve the quality of frame interpolation, particularly in scenarios involving large object motion or occlusion \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. By explicitly detecting occlusion and leveraging depth information, these methods can synthesize more accurate intermediate frames \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Similarly, motion-aware event-based video frame interpolation has been proposed to enhance visual quality by emphasizing moving regions using appropriate loss functions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\].

In addition to these methods, several works have focused on improving the efficiency and robustness of frame interpolation models. For example, the FILM method introduces a single unified network that can handle large motion by optimizing with a Gram matrix loss \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. This approach outperforms state-of-the-art methods on benchmark datasets, demonstrating its effectiveness in synthesizing high-quality, temporally coherent videos \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\].

The category "Implicit World Prior for 3D Reconstruction" encompasses a range of approaches that leverage implicit neural representations to enhance 3D reconstruction from limited or sparse input data. These methods often incorporate scene priors, learned from large-scale datasets, to improve the robustness and quality of reconstructions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. For instance, one study introduces a feature grid interpolation mechanism to continuously update object-centric neural implicit representations as new views are processed, enabling efficient online reconstruction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. Another approach utilizes a sparse but accurate depth prior to anchor the scene during reconstruction, while a dense but less accurate prior allows for flexibility in diverging from initial estimates \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. Additionally, methods such as NFP (Neural Fields incorporating scene Priors) demonstrate the ability to reconstruct complete scenes from single-view RGB-D images by merging individual frames in volumetric space without a fusion module, offering flexibility and scalability \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. Similarly, CoCo-INR integrates prior information into coordinate-based networks through attention modules, enhancing feature representation and enabling high-quality reconstructions with fewer input views \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11170)\]. These techniques highlight the growing trend of using implicit models and scene priors to overcome the limitations of traditional methods, particularly in scenarios with limited or uncalibrated views \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11599)\]. By incorporating learned priors and regularization strategies, these approaches achieve better geometry and appearance consistency, even in challenging conditions like occlusions or sparse observations \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.06860)\]. Overall, the category emphasizes the potential of implicit neural representations combined with scene priors to advance 3D reconstruction in both synthetic and real-world settings.

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-techniques'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Techniques: 16 papers found.
Video frame interpolation techniques have seen significant advancements, particularly with the integration of deep learning approaches. Traditional methods typically involve separate steps for motion estimation and pixel synthesis, which can limit performance due to reliance on optical flow \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent work has aimed to unify these steps, such as through deep fully convolutional neural networks that estimate spatially adaptive kernels for pixel synthesis \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. These models often leverage local separable convolutions to reduce parameter count and computational demand \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Additionally, some methods employ splatting-based synthesis, which enables faster and more efficient frame interpolation, especially at high resolutions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. Others focus on unsupervised learning, using cycle consistency to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring ground truth data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\]. Transformer-based frameworks have also emerged, offering content-aware aggregation and long-range dependency modeling through self-attention mechanisms \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13817)\]. Furthermore, there are efforts to improve the efficiency and interpretability of these models, such as through explainable neural networks that derive interpolation filters directly \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08936)\]. Some approaches utilize event-based cameras to enhance interpolation quality by focusing on moving regions \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.02025)\], while others address challenges like large motion and varying exposure times by developing general motion trajectory formulas \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.01161)\]. These diverse methods highlight the ongoing evolution in video frame interpolation, aiming to balance quality, efficiency, and adaptability across different applications.
