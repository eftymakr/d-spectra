## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area in the context of privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in response to the "right to be forgotten" regulation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. This concept allows users to request the removal of their data from the global model trained across distributed clients, without requiring full retraining \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) is a promising paradigm that integrates federated learning with online learning to rank, enabling continuous model updates while preserving user privacy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. However, FOLTR faces challenges in effectively and securely unlearning client contributions, especially due to the dynamic nature of online learning and the potential for data distribution inconsistencies across clients \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19218)\].

Several studies have explored federated unlearning, focusing on its effectiveness, efficiency, and security. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\] introduces a comprehensive framework for federated unlearning, emphasizing the need for mechanisms that allow the removal of specific clients' contributions without retraining the entire model. This is crucial for maintaining model performance while respecting privacy constraints. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\] presents a survey on federated unlearning, highlighting the challenges of unlearning in distributed settings, such as the need for efficient communication and the risk of malicious clients injecting backdoors into the global model.

To address these challenges, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12502)\] proposes a novel approach called FedRemoval, which enables the server to unlearn a target client without requiring client communication or additional storage. This method is certified and secure, making it a promising solution for federated unlearning. Additionally, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02216)\] introduces the concept of verifiable federated unlearning, where the unlearning effect can be independently verified, ensuring compliance with legal and ethical standards. This is particularly important in scenarios where users need to confirm that their data has been successfully removed from the model.

Another significant contribution is the development of evaluation metrics for federated unlearning. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\] presents a detailed analysis of existing methods, emphasizing the need for multiple metrics to comprehensively assess unlearning success. This aligns with the findings of \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\], which highlights the limitations of single-metric evaluations and advocates for a more holistic approach to measuring unlearning effectiveness.

### <a id='category-federated-learning-and-online-learning-to-rank-(foltr)'></a>Federated Learning and Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR): 6 papers found.
Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) combines the principles of federated learning (FL) and online learning to rank (OLTR) to address privacy concerns in collaborative ranking model training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Unlike traditional OLTR methods that rely on static datasets with human annotations, FOLTR updates models continuously using implicit user feedback, such as clicks, while preserving user privacy by not sharing raw data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. FL, which enables distributed training without exposing private data, has been widely studied for its privacy benefits, though it remains vulnerable to various privacy threats \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00342)\]. Recent research has focused on improving FL's performance, interpretability, and fairness, especially in critical domains like finance and healthcare \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13473)\]. Additionally, FL's application in the Internet-of-Things (IoT) has been explored to train models on decentralized devices, reducing the risk of data leakage \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11794)\]. Challenges such as non-i.i.d. data, disjoint classes, and signal multi-modality have been addressed through novel methods that aggregate generic model parameters on the server while maintaining client-specific parameters \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06393)\]. Furthermore, FL has been augmented with per-user domain adaptation to improve model accuracy, particularly under differential privacy constraints \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06733)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in FOLTR as a privacy-preserving approach to ranking, balancing effectiveness with user data protection.

### <a id='category-unlearning-methods-in-federated-settings'></a>Unlearning Methods in Federated Settings: 17 papers found.
The category "Unlearning Methods in Federated Settings" explores the challenges and advancements in enabling federated unlearning (FU), which allows for the removal of specific client data contributions from a global model without full retraining. This is critical for compliance with privacy regulations such as the GDPR and the "right to be forgotten," which require mechanisms for data erasure \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Federated unlearning faces unique challenges due to the distributed nature of federated learning (FL), including issues like privacy, accuracy, and efficiency trade-offs, as well as the need for secure and efficient unlearning strategies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Several studies have proposed novel approaches to address these challenges, such as the development of frameworks like OPENFEDERATEDUNLEARNING for benchmarking and evaluating federated unlearning methods \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19218)\], and FedEraser, which leverages historical parameter updates to reconstruct unlearned models efficiently \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02216)\]. Additionally, methods like FUSED have been introduced to improve the reversibility and cost-effectiveness of unlearning by using sparse adapters and knowledge overwriting \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15474)\]. Other works, such as FedAU, focus on lightweight and efficient unlearning modules that enable granular unlearning at the client or data sample level \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\]. Furthermore, the concept of "plausible deniability" has been explored to allow servers to deny client participation in training, enhancing privacy \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09947)\]. These efforts highlight the growing interest in developing robust, efficient, and privacy-preserving federated unlearning techniques that align with both legal requirements and practical constraints \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20709)\].

### <a id='category-security-and-verification-in-federated-unlearning'></a>Security and Verification in Federated Unlearning: 3 papers found.
The category "Security and Verification in Federated Unlearning" focuses on the challenges and solutions related to ensuring data privacy and verifiability in federated learning environments, particularly in the context of implementing the "right to be forgotten" (RTBF). This involves developing mechanisms that allow users to request the deletion of their data from a global model and verify that the unlearning process was successful. Current approaches often lack robust verification methods, as unlearning effects can be undermined by contributions from other participants in the federated system \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed frameworks like VeriFi, which introduces a systematic way to analyze and quantify unlearning effects, along with verification techniques that enable users to independently confirm the success of unlearning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. Additionally, other works have explored backdoor-based verification mechanisms, where users inject specific triggers into their data to monitor whether their data is still influencing the model \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04247)\]. These methods aim to provide high-confidence verification with minimal impact on model performance \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04247)\]. Furthermore, security challenges in federated unlearning have been identified, such as poisoning attacks where malicious clients attempt to retain influence over the global model during unlearning \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17396)\]. To counteract these threats, frameworks like UnlearnGuard have been introduced, which employ filtering strategies to ensure the accuracy of local model updates and maintain model integrity \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17396)\]. Overall, this category highlights the growing need for secure, verifiable, and efficient unlearning methods in federated learning systems to align with privacy regulations and user expectations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04247)\]\[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17396)\].

### <a id='category-ethical-and-compliance-considerations'></a>Ethical and Compliance Considerations: 2 papers found.
The "Right to be Forgotten" has emerged as a critical ethical and compliance consideration in machine learning, particularly in the context of data privacy and model accountability \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\]. This principle, enshrined in regulations like the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), empowers individuals to request the deletion of their personal data from trained models \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. While naive retraining on updated datasets is a straightforward method for achieving this, it is computationally infeasible for frequent or large-scale data removal requests \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. Consequently, researchers have developed efficient unlearning methods that approximate the performance of retraining without requiring full retraining \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\]. However, these methods often overlook the impact of unlearning on fairness and other critical model properties \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. Recent work has begun to address these limitations by proposing fair unlearning approaches that maintain both the effectiveness of unlearning and the fairness of the model \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. Additionally, the need for robust evaluation metrics has been highlighted, as current practices often rely on single metrics, leading to incomplete assessments of unlearning success \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\]. This has spurred the development of more comprehensive evaluation frameworks that consider multiple dimensions of model performance \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\]. The integration of these considerations into federated learning systems, such as Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR), further underscores the importance of aligning unlearning strategies with both privacy and fairness goals \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\].

### <a id='category-evaluation-metrics-for-unlearning'></a>Evaluation Metrics for Unlearning: 2 papers found.
The category "Evaluation Metrics for Unlearning" highlights the critical need for reliable and comprehensive evaluation frameworks to assess the success of unlearning in machine learning models. Recent research has emphasized that existing evaluation practices often rely on either adversarial attacks or comparisons to retrained models, which may not accurately reflect the true effectiveness of unlearning \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10836)\]. This limitation has spurred the development of new metrics that better capture the nuances of unlearning, particularly in complex models like diffusion models used for text-to-image generation \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05668)\]. For instance, the Concept Retrieval Score (CRS) and Concept Confidence Score (CCS) have been introduced to evaluate the extent to which targeted concepts are successfully erased from models, using adversarial attack setups to recover potentially forgotten concepts \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05668)\]. These metrics provide a more robust and verifiable way to assess unlearning, addressing the shortcomings of previous single-metric approaches. Additionally, the focus on epistemic uncertainty as a basis for evaluation metrics offers a novel perspective, aiming to quantify the model's confidence in its predictions after unlearning \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10836)\]. These developments underscore the ongoing efforts to refine evaluation practices, ensuring that unlearning methods align with the goals of data privacy and user control, as mandated by regulations like GDPR and CCPA \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.10836)\].
