## Related Works

Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention due to its ability to address the cold-start and data sparsity issues prevalent in single-domain sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Traditional CDSR approaches typically rely on overlapping users to transfer knowledge between domains, which limits their applicability in real-world scenarios where such users are rare \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13588)\]. Moreover, these methods often struggle with capturing complex transition patterns from mixed behavior sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Recent studies have explored the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into CDSR to enhance semantic understanding and improve cross-domain preference modeling \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19862)\].

Several works have attempted to leverage LLMs for recommendation tasks. For instance, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07761)\] explores the use of LLMs in cross-domain recommendation (CDR) by introducing prompt designs tailored for CDR tasks, demonstrating that LLMs can outperform state-of-the-art CDR baselines across various metrics and domain combinations. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\] proposes LLM4CDR, a novel CDR pipeline that constructs context-aware prompts using users' purchase history sequences and shared features between source and target domains. This approach achieves strong performance, particularly when using large LLMs and when the source and target domains have smaller domain gaps \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Additionally, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\] investigates how key factors such as source domain data, domain gap, prompt design, and LLM size impact LLM4CDR's effectiveness in CDR tasks, highlighting that LLM4CDR excels when leveraging a single, closely related source domain and benefits significantly from larger LLMs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving the integration of LLMs into sequential recommendation systems. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\] introduces DARec, an LLM-based sequential recommendation model that incorporates context masking to model intra-item relations and collaborative knowledge injection to incorporate long-term collaborative knowledge. This model is further enhanced with a dynamic adaptation mechanism that uses Bayesian optimization to flexibly choose layer-wise adapter architectures \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\]. Similarly, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\] proposes SLMRec, which distills large language models into smaller models for sequential recommendation, achieving up to 6.6x and 8.0x speedups in training and inference time while maintaining strong performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].

In addition to LLM-based approaches, several studies have focused on improving cross-domain recommendation through graph-based and contrastive learning techniques. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.07427)\] introduces C2DSR, which simultaneously leverages intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to capture precise user preferences. This method uses graph neural networks

### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation-(cdsr)-overview'></a>Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) Overview: 6 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research area aimed at addressing data sparsity and cold-start problems by leveraging interactions across multiple domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04971)\]. Existing methods often rely on overlapping users with abundant behaviors to propagate cross-domain information, but this approach struggles in real-world scenarios where most users are long-tailed or cold-start users with sparse interactions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04760)\]. To overcome these limitations, recent works have introduced frameworks that adapt to open-world assumptions, such as the Adaptive Multi-Interest Debiasing (AMID) framework, which enhances model performance by considering both overlapping and non-overlapping users and mitigating domain biases \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. Additionally, methods like Image Fusion for Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (IFCDSR) have incorporated visual data to better capture user preferences, using frozen CLIP models to generate image embeddings and multiple attention layers to model cross-domain interests \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15694)\]. Furthermore, federated learning approaches such as FedDCSR have been proposed to preserve data privacy while enabling cross-domain knowledge sharing, addressing sequence feature heterogeneity through disentangled representation learning \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08420)\]. These advancements highlight the growing focus on improving model adaptability, effectiveness, and privacy in CDSR.

[Snippet]: «Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) is a hot topic in sequence-based user interest modeling, which aims at utilizing a single model to predict the next items for different domains. To tackle the CDSR, many methods are focused on domain overlapped users' behaviors fitting, which heavily relies on the same user's different-domain item sequences collaborating signals to capture the synergy of cross-domain item-item correlation. Indeed, these overlapped users occupy a small fraction of the entire user set only, which introduces a strong assumption that the small group of domain overlapped users is enough to represent all domain user behavior characteristics. Further, it is not trivial to model non-overlapped user behaviors in CDSR because there are no other domain behaviors to collaborate with, which causes the observed single-domain users' behavior sequences to be hard to contribute to cross-domain knowledge mining. Considering such a phenomenon, we raise a challenging and unexplored question: How to unleash the potential of non-overlapped users' behaviors to empower CDSR?»

[Snippet]: «Recent cross-domain recommendation (CDR) studies assume that disentangled domain-shared and domain-specific user representations can mitigate domain gaps and facilitate effective knowledge transfer. However, achieving perfect disentanglement is challenging in practice, because user behaviors in CDR are highly complex, and the true underlying user preferences cannot be fully captured through observed user-item interactions alone. Given this impracticability, we instead propose to model (\it joint identifiability) that establishes unique correspondence of user representations across domains, ensuring consistent preference modeling even when user behaviors exhibit shifts in different domains. To achieve this, we introduce a hierarchical user preference modeling framework that organizes user representations by the neural network encoder's depth, allowing separate treatment of shallow and deeper subspaces. In the shallow subspace, our framework models the interest centroids for each user within each domain, probabilistically determining the users' interest belongings and selectively aligning these centroids across domains to ensure fine-grained consistency in domain-irrelevant features. For deeper subspace representations, we enforce joint identifiability by decomposing it into a shared cross-domain stable component and domain-variant components, linked by a bijective transformation for unique correspondence. Empirical studies on real-world CDR tasks with varying domain correlations demonstrate that our method consistently surpasses state-of-the-art, even with weakly correlated tasks, highlighting the importance of joint identifiability in achieving robust CDR.»
