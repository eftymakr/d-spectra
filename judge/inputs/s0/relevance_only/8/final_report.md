## Related Works

The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) has underscored the critical need for robust fairness benchmarks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations and often overlook the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. This limitation is well-documented in the literature, which highlights that conventional fairness measures fail to capture the nuanced differences in model confidence across groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. For instance, models may exhibit high confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups, leading to unfair outcomes that are not detected by traditional metrics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

Several studies have explored the limitations of existing fairness metrics in the context of LLMs. For example, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\] emphasizes that fairness risks cannot be reliably assessed from benchmark performance alone, as results on one prompt dataset may overstate or understate risks for another \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]. This underscores the importance of context-specific fairness evaluation, a principle also echoed in \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\], where the authors argue that fairness evaluation must be grounded in the specific deployment context \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

The issue of fairness in machine learning is not new, and a substantial body of work has been devoted to understanding and mitigating algorithmic bias \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. This includes the development of fairness-enhancing mechanisms such as pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing techniques \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. However, many of these methods are sensitive to dataset composition and preprocessing, indicating that fairness interventions might be more brittle than previously thought \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04422)\]. This aligns with the findings in \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\], which highlight the variability in fairness outcomes across different datasets and model configurations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

In addition to fairness metrics, the design of fair datasets is crucial for evaluating LLMs. Current datasets often suffer from issues related to data size, diversity, and clarity, which can limit the effectiveness of fairness evaluations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. To address this, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\] introduces a new gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset with 31,756 samples, designed to offer a more diverse and suitable benchmark for modern LLMs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. This dataset enables a more fine-grained evaluation of model fairness, particularly in scenarios involving co-reference resolution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

The proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metric, UCerF, builds on these insights by incorporating model uncertainty into fairness evaluation. This approach is inspired by the broader discussion in \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04783)\], which explores the trade-offs between different fairness definitions and highlights the importance of aligning fairness metrics with societal expectations \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04783)\]. UCerF aims to capture the internal bias in model decisions more accurately than conventional fairness measures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

### <a id='category-impact-of-dataset-composition-on-fairness-outcomes'></a>Impact of Dataset Composition on Fairness Outcomes: 7 papers found.
The category "Impact of Dataset Composition on Fairness Outcomes" highlights the critical role that dataset characteristics play in shaping the fairness of machine learning models. Studies show that biased or imbalanced datasets can lead to models that disproportionately disadvantage certain subgroups, even when the models themselves are not explicitly designed to be unfair \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. For instance, research indicates that deep neural networks (DNNs) can exhibit robustness bias, where certain subgroups—often based on sensitive attributes like race or gender—are less robust to adversarial attacks, leading to unfair outcomes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\]. This issue is exacerbated by the complex decision boundaries learned by DNNs, making it challenging to mitigate such biases \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. Similarly, in the context of generative models, the use of biased training data can result in synthetic media that perpetuates existing societal inequalities, as demonstrated by the UnfairFace benchmark, which replicates racial distributions found in common datasets \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13555)\]. Furthermore, the lack of standardized fairness metrics and the variability in dataset composition complicate efforts to evaluate and improve fairness, as seen in medical imaging applications where model selection criteria significantly influence fairness outcomes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]. These findings underscore the necessity of developing more robust and diverse datasets, along with fairness-aware evaluation frameworks, to ensure that machine learning systems operate equitably across different populations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12621)\].
