## Related Works

Nonverbal communication plays a crucial role in human interaction, encompassing gestures, facial expressions, and body language that convey intent and emotion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. However, existing large language models (LLMs) often lack the ability to effectively incorporate these nonverbal elements, limiting their capacity to create fully immersive conversational experiences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. This limitation has been widely recognized in the field of multimodal language analysis, where researchers have explored the integration of nonverbal cues with textual information to enhance the understanding of human conversational utterances \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16427)\]. 

Several studies have attempted to model nonverbal behaviors using multimodal approaches. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\] proposed the Recurrent Attended Variation Embedding Network (RAVEN), which dynamically shifts word representations based on nonverbal cues such as vocal patterns and facial expressions. This work highlights the importance of modeling nonverbal subword sequences to capture the dynamic nature of human communication. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\] introduced the concept of nonverbal alphabets, which are symbols and alphabets that can be communicated through nonverbal means, such as body gestures and eye tracking. These symbols are generated and estimated using multimodal data sources and machine learning methods, demonstrating their potential in applications like synthetic languages and Human-Computer Interfaces \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\].

In addition to modeling nonverbal cues, researchers have also focused on improving the understanding of human communication through multimodal models. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00923)\] proposed the Multi-attention Recurrent Network (MARN), which uses a Multi-attention Block (MAB) to discover interactions between modalities over time and stores them in a hybrid memory structure called the Long-short Term Hybrid Memory (LSTHM). This model has shown state-of-the-art results in multimodal sentiment analysis and emotion recognition \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00923)\]. Furthermore, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16410)\] introduced LENS, a modular approach that leverages large language models (LLMs) to reason over outputs from independent vision modules, enabling the model to perform well in zero- and few-shot object recognition tasks without any multimodal training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16410)\].

The integration of nonverbal cues with text has also been explored in the context of video-based conversation models. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\] introduced Video-ChatGPT, a multimodal model that merges a video-adapted visual encoder with an LLM to understand and generate detailed conversations about videos. This model was trained on a large dataset of video-instruction pairs and evaluated using a quantitative framework to assess its performance in video-based dialogue tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\]. ### <a id='category-limitations-of-existing-llms-in-nonverbal-integration'></a>Limitations of Existing LLMs in Nonverbal Integration: 3 papers found.
Current research highlights significant limitations in existing large language models (LLMs) regarding their ability to effectively integrate nonverbal communication cues such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. These models predominantly rely on language-only or language-dominant approaches, which hinder their capacity to interpret and generate nonverbal social interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16671)\]. For instance, studies show that state-of-the-art video large language models (VideoLLMs) achieve low accuracy, typically ranging from 20-30%, when evaluating their ability to understand nonverbal social cues, while humans score significantly higher at 86% \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. Similarly, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) struggle to comprehend complex cognitive-level semantics, with even fine-tuned models achieving only about 60-70% accuracy in understanding multimodal utterances \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\]. These findings underscore the critical need for more robust models capable of seamlessly integrating verbal and nonverbal information to enhance conversational AI. The introduction of novel datasets such as MimeQA, which leverages mime videos rich in nonverbal interactions, and MMLA, which covers six core dimensions of multimodal semantics, represents a step toward addressing these limitations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\]. However, the gap remains substantial, motivating further research into models that can truly embody social intelligence by effectively interpreting and generating nonverbal human interactions.

### <a id='category-evaluation-frameworks-for-multimodal-models'></a>Evaluation Frameworks for Multimodal Models: 2 papers found.
The category "Evaluation Frameworks for Multimodal Models" encompasses efforts to develop benchmarks and datasets that assess the performance of large multimodal models in complex, real-world scenarios. Recent works have highlighted the limitations of existing benchmarks, which often focus on single-turn interactions or simplified tasks, failing to capture the nuances of multi-modal human-AI conversations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11833)\]. To address these gaps, researchers have introduced comprehensive benchmarks such as MMDU, which evaluates models in multi-turn, multi-image settings with extended context lengths, up to 18k image+text tokens and 27 turns \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11833)\]. Similarly, the LAMM-Dataset and LAMM-Benchmark extend the evaluation of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to 3D point clouds and high-level vision tasks, offering a broader scope for assessing model capabilities across different modalities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. These frameworks emphasize the importance of instruction-tuning datasets and extensible training methods to enable models to handle diverse and complex input modalities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. By providing detailed methodologies for constructing benchmarks and datasets, these works contribute to the development of more robust and adaptable multimodal models capable of engaging in realistic, extended conversations with humans \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11833)\].
