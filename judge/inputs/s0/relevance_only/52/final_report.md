## Related Works

Dense retrieval (DR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to generate high-quality text representations for efficient search. Early approaches relied on pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT, which were fine-tuned using contrastive learning to align query and document embeddings in a shared embedding space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. The success of these models was largely attributed to the ability of PLMs to capture semantic relationships between words and phrases \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in DR has been a topic of debate. Some studies argue that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and fine-tuning mainly adjusts neuron activations rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. This claim was initially tested in the context of BERT-based encoders using Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) as a representative DR model \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. 

Recent research has expanded on this idea by exploring different representation approaches, such as using CLS tokens versus mean pooling, and varying backbone architectures, such as encoder-only BERT versus decoder-only LLaMA \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\]. The results indicate that while pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance in DPR tuning, this pattern does not universally apply to all models. For example, in mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based models like LLaMA, the role of fine-tuning appears to be more significant \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. 

Contrastive pre-training has also been explored as a way to improve the discriminative ability of autoencoder-based language models for dense retrieval. Techniques such as contrastive span prediction and relevance-aware contrastive learning have been proposed to enhance the quality of text representations by focusing on key words and reducing the impact of common words \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. These methods have shown promising results in improving retrieval performance on benchmarks such as BEIR and MS MARCO \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14218)\]. 

Additionally, the use of unsupervised pre-training strategies, such as masked auto-encoder (MAE) and contrastive learning, has been investigated to reduce the reliance on labeled data and improve generalization across domains \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00635)\]. For instance, RetroMAE introduces a novel MAE workflow that enables efficient pre-training of dense retrievers by leveraging asymmetric masking ratios and model structures \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)\]. Similarly, Llama2Vec proposes an unsupervised adaptation approach for large language models, where the model is fine-tuned using pretext tasks that encourage the generation of discriminative embeddings \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-approaches-for-retrieval'></a>Contrastive Learning Approaches for Retrieval: 4 papers found.
Contrastive learning approaches have been increasingly explored to enhance dense retrievers by improving the quality of text representations and the discriminative power of learned embeddings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. These methods aim to address limitations in traditional autoencoder-based models, where weak decoders can lead to suboptimal representations due to the uniform treatment of tokens during decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. To mitigate this, contrastive strategies have been proposed to generate word distributions in a non-autoregressive manner, pulling together masked versions of the same text while pushing away from others, thereby suppressing common words and highlighting representative ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. This approach has shown significant improvements over state-of-the-art autoencoder-based models and other pre-trained models in dense retrieval tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Additionally, contrastive learning has been leveraged to train smaller, more efficient retrievers by utilizing pruned large language models (LLMs) as backbones, enabling better multilingual and long-context capabilities while maintaining efficiency \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18460)\]. Furthermore, unsupervised contrastive learning has demonstrated strong performance in various retrieval settings, outperforming traditional term-frequency methods like BM25 on multiple datasets and achieving competitive results even with limited training data \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. The use of contrastive learning also allows for cross-lingual retrieval, enabling models to retrieve documents in one language from queries in another, which is not feasible with term-matching methods \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. Moreover, contrastive dual learning has been introduced to refine query and document representations by incorporating dual training objectives, leading to more uniform and discriminative embedding spaces that improve retrieval performance \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of contrastive learning in advancing dense retrieval systems by enhancing both representation quality and model efficiency.

### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-in-dense-retrieval'></a>Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval: 10 papers found.
Recent research has focused on improving dense retrieval by addressing the limitations of pre-trained language models (PLMs) and their fine-tuning processes. While PLMs like BERT and LLaMA have become foundational for dense retrieval, challenges remain in effectively leveraging their pre-trained knowledge during fine-tuning. For instance, dense retrievers often require extensive fine-tuning pipelines to achieve optimal performance, which can be resource-intensive and prone to data noise \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. To mitigate these issues, novel pre-training architectures such as Condenser have been proposed, which aim to better align PLMs with the requirements of dense retrieval by learning to condense information into dense vectors during pre-training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, coCondenser introduces an unsupervised contrastive loss to further refine passage embeddings without requiring large batches or heavy data engineering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Other approaches, such as Aggretriever, focus on aggregating contextualized token embeddings into a dense vector, improving retrieval effectiveness without significant training overhead \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. Additionally, RetroMAE introduces an asymmetric MAE workflow with tailored masking ratios for encoder and decoder, leading to competitive performance on retrieval benchmarks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance dense retrieval by refining both pre-training and fine-tuning strategies, with a growing emphasis on reducing reliance on large-scale data and complex engineering \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\].
