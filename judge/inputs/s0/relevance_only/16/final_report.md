## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to detect objects from arbitrary text descriptions, surpassing the limitations of closed-vocabulary detectors that are restricted to predefined categories \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. Prior work has explored various strategies to enable this capability, including leveraging pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) for zero-shot recognition \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\], using image-caption pairs for training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06160)\], and employing pseudo bounding-box labels to expand the vocabulary \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. These methods have shown promising results, particularly in detecting novel categories without requiring additional annotations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\].

A key challenge in OVD is the misalignment between visual and textual representations, which can lead to poor performance when the vocabulary is overly broad or mis-specified \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. To address this, some approaches have focused on improving the alignment between image regions and text descriptions through contrastive learning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\] or region-word matching \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14862)\]. Others have proposed methods to refine the vocabulary during inference, such as using language models to generate more accurate class descriptions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16833)\]. However, these approaches often require additional training or are limited in their ability to adapt to new scenes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16513)\].

The proposed Vocabulary Adapter (VocAda) builds upon these prior efforts by introducing a plug-and-play framework that operates at inference time without requiring any training \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11876)\]. This is a significant advancement over existing methods that rely on pre-defined vocabularies or require extensive fine-tuning \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09506)\]. VocAda uses an image captioner to describe visible objects, then parses nouns from those captions to identify relevant classes \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1e651d05e2f6c71081a33cfbc8375093c3c8b043)\]. This approach is particularly effective in reducing the impact of irrelevant or overly broad vocabularies, as it dynamically selects only the most relevant classes for each image \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01393)\].

Several studies have explored similar ideas, such as using pseudo captions to improve knowledge distillation \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13076)\] or employing multi-modal prompts to enhance alignment \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/776a3d4f8b84fe3f96853ed5e6a80ab2655e3076)\]. However, these methods often require additional training or are not as flexible as VocAda, which operates independently of any training process \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17109)\]. The ability to refine the vocabulary at inference time makes VocAda a versatile tool for improving detection accuracy across different detectors and datasets \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01424)\].

In addition to improving detection performance, VocAda also addresses the issue of confidence bias toward base categories, which is a common problem in open-vocabulary detection \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]. By focusing on the most relevant classes for each image, VocAda reduces the likelihood of misclassifying objects that are similar to known categories \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16486)\]. ### <a id='category-open-vocabulary-object-detection-frameworks'></a>Open-vocabulary object detection frameworks: 24 papers found.
Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling detectors to recognize objects from novel categories described in natural language \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. Recent works have focused on leveraging pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) to bridge the gap between visual and textual modalities, allowing for zero-shot or one-shot detection of unseen classes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. For instance, methods like ViLD and CORA utilize CLIP or ALIGN to align image regions with textual descriptions, improving detection accuracy without requiring additional training data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01393)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09506)\]. These approaches often rely on region prompting or anchor pre-matching to address the distribution mismatch between whole-image and region-level representations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.09506)\]. Additionally, some studies have explored multi-modal fusion strategies, combining language descriptions with image exemplars to enhance classifier performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. Others have introduced consistency regularization or pseudo-labeling techniques to improve generalization and data efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06160)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. Furthermore, efforts have been made to address challenges such as visual-textual misalignment and long-tailed category imbalance, with solutions like multimodal question answering detection (MQADet) and de-biased triplet cross-modal contrastive learning \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13076)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17109)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of OVD in real-world applications, such as autonomous driving and 3D scene understanding, where detecting novel objects is essential \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/776a3d4f8b84fe3f96853ed5e6a80ab2655e3076)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]. Overall, the field has seen significant progress, with ongoing efforts to refine vocabulary adaptation, improve localization accuracy, and enhance robustness to out-of-distribution scenarios \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16486)\].

Recent approaches, such as novel object captioning (NOC), focus on improving the adaptability of captioning models to objects not present in training data by selecting relevant objects and constraining the generative process of language models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. This aligns with efforts to enhance open-vocabulary detection by leveraging large-scale datasets and pseudo caption labels, which provide dense and diverse descriptions of object instances \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. These methods aim to extract richer knowledge about objects, including their attributes and relationships, to improve generalization to novel classes. For instance, pseudo caption labeling (PCL) generates detailed captions that serve as pseudo labels for knowledge distillation, enabling models to better understand object interactions and context \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]. Such advancements support the development of systems like VocAda, which refines user-defined vocabularies by extracting relevant object nouns from image captions, thereby improving detection performance without requiring additional training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06160)\]. These contributions highlight the growing importance of integrating captioning models with detection frameworks to enhance flexibility and accuracy in real-world scenarios.
