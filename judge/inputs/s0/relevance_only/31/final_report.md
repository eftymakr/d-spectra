## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has become a cornerstone in modern machine learning, particularly in computer vision and natural language processing, where it enables models to learn rich representations from large amounts of unlabeled data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. In the context of genomics, recent efforts have attempted to apply similar paradigms to DNA pre-training, aiming to learn meaningful representations from pure sequence data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. However, these approaches often fall short due to the limited information content of DNA sequences alone, as genomic functions are regulated by additional factors such as chromatin accessibility and regulatory elements \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of self-supervised learning for genomic data. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13769)\] introduced a method for predicting DNA methylation levels using features such as neighboring CpG site methylation, genomic distance, and regulatory elements, achieving high accuracy. Similarly, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\] proposed a self-supervised contrastive pre-training method for dense land cover classification, leveraging the large availability of satellite imagery to improve performance on downstream tasks. These works highlight the potential of self-supervised learning in genomics, but they often focus on single-modal data or lack the flexibility to handle multi-profile and multi-species genomic data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08663)\].

In contrast, supervised training for genomic profile prediction has emerged as a more effective alternative. For example, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02252)\] demonstrated that supervised learning can improve the accuracy of predicting DNA methylation levels, particularly when incorporating genomic features such as CpG islands and regulatory elements. Similarly, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\] showed that supervised pre-training on DNA sequences can lead to better performance in downstream tasks such as gene regulation prediction. These findings align with the argument that supervised training, when applied to genomic profiles, can yield more informative and robust representations than pure sequence-based approaches \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05415)\].

To address the challenges of multi-species and multi-profile genomic data, recent work has introduced models that leverage the Mixture of Experts (MoE) framework. For instance, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03499)\] proposed a model that combines multiple experts to capture the relationships between DNA sequences across different species and genomic profiles, leading to improved performance in tasks such as gene function prediction. ### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-in-genomic-representation'></a>Contrastive Learning in Genomic Representation: 4 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for unsupervised representation learning, with applications spanning diverse domains such as genomics, computer vision, and reinforcement learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. In the context of genomic representation, recent work has focused on leveraging contrastive learning to capture meaningful patterns from high-dimensional data, particularly when labeled data is scarce \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. For instance, methods like Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) have been adapted to learn latent representations by predicting future samples in latent space, using a probabilistic contrastive loss to ensure the model remains tractable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. Similarly, Prototypical Contrastive Learning (PCL) introduces prototypes as latent variables, enabling the model to encode semantic structures into the embedding space through an Expectation-Maximization framework \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. These approaches have demonstrated strong performance across multiple tasks, including speech, images, text, and reinforcement learning in 3D environments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. In the field of Earth observation, self-supervised contrastive pre-training methods have been proposed to leverage the abundance of satellite imagery, significantly improving performance on downstream tasks like land cover classification, even when ground truth data is limited \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. Furthermore, improvements to CPC have led to state-of-the-art results on benchmarks such as ImageNet, enabling data-efficient recognition with fewer labeled examples \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13769)\]. These developments highlight the versatility and effectiveness of contrastive learning in scenarios where labeled data is limited, making it a promising direction for genomic and other high-dimensional data representation tasks.

### <a id='category-supervised-genomic-profile-prediction'></a>Supervised Genomic Profile Prediction: 5 papers found.
In the context of supervised genomic profile prediction, several studies have explored computational methods to predict genomic features with varying degrees of accuracy and efficiency. For instance, nucleic acid reassociation kinetics have traditionally been used to determine prokaryotic genome relatedness, but these methods are technically challenging and not cost-effective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. In contrast, recent approaches leverage whole-genome sequence comparisons to computationally predict DNA-DNA hybridization values, offering a more feasible and accurate alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. Similarly, in the realm of medical genomics, supervised learning models have been employed to predict patient survival in cancer using neural networks, demonstrating advantages over traditional survival analysis methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Additionally, representation learning has been applied to disentangle factors of variation in images, with models like DNA-GAN producing latent representations that mimic DNA-like structures, enabling more interpretable and controllable image generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. In human genomics, predictive models have been developed to estimate complex traits such as height and disease risk based on polygenic scores, highlighting the potential of genomic data in personalized medicine \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13769)\]. Furthermore, the prediction of DNA methylation levels at single-CpG-site resolution has been achieved using random forest classifiers, incorporating features such as genomic distance, regulatory elements, and histone modifications to enhance accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.5211)\]. These studies collectively underscore the growing importance of supervised learning in genomic profile prediction, emphasizing the need for models that can effectively integrate diverse genomic data to improve predictive performance and biological interpretability.
