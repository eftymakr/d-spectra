## Related Works

Diffusion Transformers (DiT) have emerged as a powerful architecture for image and video generation, offering superior performance compared to traditional diffusion models based on U-Net architectures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. However, their large parameter counts and high computational demands pose significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. To address these limitations, model quantization has been explored as a means to reduce storage requirements and accelerate inference by lowering the bit-width of model parameters \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. While quantization has been extensively studied for image generation models, its application to video generation tasks remains challenging due to the unique temporal dynamics and multi-frame processing involved \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\].

Several recent works have investigated quantization techniques tailored for diffusion models. ViDiT-Q was introduced as an efficient quantization method for DiT models, achieving W8A8 quantization with minimal degradation in visual quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Q-DiT further advanced this field by addressing the spatial and temporal variance in DiT weights and activations, achieving a notable reduction in FID when quantizing DiT-XL/2 to W6A8 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Similarly, DVD-Quant proposed a data-free quantization framework for video DiTs, enabling W4A4 PTQ without compromising video quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18663)\]. These methods highlight the growing interest in quantizing DiT models for efficient deployment.

The challenges of quantization in diffusion models are further compounded by the temporal variation in activations and the asymmetric distribution of network values \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04056)\]. TQ-DiT introduced time-aware quantization techniques to mitigate these issues, achieving performance comparable to full-precision models at W8A8 \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14172)\]. PTQ4DiT was specifically designed for DiTs, addressing salient channel magnitudes and temporal variability in activation distributions, enabling W4A8 quantization for the first time \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. These approaches demonstrate the importance of tailoring quantization strategies to the unique characteristics of DiT models.

In addition to post-training quantization (PTQ), quantization-aware training (QAT) has also been explored for diffusion models. EfficientDM introduced a data-free and parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework, achieving QAT-level performance with PTQ-like efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03270)\]. QVGen further advanced this area by proposing a quantization-aware training framework tailored for video diffusion models under extremely low-bit quantization \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11497)\]. These methods underscore the potential of QAT in achieving high-quality quantization for diffusion models.

### <a id='category-post-training-quantization-approaches'></a>Post-training Quantization Approaches: 13 papers found.
Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a critical technique for reducing the computational and memory costs of diffusion transformers (DiTs), particularly in video generation tasks, where large model sizes and multi-frame processing pose significant challenges \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Existing PTQ methods, originally designed for traditional diffusion models, often suffer from biased quantization and performance degradation when applied to DiTs due to their unique architectural characteristics, such as spatial and temporal variance in weights and activations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. To address these issues, several works have proposed tailored quantization frameworks. For instance, ViDiT-Q introduces an improved quantization scheme specifically for DiT models, achieving W8A8 and W4A8 quantization with minimal degradation in visual quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Similarly, Q-DiT integrates automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization to handle the spatial and temporal variance in DiTs, achieving a notable reduction in FID when quantizing DiT-XL/2 to W6A8 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. DVD-Quant further advances the field by introducing a data-free quantization framework for Video DiTs, achieving a 2× speedup while maintaining visual fidelity and enabling W4A4 quantization without quality loss \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18663)\]. PTQ4DiT tackles the challenges of salient channels and temporal variability in DiT activations by proposing Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC), enabling W8A8 and W4A8 quantization \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04056)\]. HQ-DiT introduces hybrid floating-point quantization, leveraging 4-bit floating-point (FP) precision for both weights and activations, which minimizes quantization error compared to fixed-point methods \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14172)\]. QVD, designed for video diffusion models, addresses temporal and inter-channel disparities through High Temporal Discriminability Quantization (HTDQ) and Scattered Channel Range Integration (SCRI), achieving near-lossless performance degradation on W8A8 \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. These methods collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to develop PTQ strategies that are both effective and adaptable to the unique requirements of DiT models in video generation.

### <a id='category-quantization-techniques-for-image-models'></a>Quantization Techniques for Image Models: 5 papers found.
Document 1 introduces Multi-region quantization (MRQ) and Time-grouping quantization (TGQ) to enhance the computational efficiency of Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) by addressing the asymmetric distribution of network values and temporal variation in activations, respectively \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Document 2 proposes Time-aware quantization for DiTs (TaQ-DiT), which tackles issues such as non-convergence during reconstruction and sensitivity of Post-GELU activations to quantization by introducing joint reconstruction and time-variance-aware static transformations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Document 3 highlights the challenge of temporal variation in activation within diffusion models and introduces a novel quantization method that dynamically adjusts the quantization interval based on time step information, improving output quality without additional computational overhead \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18663)\]. Document 4 presents Time-Rotation Diffusion Quantization (TR-DQ), which incorporates time-step and rotation-based optimization to address the impact of time-steps variation during sampling and enables dynamic quantization across different time steps, achieving state-of-the-art performance and significant speedup and memory reduction \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04056)\]. Document 5 introduces Vector Quantized Diffusion (VQ-Diffusion), a method based on vector quantized variational autoencoder (VQ-VAE) for text-to-image generation, which eliminates unidirectional bias and incorporates a mask-and-replace diffusion strategy to avoid error accumulation, resulting in improved image quality and computational efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14172)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the importance of addressing temporal variation and quantization sensitivity in diffusion models to achieve efficient and high-quality generative results.

### <a id='category-quantization-aware-training-frameworks'></a>Quantization-aware Training Frameworks: 3 papers found.
Efficient quantization frameworks for diffusion models have been actively explored to address the computational and memory challenges associated with high-bit models. Recent approaches such as EfficientDM \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\] and QVGen \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\] have introduced novel strategies to achieve high-quality results with reduced bit-widths, particularly in image and video generation tasks. EfficientDM proposes a data-free, parameter-efficient fine-tuning framework that combines the efficiency of post-training quantization (PTQ) with the performance benefits of quantization-aware training (QAT), achieving QAT-level performance with PTQ-like efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Similarly, QVGen introduces a quantization-aware training (QAT) framework tailored for video diffusion models, focusing on reducing gradient norm to improve convergence under low-bit quantization, and employing auxiliary modules and a rank-decay strategy to minimize inference overhead \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. These methods highlight the importance of addressing the unique challenges of quantizing diffusion models, such as maintaining denoising capabilities and preserving spatiotemporal coherence in video generation. Additionally, research on transformer-based models has emphasized the need for variation-aware quantization schemes to handle the distinct behavior of transformers compared to ConvNets, as seen in the work on low-bit quantization for vision and language transformers \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18663)\]. These studies collectively underscore the ongoing efforts to develop robust and efficient quantization techniques for diffusion models, balancing performance and computational efficiency.

### <a id='category-resource-constraints-and-deployment-considerations'></a>Resource Constraints and Deployment Considerations: 4 papers found.
The category "Resource Constraints and Deployment Considerations" highlights the challenges of deploying diffusion models, particularly Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), on resource-constrained edge devices. These challenges include computational inefficiency, high memory usage, and the difficulty of maintaining temporal consistency in video generation. For instance, DiTs, while effective in generating high-fidelity videos, suffer from quadratic attention complexity, which becomes a bottleneck when scaling to high-definition or long-duration videos \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07590)\]. This complexity limits their deployment on devices with limited computational resources. Additionally, the need for efficient inference and storage has driven research into model quantization, which reduces parameter bit-width to accelerate inference and lower storage requirements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. However, existing quantization methods for image generation models do not generalize well to video tasks, as they fail to account for the unique challenges of temporal consistency and spatiotemporal correlations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. To address these issues, recent work has focused on developing specialized quantization techniques tailored for video DiTs, such as Adaptive Caching (AdaCache), which optimizes inference speed by caching computations based on video-specific characteristics \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.02397)\]. Similarly, methods like Anchored Diffusion aim to generate seamless long videos by incorporating temporal information and ensuring consistency across frames \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15153)\]. These approaches reflect the growing emphasis on improving the efficiency and practicality of diffusion models for real-world deployment.

### <a id='category-diffusion-transformer-architectures'></a>Diffusion Transformer Architectures: 5 papers found.
Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) have shown significant potential in generating high-quality video content, but challenges remain in scaling to long videos and maintaining temporal consistency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Existing methods often break long videos into short segments, requiring multiple sampling iterations and specialized modules to ensure coherence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. To address these limitations, Video Interface Networks (VINs) were introduced, enabling parallel inference of video chunks by encoding global semantics from local chunks and guiding denoising in parallel \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Additionally, DiT-3D extends the Transformer architecture to 3D shape generation, demonstrating scalability and high-quality results by incorporating 3D positional and patch embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. For multimodal content, AV-DiT leverages a shared DiT backbone with modality-specific adaptations, achieving efficient audio-visual generation with fewer tunable parameters \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18663)\]. Similarly, Mask2DiT enables multi-scene video generation by establishing fine-grained alignment between video segments and text annotations through symmetric binary masks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04056)\]. Meanwhile, MALT Diffusion addresses long video generation by using memory-augmented latent transformers to maintain temporal context across segments \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14172)\]. These approaches highlight the evolving capabilities of DiTs in handling diverse video generation tasks, from single-scene to multi-scene and long-duration videos, while addressing computational and consistency challenges.
