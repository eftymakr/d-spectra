## Related Works

Representation learning is a cornerstone of modern recommender systems, enabling efficient user-item matching through vectorized embeddings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. However, the computational and memory costs of dense embeddings have motivated research into binarization techniques, which convert embeddings into compact binary representations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. This approach reduces both storage and inference costs, making it suitable for resource-constrained environments. Despite these benefits, early methods primarily focused on numerical quantization, often at the expense of information retention, leading to performance degradation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\].

Several works have explored the use of graph-based methods for collaborative filtering, leveraging the structure of user-item interactions to improve recommendation accuracy. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item graphs into the embedding process, enabling the modeling of high-order connectivity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) explicitly incorporates multiple graphs, including user-user and item-item relationships, to capture richer interaction patterns \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These models emphasize the importance of graph structure in capturing collaborative signals, which are often overlooked in traditional matrix factorization approaches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

The challenge of information loss during binarization has also been addressed in prior work. Binarized Collaborative Filtering with Distilling Graph Convolutional Networks (Binarized GCN) introduces a framework to convert binary constrained optimization into a continuous optimization problem, allowing for efficient training with popular solvers like SGD and Adam \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. This approach mitigates the loss of information during training, improving the quality of binary embeddings. Similarly, Near-lossless Binarization of Word Embeddings proposes an autoencoder-based method that preserves semantic information while reducing vector size by 97% \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. These techniques demonstrate the feasibility of binarization without significant accuracy loss.

Recent advances in graph neural networks (GNNs) have further enhanced collaborative filtering. LightGNN introduces a lightweight and distillation-based framework for GNN pruning, reducing model complexity while preserving essential collaboration modeling capabilities \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03228)\]. SimRec, on the other hand, combines knowledge distillation and contrastive learning to address over-smoothing and improve scalability in large-scale datasets \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\]. These models highlight the potential of GNNs in recommendation systems, particularly in handling noisy and sparse data.

In addition, variational inference has been applied to improve graph-based collaborative filtering. The Graph Variational Embedding Collaborative Filtering (GVECF) framework uses variational graph auto-encoders to pre-train embeddings, enhancing feature propagation through graph convolutional networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11824)\]. ### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering-models'></a>Graph-Based Collaborative Filtering Models: 8 papers found.
Graph-based collaborative filtering models have emerged as a powerful approach in recommendation systems, aiming to capture complex user-item interactions through graph structures. These models typically leverage bipartite graphs to represent user-item interactions and employ graph neural networks (GNNs) to propagate embeddings, thereby encoding collaborative signals explicitly \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. However, challenges such as over-smoothing, noise effects, and scalability issues have been identified, prompting the development of advanced techniques like knowledge distillation and contrastive learning to enhance model performance \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3c8fba26e95c922d2616ecfb891de2542d01aea8)\]. For instance, methods such as NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\] and Multi-GCCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11824)\] integrate user-item graphs and additional user-user or item-item graphs to model high-order connectivity and proximal information, leading to more accurate embeddings. Furthermore, recent approaches like SimRec \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3c8fba26e95c922d2616ecfb891de2542d01aea8)\] and CECL \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\] address data sparsity and improve sampling strategies by incorporating community detection and adaptive weight learning, respectively. These innovations highlight the ongoing efforts to refine graph-based collaborative filtering by addressing limitations in embedding propagation, information loss, and scalability, ultimately leading to more robust and efficient recommendation systems.

### <a id='category-representation-learning-in-recommender-systems'></a>Representation Learning in Recommender Systems: 2 papers found.
Representation learning has emerged as a critical approach in recommender systems, particularly in collaborative filtering, where it enables the creation of more informative and efficient representations of users and items \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04163)\]. Traditional methods often rely on manually engineered metafeatures, which may not fully capture the underlying patterns necessary for accurate algorithm selection \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. In contrast, representation learning leverages data-driven approaches to automatically generate distributed representations, allowing for more robust and adaptive models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. For instance, graph-based methods like graph2vec have been used to model collaborative filtering as graphs, producing representations that are competitive with state-of-the-art metafeatures while requiring less data and minimal human input \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. Additionally, deep learning frameworks have been proposed to jointly model distributional representations for users and items, achieving better performance than existing methods \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04163)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of representation learning to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of collaborative filtering systems by capturing complex relationships in the data without relying on predefined features \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04163)\].
