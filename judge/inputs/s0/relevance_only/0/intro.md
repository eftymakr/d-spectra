## Related Works

Economic inequality remains a critical challenge, influencing disparities in education, healthcare, and social stability \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13994)\]. Traditional tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, aim to reduce inequality but often lack adaptability in response to heterogeneous taxpayer behavior and irrational decision-making \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13994)\]. Recent studies have explored dynamic taxation frameworks, such as the Saez Optimal Taxation model, which adjusts tax rates based on economic conditions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13994)\]. However, these models often fail to capture the complexity of taxpayer heterogeneity and behavioral irrationality \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13994)\]. 

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has emerged as a powerful tool for simulating economic systems, allowing for the representation of diverse agent behaviors and interactions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08932)\]. Early ABM studies focused on simplifying economic agents and their interactions, often using heuristic rules or regression models to define behavior \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. More recent work has integrated reinforcement learning (RL) into ABM frameworks, enabling agents to adapt and learn over time \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. This approach has been applied to various economic scenarios, including tax compliance \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\], economic inequality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.09284)\], and policy evaluation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09563)\]. 

The integration of large language models (LLMs) into ABM represents a novel advancement, offering the potential to enhance agent decision-making and policy simulation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. LLMs can provide rich contextual understanding and generate adaptive responses, making them suitable for modeling complex economic systems \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. This aligns with broader efforts to use LLMs in agent-based modeling, where they have been applied to simulate economic systems with learning agents \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]. Additionally, LLMs have been used to improve the realism of economic simulations by incorporating heterogeneous agent behavior and adaptive learning mechanisms \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09563)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of RL in economic modeling, particularly in the context of tax policy and economic inequality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. For example, RL-based ABMs have been used to evaluate the effectiveness of tax policies, demonstrating their ability to produce reward-seeking behaviors and outperform traditional models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. These findings support the use of RL in the TaxAgent framework, where the government agent utilizes LLMs to iteratively optimize tax rates \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13994)\]. 

In addition to economic modeling, ABM has been applied to study social and environmental systems, including the impact of tax evasion and the role of social networks in compliance \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. These studies highlight the versatility of ABM in capturing complex interactions and emergent behaviors \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.08932)\].
