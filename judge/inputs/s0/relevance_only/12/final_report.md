## Related Works

Event cameras, which capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis, have gained significant attention due to their high temporal resolution and ability to handle high dynamic range and fast motion scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces challenges in processing and analysis, particularly in terms of bandwidth, memory, and computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\]. Prior work has explored various strategies to mitigate these challenges, including event downsampling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\]. Most existing approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit their adaptability to different applications and environments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\]. These methods often fail to account for the varying importance of events in different contexts, leading to suboptimal performance in tasks such as object classification and detection \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\].

To address these limitations, recent research has focused on developing more adaptive and intelligent sampling strategies. For instance, EV-FlowNet introduces a self-supervised deep learning pipeline for optical flow estimation from event-based cameras, using grayscale images captured at the same time as events as a supervisory signal \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. This approach enables accurate estimation of dense optical flow without requiring labeled data, making it suitable for real-world applications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Similarly, E-RAFT proposes a method that incorporates feature correlation and sequential processing into dense optical flow estimation from event cameras, improving performance by reducing end-point error on benchmark datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10368)\]. These methods highlight the potential of deep learning for event-based vision tasks but often rely on specific architectures and training strategies.

Another line of research focuses on the reconstruction of intensity images from event streams, which is essential for applying conventional computer vision techniques to event data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.08597)\]. Events-To-Video proposes a recurrent neural network to reconstruct videos from event data, surpassing state-of-the-art methods in terms of image quality \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.08597)\]. High Speed and High Dynamic Range Video with an Event Camera further extends this work by demonstrating the ability to synthesize high framerate videos and provide high dynamic range reconstructions in challenging lighting conditions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. These approaches show that event data can be effectively converted into usable intensity images, enabling the use of off-the-shelf vision algorithms.

In addition to reconstruction, there has been growing interest in developing self-supervised learning methods for event cameras, which reduce the dependency on labeled data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. Masked Event Modeling (MEM) introduces a self-supervised framework for event cameras, pretraining a neural network on unlabeled events and achieving state-of-the-art classification accuracy across multiple datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\]. This method is particularly useful for tasks such as semantic image segmentation, where labeled data is scarce \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19916)\].

They have received increasing research interest due to their robustness to lighting conditions and motion blur. Two main approaches exist in the literature to feed the event-based processing algorithms: packaging the triggered events in event packages and sending them one-by-one as single events. These approaches suffer limitations from either processing overflow or lack of responsivity. Processing overflow is caused by high event generation rates when the algorithm cannot process all the events in real-time. Conversely, lack of responsivity happens in cases of low event generation rates when the event packages are sent at too low frequencies. This paper presents ASAP, an adaptive scheme to manage the event stream through variable-size packages that accommodate to the event package processing times. The experimental results show that ASAP is capable of feeding an asynchronous event-by-event clustering algorithm in a responsive and efficient manner and at the same time prevents overflow.»

[Snippet]: «Cross-platform adaptation in event-based dense perception is crucial for deploying event cameras across diverse settings, such as vehicles, drones, and quadrupeds, each with unique motion dynamics, viewpoints, and class distributions. In this work, we introduce EventFly, a framework for robust cross-platform adaptation in event camera perception. Our approach comprises three key components: i) Event Activation Prior (EAP), which identifies high-activation regions in the target domain to minimize prediction entropy, fostering confident, domain-adaptive predictions; ii) EventBlend, a data-mixing strategy that integrates source and target event voxel grids based on EAP-driven similarity and density maps, enhancing feature alignment; and iii) EventMatch, a dual-discriminator technique that aligns features from source, target, and blended domains for better domain-invariant learning. To holistically assess cross-platform adaptation abilities, we introduce EXPo, a large-scale benchmark with diverse samples across vehicle, drone, and quadruped platforms. Extensive experiments validate our effectiveness, demonstrating substantial gains over popular adaptation methods. We hope this work can pave the way for more adaptive, high-performing event perception across diverse and complex environments.»
