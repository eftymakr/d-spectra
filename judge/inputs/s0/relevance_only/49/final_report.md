## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have become a cornerstone of modern information retrieval systems, particularly in Web search, due to their ability to learn complex representations from raw data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11903)\]. However, these models typically require substantial amounts of labeled query-document pairs for training, which are both costly and labor-intensive to obtain \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. To address this challenge, researchers have explored synthetic query generation as a scalable alternative, leveraging large language models (LLMs) to generate queries from Web document collections like ClueWeb22 \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. While this approach offers promise, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal downstream retrieval performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\].

Existing methods typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08375)\]. However, this approach introduces additional complexity and may not fully address the root cause of suboptimal performance. In contrast, our work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach aims to optimize the model towards generating high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

DPO has been widely adopted for preference alignment of LLMs due to its simplicity and effectiveness \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\]. It directly optimizes LLMs using human preferences, bypassing the need for explicit reward modeling \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This method has been shown to be stable, performant, and computationally lightweight, eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or significant hyperparameter tuning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been extended to address various challenges, such as token-level importance sampling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\], the inclusion of rationales in preference data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14477)\], and the optimization of dense retrieval models with hard negatives \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\].

In addition to DPO, other approaches have explored the use of synthetic queries for retrieval tasks. For example, some studies have investigated the use of synthetically generated queries for evaluating retrievability bias in PLM-based rankers \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]. These studies have shown that synthetically generated queries can lead to lower bias compared to user-generated queries, suggesting that they may be more suitable for training retrieval models \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8bd0bac933600ccae6d8ab31c465bfc6894daa45)\]. Similarly, other works have explored the use of weak supervision for training neural ranking models, using pseudo query-document pairs that already exhibit relevance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\]. These approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in improving retrieval performance without requiring extensive manual labeling \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.00189)\].

In summary, while synthetic query generation offers a scalable alternative to labeled data in neural retrieval, it remains a challenging task due to the variability in query quality

### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 4 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) for aligning large language models with human preferences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Unlike RLHF, which requires complex reward modeling and reinforcement learning stages, DPO directly optimizes the model using human preferences without the need for explicit reward functions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\]. This simplification leads to improved computational efficiency and training stability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO has been applied in various domains, including generative information retrieval, where it addresses token-level misalignment by directly optimizing pairwise ranking without reinforcement learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. Additionally, DPO bypasses certain approximations inherent in RLHF, such as the assumption that reward models generalize to out-of-distribution data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\]. While DPO relies on the assumption that pairwise preferences can be substituted with pointwise rewards, recent work has aimed to refine this by introducing new objectives that eliminate both approximations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05181)\]. These advancements highlight DPO's potential to streamline the alignment process while maintaining high performance in tasks such as sentiment control, summarization, and dialogue generation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. The framework's simplicity and effectiveness have made it a focal point for ongoing research, with efforts to further improve its theoretical foundations and practical implementation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\].

### <a id='category-dpo-applications-and-extensions'></a>DPO Applications and Extensions: 5 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a powerful technique for aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences, particularly in scenarios where high-quality, contextually relevant responses are critical \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. Unlike traditional methods that rely on extensive labeled data or external re-rankers to filter noisy query-document pairs, DPO integrates preference signals directly into the training process, enabling models to learn from pairwise comparisons of preferred and dispreferred responses \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. This approach has been shown to improve performance across various downstream tasks, including reasoning, summarization, and alignment, by modeling the relative probability of selecting one response over another \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been extended to address challenges such as token-level importance sampling, where models are optimized based on the significance of individual tokens within responses \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.04350)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility of DPO in enhancing model alignment while maintaining efficiency and scalability. Additionally, recent work has explored the integration of rationale-augmented preference data to improve learning efficiency and model interpretability \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14477)\]. By incorporating such enhancements, DPO-based frameworks have demonstrated superior performance in real-world applications, such as E-commerce search, where generating high-quality queries and aligning with user preferences is essential \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. Overall, DPO represents a significant step forward in preference-based alignment, offering a flexible and effective approach to model training and optimization.

### <a id='category-synthetic-query-generation-for-retrieval-tasks'></a>Synthetic Query Generation for Retrieval Tasks: 3 papers found.
The category "Synthetic Query Generation for Retrieval Tasks" explores methods to enhance retrieval performance by generating synthetic queries that better align with real-world usage. These approaches leverage various techniques, such as using language models to create private synthetic queries \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\], employing sequence-to-sequence models to predict relevant queries for documents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\], and constructing query equivalents to reduce dependence on top-ranked documents \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. These methods aim to improve the quality of synthetic queries, which in turn leads to better retrieval outcomes. For instance, generating private synthetic queries with DP language models ensures query privacy while maintaining retrieval quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11701)\]. Similarly, predicting queries for documents using sequence-to-sequence models allows for more effective expansion of document content, leading to state-of-the-art results when combined with re-ranking \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Additionally, constructing query equivalents based on term distributions helps in achieving higher precision at top-ranks by considering a broader range of query variations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15453)\]. These strategies collectively address the limitations of traditional synthetic query generation, offering more robust and effective solutions for retrieval tasks.
