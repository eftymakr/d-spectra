Neural retrieval methods using transformer-based pre-trained language models have significantly advanced multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\]. These models demonstrate notable improvements over existing multilingual baselines, such as Arctic Embed 2.0 \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.03324)\].

The use of pre-trained language models for information retrieval has been extensively studied. For instance, ColBERT \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\] introduces a late interaction architecture that independently encodes the query and document using BERT and then employs a cheap yet powerful interaction step to model their fine-grained similarity. This approach significantly improves retrieval effectiveness while reducing computational costs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. Similarly, ColBERT-X \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\] extends this idea to support cross-language information retrieval by using the XLM-RoBERTa encoder, enabling zero-shot training on the English MS MARCO collection. ### <a id='category-dense-retrieval-models'></a>Dense Retrieval Models: 7 papers found.
Dense retrieval models have gained significant attention for their ability to capture semantic relationships between queries and documents, surpassing traditional term-frequency based methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. These models leverage contextualized embeddings, such as those from BERT or RoBERTa, to represent both queries and documents in a fixed-length vector space, allowing for more effective semantic matching \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. Recent approaches like RepBERT have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on tasks such as the MS MARCO Passage Ranking task, achieving competitive efficiency compared to bag-of-words methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. However, dense retrievers often face challenges in low-resource settings, where training data is scarce, and they may underperform compared to unsupervised term-frequency methods like BM25 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.00785)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have explored contrastive learning and unsupervised training strategies, leading to models that outperform BM25 on multiple benchmarks, including the BEIR dataset \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.00785)\]. Additionally, methods like Learning To Retrieve (LTRe) have been proposed to improve training efficiency and effectiveness by avoiding negative sampling and performing full retrieval during training \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.15059)\]. The use of multiple representations, such as in ColBERT, has also shown superior performance in terms of MAP and MRR@10 compared to single representation models like ANCE \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18485)\]. Furthermore, dense retrieval has been adapted for specialized tasks, such as Math Information Retrieval (MIR), where it complements structure-based search methods to enhance retrieval effectiveness \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. To improve efficiency without compromising performance, techniques like LADR have been introduced, combining lexical retrieval with dense retrieval to achieve high recall and precision while reducing computational overhead \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01852)\]. These advancements highlight the evolving landscape of dense retrieval, emphasizing the importance of both semantic modeling and efficient implementation for real-world applications.
