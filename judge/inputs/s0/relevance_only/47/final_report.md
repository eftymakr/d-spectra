## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to enhance recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge transfer across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. Traditional MDR methods often assume a single transfer structure, which may not be suitable for complex domain interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. This limitation has led to the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP), where unselective knowledge transfer between domains can degrade performance, especially when there are conflicts or poor quality in certain domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. 

Several studies have attempted to address the NTP by introducing more sophisticated transfer mechanisms. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\] investigates Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR), which considers the sequential nature of user interactions and introduces a cooperative game-theoretic approach to assess and mitigate negative transfer. This method assigns low weights to prediction losses from domains that contribute negatively to model performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Similarly, \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\] proposes a Multi-Domain Sequential Recommendation via Domain Space Learning (MDSR-DSL) that uses cross-domain items to supplement missing sequential context and introduces a domain-to-domain adaptation mechanism to align item representations across domains \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\].

In the context of cross-domain recommendation, \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/161f9d8c3694ed32e07a716c63c109d60bf2d4ee)\] introduces a tag tree-guided multi-grained alignment approach that leverages shared tag structures to enable domain alignment in multi-domain short video recommendation. This method integrates publisher and tag nodes into a user-video bipartite graph to facilitate alignment across domains \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/161f9d8c3694ed32e07a716c63c109d60bf2d4ee)\]. Additionally, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\] proposes a method called EDDA that disentangles domain-specific and domain-shared knowledge through embedding disentangling and domain alignment, improving knowledge transfer across domains with small overlaps \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\].

The challenge of domain-specific preferences and the need for adaptive domain selection have also been addressed in recent work. For example, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03357)\] introduces a hierarchical hypergraph network for multi-domain recommendation that captures correlative preference transfer by modeling multi-domain user-item interactions in a unified graph. This approach includes modules for dynamic item transfer and adaptive user aggregation to improve user representations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03357)\]. Similarly, \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\] proposes a framework for Dual-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation (DTCDR) that utilizes multi-source content information and multi-task learning to improve performance on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\].

Recent advancements in cross-domain recommendation also include methods that focus on the dynamic selection of domains based on similarity. As a remedy, we propose a new CDSR framework that addresses the problem of negative transfer by assessing the extent of negative transfer from one domain to another and adaptively assigning low weight values to the corresponding prediction losses. ### <a id='category-multi-domain-recommendation-(mdr)-overview'></a>Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) Overview: 3 papers found.
Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a critical research direction in addressing the limitations of traditional recommendation systems, particularly data sparsity and cold-start problems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. The core idea of MDR is to leverage information across multiple domains to improve recommendation performance, enabling scalability and adaptability to diverse scenarios \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Existing MDR methods often employ a shared structure with domain-specific components to balance reusable features and domain-specific information \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\]. However, challenges such as domain conflicts, overfitting on sparse data, and the difficulty of generalizing across different data distributions persist \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/161f9d8c3694ed32e07a716c63c109d60bf2d4ee)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various frameworks, such as MAMDR, which introduces strategies like Domain Negotiation and Domain Regularization to enhance model generalizability and reduce domain conflicts \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. Additionally, HAMUR has been introduced as a flexible solution that dynamically adapts to different domains through a domain-specific adapter and a domain-shared hyper-network \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03357)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of MDR systems, with a focus on scalability, adaptability, and avoiding negative transfer \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\].
