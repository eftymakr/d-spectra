## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts only the immediate next token, MTP predicts multiple future tokens at each step, often using multiple prediction heads \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly for large models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in effectively leveraging MTP, as prior work indicates \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. To address this, we propose a curriculum learning strategy for MTP training, exploring forward and reverse curricula \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\].

Curriculum learning has been widely studied in various domains, including natural language understanding \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\], neural machine translation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\], and reinforcement learning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00285)\]. In natural language understanding, curriculum learning has been shown to improve performance by arranging training examples in an easy-to-difficult order \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\]. Similarly, in neural machine translation, curriculum learning has been used to reduce training time and improve performance by filtering training samples based on difficulty and model competence \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\]. These approaches align with our forward curriculum, which gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\].

In reinforcement learning, curriculum learning has been applied to automatically select a sequence of tasks to accelerate learning of a complex target task \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00285)\]. This concept is closely related to our reverse curriculum, which starts with MTP and gradually reduces complexity to NTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. The effectiveness of curriculum learning in these domains suggests that similar strategies could benefit MTP training for SLMs.

Recent work has also explored the benefits of multi-token prediction in improving inference efficiency. For example, L-MTP introduces a leap-based mechanism to predict non-sequential tokens in a single forward pass, enhancing long-range dependency capture and inference speed \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Similarly, Future Token Prediction (FTP) generates embedding vectors that better capture the overall meaning of longer sequences, improving topic coherence and classification performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18160)\]. These methods highlight the potential of multi-token prediction to enhance both training and inference processes.

While our work does not directly address speculative decoding, the principles of multi-token prediction and curriculum learning could be combined to further improve inference efficiency.

### <a id='category-curriculum-learning-strategies'></a>Curriculum learning strategies: 6 papers found.
Curriculum learning strategies have been extensively explored to enhance the training and performance of various language models, particularly in natural language understanding (NLU) and neural machine translation (NMT) tasks. These strategies aim to structure the learning process by gradually increasing the difficulty of training examples, mimicking human learning procedures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. For instance, curriculum learning has been shown to significantly improve performance across a wide range of NLU tasks by distinguishing easy examples from difficult ones and arranging them in an order that aligns with the model's evolving competence \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Similarly, in NMT, curriculum learning frameworks have been proposed to reduce training time and improve performance by filtering training samples based on their estimated difficulty and the model's current capabilities, leading to faster convergence and better solutions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10518)\]. In sequence prediction tasks, curriculum learning has also been applied to gradually shift the training process from fully guided schemes to less guided ones, reducing error accumulation and improving results \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\]. Furthermore, automated curriculum selection methods have been introduced to optimize learning efficiency by using reward signals derived from learning progress indicators, such as prediction accuracy and network complexity, leading to accelerated training times \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2d315208dd88eed245dc4ae1ae398af75aa291f8)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of curriculum learning to enhance both training efficiency and model performance across different domains.

### <a id='category-curriculum-learning-in-reinforcement-learning'></a>Curriculum learning in reinforcement learning: 2 papers found.
Curriculum learning in reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising approach to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of training complex models, particularly in the context of large language models (LLMs). This methodology involves structuring the training process by gradually increasing the difficulty of tasks, allowing models to build upon simpler tasks before tackling more complex ones \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00285)\]. Recent work has focused on developing principled frameworks for curriculum design, such as formulating the curriculum sequencing problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), which enables the automatic selection of optimal task sequences \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.00285)\]. Additionally, there is growing interest in distribution-aware curriculum strategies that adaptively schedule training across heterogeneous data distributions, leveraging metrics like policy advantages to prioritize distributions with high potential for learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. These approaches aim to enhance convergence speed and final performance by aligning training with the model's evolving capabilities. The integration of curriculum learning with RL-based post-training methods, such as GRPO, further demonstrates its applicability in improving reasoning capabilities and handling complex tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\]. Overall, curriculum learning in RL continues to evolve, with a focus on both theoretical foundations and practical implementations across diverse domains.
