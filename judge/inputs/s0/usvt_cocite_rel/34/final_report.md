## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advances focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as prediction-only or predict-then-optimize (PnO) methods suffer from goal mismatch, where the forecasting and allocation objectives are not aligned \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. This issue is further exacerbated by the introduction of state-of-the-art forecasting models, which introduce additional uncertainty into the predicted results \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. To address these challenges, this paper introduces the Risk-aware Time-Series Predict-and-Allocate (RTS-PnO) framework, which is designed to be agnostic to the underlying forecasting model and incorporates adaptive uncertainty calibration \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\].

Several recent works have explored the use of deep learning for time series forecasting. Transformers have gained significant traction due to their ability to capture long-range dependencies, although they face challenges in non-stationary data and computational efficiency \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. The PatchTST model, for instance, addresses these issues by introducing a segmentation-based approach and channel-independent design, improving long-term forecasting accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) combines recurrent and self-attention layers to enable interpretable multi-horizon forecasting \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. These models, however, often require extensive tuning and are not inherently designed for integration with allocation strategies.

The use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting has also been explored, with studies showing that models like GPT-3 and LLaMA-2 can perform zero-shot forecasting comparable to specialized models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. This approach, however, relies on encoding time series as strings of numerical digits and may not generalize well to complex financial data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. The Time-LLM framework attempts to bridge this gap by reprogramming LLMs for time series forecasting while preserving the backbone of the language model \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\].

In the context of financial applications, the DeepAR model has demonstrated improvements in probabilistic forecasting, particularly in retail and demand prediction \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. However, it is limited to autoregressive recurrent networks and does not address the integration of forecasting with allocation decisions. The Deep Factors model, on the other hand, combines classical statistical models with deep neural networks to handle uncertainty and scalability in large collections of time series \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\].

The recent development of the Autoformer model introduces an Auto-Correlation mechanism, which enhances the efficiency and accuracy of long-term forecasting by capturing periodic dependencies \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12417)\]. ### <a id='category-transformer-based-forecasting-models'></a>Transformer-Based Forecasting Models: 7 papers found.
Recent advancements in Transformer-based forecasting models have significantly improved the accuracy of long-term time series predictions, addressing challenges such as non-stationarity, computational efficiency, and the integration of complex temporal dependencies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. Models like PatchTST introduce a channel-independent patching design that retains local semantic information while reducing computational overhead, enabling better long-term forecasting \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) combines recurrent layers with self-attention mechanisms to handle multi-horizon forecasting tasks, offering both high performance and interpretability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. The Non-stationary Transformers framework further enhances model robustness by addressing the issue of over-stationarization, which can hinder the detection of real-world bursty events \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. Meanwhile, the iTransformer model restructures the attention mechanism to focus on variate-level representations, improving performance across different lookback windows \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. Additionally, Autoformer introduces an Auto-Correlation mechanism that outperforms traditional self-attention in both efficiency and accuracy, particularly for long-term forecasting \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to refine Transformer architectures for time series forecasting, emphasizing adaptability, interpretability, and efficiency \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\].

### <a id='category-deep-learning-for-time-series-forecasting'></a>Deep Learning for Time Series Forecasting: 7 papers found.
The category "Deep Learning for Time Series Forecasting" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the accuracy and robustness of time series analysis. Recent works have focused on addressing the limitations of traditional methods by leveraging deep learning architectures that can capture complex temporal patterns and dependencies. For instance, methods like TimesNet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\] introduce 2D tensor transformations to model both intra- and inter-period variations, enabling more effective temporal variation modeling. Similarly, hybrid models that combine classical statistical methods with neural networks have been proposed to balance data efficiency and scalability \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12417)\]. These models often incorporate attention mechanisms or graph-based structures to better handle spatial and temporal dependencies, as seen in frameworks like Spatio-Temporal Graph Convolutional Networks (STGCN) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.04875)\] and TSMixer \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06053)\], which utilize multi-layer perceptrons to efficiently extract information across time and features. Additionally, there is growing interest in developing foundation models inspired by large language models, such as TEMPO \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04948)\], which leverages inductive biases like decomposition of trend, seasonality, and residual components to improve generalization across diverse time series tasks. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to create more flexible, accurate, and adaptable deep learning models for time series forecasting.
