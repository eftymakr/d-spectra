## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommendation systems, with recent advancements leveraging graph-based models to better capture user-item interactions \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\]. Early approaches such as matrix factorization \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf6f83fcce274606bf0264c59d1c78a30c9c9d18)\] and neural collaborative filtering (NCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] have laid the groundwork for modern methods that incorporate deep learning. However, these models often rely on simplistic assumptions about user-item relationships and fail to fully exploit the rich structure of interaction graphs \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\].

Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling such interactions, enabling more expressive and accurate representations \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\]. The success of GCNs in recommendation systems has been demonstrated by models like LightGCN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which simplifies the GCN architecture by removing non-linearities and focusing on neighborhood aggregation. This approach has shown significant improvements over earlier models, including Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\], by reducing training complexity and improving performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

Despite these advancements, existing GCN-based methods face challenges in modeling high-order interactions and handling large-scale graphs. For instance, models like NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] and RippleNet \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] attempt to integrate graph structures and side information, but they often struggle with over-smoothing and scalability issues. Additionally, the use of non-linear activation functions in GCNs can complicate training and degrade performance, especially in sparse interaction scenarios \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. To address these limitations, recent works such as LightGCN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] and Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\] have proposed simplified architectures that focus on linear aggregation and disentangled representations, respectively.

Moreover, the integration of knowledge graphs into recommendation systems has gained traction, with models like Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\] and RippleNet \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] demonstrating the potential of leveraging semantic relationships between items. These models enhance recommendations by incorporating richer contextual information, although they may still face challenges in efficiently capturing complex interactions \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\].

In addition to GCN-based methods, other approaches such as variational autoencoders \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\] and self-supervised learning \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\] have been explored to improve the robustness and accuracy of recommendation systems. Variational autoencoders provide a probabilistic framework for modeling user preferences, while self-supervised learning techniques aim to enhance node representations by introducing auxiliary tasks that improve the model's ability to generalize \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\].
