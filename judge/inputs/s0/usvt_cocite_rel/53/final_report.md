## Related Works

Deep sequential recommendation models have seen significant advancements, with various approaches addressing the challenges of modeling user behavior sequences. Early methods such as Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) were widely used to capture sequential patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. MCs assume that a user's next action is based on their last few actions, while RNNs allow for longer-term semantics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, MC-based methods often struggle with dense datasets, while RNNs can be computationally expensive \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, attention-based models such as SASRec were proposed, which combine the efficiency of MCs with the flexibility of RNNs by using self-attention to identify relevant items in a user's history \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. This approach allows for capturing long-term semantics while maintaining computational efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\].

Recent works have also explored self-supervised learning for sequential recommendation, such as S3-Rec, which leverages mutual information maximization to enhance data representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. This method introduces auxiliary objectives to learn correlations between items, attributes, and subsequences, improving performance, especially with limited training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. Similarly, BERT4Rec extends the bidirectional self-attention mechanism from the Transformer model to sequential recommendation, allowing for bidirectional context modeling and improving recommendation accuracy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. This approach avoids information leakage by using the Cloze objective to predict masked items based on both left and right contexts \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\].

Time-aware models such as TiSASRec have also been developed to explicitly model time intervals between interactions, improving the accuracy of next-item predictions by considering both the order and timing of user actions \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. Feature-level models like FDSA further enhance sequential recommendation by considering both item-level and feature-level transitions, capturing more comprehensive sequential patterns \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/63555b4f363ee72dd8d49d8deca68760542c0cd2)\]. These methods demonstrate the importance of modeling both item and feature-level interactions for better recommendation performance.

Session-based recommendation methods have also evolved, with models such as NARM incorporating attention mechanisms to capture user intent and session context \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\]. These models use hybrid encoders to represent sessions and compute item scores based on a bi-linear matching scheme \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\]. Additionally, graph-based models such as SR-GNN have been introduced to capture complex item transitions by modeling sessions as graph-structured data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00855)\].

To address the limitations of traditional models, recent works have focused on parameter-efficient transfer learning, such as PeterRec, which allows for efficient adaptation of pre-trained models to new tasks without retraining the entire network \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2ca1abd015c72e7c04d63fa653b0a27d9592fc02)\]. ### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-models'></a>Sequential Recommendation Models: 8 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models aim to predict the next item a user will interact with based on their historical behavior, emphasizing the importance of sequential patterns and temporal dynamics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Traditional approaches like Markov Chains (MCs) assume predictions are based on recent actions, while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) capture longer-term dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, MC-based methods excel in sparse datasets due to their simplicity, whereas RNNs perform better in dense settings with higher complexity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To balance these trade-offs, self-attention based models like SASRec have been introduced, allowing for efficient modeling of long-term semantics while focusing on relevant actions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Similarly, Caser uses convolutional filters to extract local features from item sequences, offering a flexible framework for capturing both general preferences and sequential patterns \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. BERT4Rec extends this by employing bidirectional self-attention, enabling models to consider both left and right contexts in user behavior sequences, thus improving representation power \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Feature-level models like FDSA further enhance recommendations by incorporating explicit and implicit feature-level sequences, capturing richer sequential patterns \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/63555b4f363ee72dd8d49d8deca68760542c0cd2)\]. Additionally, models like FMLP-Rec integrate filtering algorithms to mitigate noise in logged data, improving generalization and performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13556)\]. Recent works also address representation degeneration in embeddings, proposing methods like DuoRec that use contrastive regularization to improve distribution uniformity \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]. Finally, frameworks like ComiRec introduce controllable multi-interest modules to better capture diverse user preferences and improve recommendation diversity \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09347)\]. These advancements collectively highlight the evolving landscape of sequential recommendation, emphasizing the need for models that balance efficiency, accuracy, and adaptability to varying data characteristics.

### <a id='category-self-supervised-learning-approaches'></a>Self-Supervised Learning Approaches: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Recently, significant progress has been made in sequential recommendation with deep learning. Existing neural sequential recommendation models usually rely on the item prediction loss to learn model parameters or data representations. However, the model trained with this loss is prone to suffer from data sparsity problem. Since it overemphasizes the final performance, the association or fusion between context data and sequence data has not been well captured and utilized for sequential recommendation. To tackle this problem, we propose the model S3-Rec, which stands for Self-Supervised learning for Sequential Recommendation, based on the self-attentive neural architecture. The main idea of our approach is to utilize the intrinsic data correlation to derive self-supervision signals and enhance the data representations via pre-training methods for improving sequential recommendation. For our task, we devise four auxiliary self-supervised objectives to learn the correlations among attribute, item, subsequence, and sequence by utilizing the mutual information maximization (MIM) principle. MIM provides a unified way to characterize the correlation between different types of data, which is particularly suitable in our scenario. Extensive experiments conducted on six real-world datasets demonstrate the superiority of our proposed method over existing state-of-the-art methods, especially when only limited training data is available. Besides, we extend our self-supervised learning method to other recommendation models, which also improve their performance.»

### <a id='category-time-aware-models'></a>Time-Aware Models: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Sequential recommender systems seek to exploit the order of users' interactions, in order to predict their next action based on the context of what they have done recently. Traditionally, Markov Chains(MCs), and more recently Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Self Attention (SA) have proliferated due to their ability to capture the dynamics of sequential patterns. However a simplifying assumption made by most of these models is to regard interaction histories as ordered sequences, without regard for the time intervals between each interaction (i.e., they model the time-order but not the actual timestamp). In this paper, we seek to explicitly model the timestamps of interactions within a sequential modeling framework to explore the influence of different time intervals on next item prediction. We propose TiSASRec (Time Interval aware Self-attention based sequential recommendation), which models both the absolute positions of items as well as the time intervals between them in a sequence. Extensive empirical studies show the features of TiSASRec under different settings and compare the performance of self-attention with different positional encodings. Furthermore, experimental results show that our method outperforms various state-of-the-art sequential models on both sparse and dense datasets and different evaluation metrics.»

### <a id='category-session-based-recommendation'></a>Session-Based Recommendation: 3 papers found.
Session-based recommendation systems have seen significant advancements with the introduction of deep learning techniques. Early approaches focused on capturing sequential user behavior within a session, often using recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to model the temporal dynamics of user interactions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06939)\]. However, these methods struggled with long sessions and failed to explicitly model the user's main purpose during the session \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\]. To address these limitations, researchers proposed the Neural Attentive Recommendation Machine (NARM), which integrates an attention mechanism with a hybrid encoder to better capture both sequential behavior and session-specific intent \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\]. This approach was further refined with convolutional neural networks (CNNs), where item embeddings were treated as images to leverage convolution and pooling operations for capturing both short- and long-range dependencies \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05163)\]. Additionally, modifications to classic RNNs, such as introducing a ranking loss function, improved the practical viability of session-based recommendations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06939)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of session-based recommendation systems by incorporating advanced neural network architectures and attention mechanisms.

### <a id='category-parameter-efficient-transfer-learning'></a>Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning: 2 papers found.
Parameter-efficient transfer learning has emerged as a critical approach in addressing the limitations of traditional sequential recommendation models, particularly in terms of adaptability across different recommendation scenarios. Existing methods often rely on explicit item IDs to model user behavior sequences, which limits their transferability to new domains \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]. To overcome this, UniSRec introduces a universal sequence representation learning approach that leverages item description texts to learn transferable representations, enabling effective adaptation to new recommendation platforms without retraining the entire model \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]. Similarly, PeterRec focuses on parameter-efficient transfer learning by allowing pre-trained parameters to remain unchanged during fine-tuning, instead injecting small, expressive neural networks to adapt to downstream tasks \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2ca1abd015c72e7c04d63fa653b0a27d9592fc02)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of designing lightweight and adaptable architectures that can generalize across diverse recommendation settings, particularly when data is limited. Both methods demonstrate that parameter-efficient strategies can achieve comparable or even superior performance compared to full fine-tuning, making them valuable for real-world deployment \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]\[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2ca1abd015c72e7c04d63fa653b0a27d9592fc02)\].

### <a id='category-graph-based-models'></a>Graph-Based Models: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «The problem of session-based recommendation aims to predict user actions based on anonymous sessions. Previous methods model a session as a sequence and estimate user representations besides item representations to make recommendations. Though achieved promising results, they are insufficient to obtain accurate user vectors in sessions and neglect complex transitions of items. To obtain accurate item embedding and take complex transitions of items into account, we propose a novel method, i.e. Session-based Recommendation with Graph Neural Networks, SR-GNN for brevity. In the proposed method, session sequences are modeled as graphstructured data. Based on the session graph, GNN can capture complex transitions of items, which are difficult to be revealed by previous conventional sequential methods. Each session is then represented as the composition of the global preference and the current interest of that session using an attention network. Extensive experiments conducted on two real datasets show that SR-GNN evidently outperforms the state-of-the-art session-based recommendation methods consistently.»
