## Related Works

Large language models (LLMs) have seen significant advancements in recent years, with a focus on improving their efficiency, scalability, and performance across diverse tasks. Early work on LLMs primarily focused on pre-training on large corpora and fine-tuning for specific tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. For instance, GPT-3 demonstrated strong few-shot learning capabilities, achieving competitive performance on various NLP tasks without any fine-tuning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. This was further extended by FLAN, which showed that instruction tuning significantly improves zero-shot performance on unseen tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Instruction tuning involves fine-tuning models on a collection of tasks described via natural language instructions, leading to substantial improvements in performance across multiple benchmarks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\].

The training of large models also presents significant challenges, particularly in terms of computational resources and memory constraints. Megatron-LM introduced techniques for training multi-billion parameter models using model parallelism, enabling the training of models up to 8.3 billion parameters on 512 GPUs \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053)\]. This work demonstrated the importance of careful architectural design and optimization to achieve state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053)\].

Recent research has also explored the use of LLMs for code generation and mathematical reasoning. Codex, a GPT model fine-tuned on GitHub code, demonstrated impressive capabilities in writing Python code, solving 28.8% of problems on the HumanEval benchmark \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)\]. Similarly, GSM8K was introduced as a dataset of grade school math word problems, highlighting the limitations of current models in multi-step mathematical reasoning \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\]. To address this, researchers proposed training verifiers to judge the correctness of model completions, significantly improving performance on GSM8K \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\].

Instruction tuning has also been shown to enhance the performance of LLMs on a variety of tasks. WizardLM, for example, used an evolutionary instruction generation method to create complex instructions and fine-tune LLaMA, achieving performance comparable to ChatGPT on 17 out of 29 skills \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. This work demonstrated the potential of AI-evolved instructions for improving LLM performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\].

Furthermore, the development of efficient finetuning methods has enabled the training of large models on limited resources. QLoRA introduced a method for efficient finetuning of quantized LLMs, allowing the finetuning of a 65B parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit finetuning performance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)\]. ### <a id='category-instruction-tuning-and-zero-shot-learning'></a>Instruction Tuning and Zero-shot Learning: 2 papers found.
Instruction Tuning and Zero-shot Learning has been a focal area in improving the adaptability of large language models (LLMs) to new tasks without explicit training data. Recent work has demonstrated that instruction tuning, which involves fine-tuning models on a diverse set of tasks described via natural language instructions, significantly enhances zero-shot performance on unseen tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. This approach has shown remarkable success, with models like FLAN outperforming even large-scale models such as GPT-3 on multiple benchmarks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Similarly, research on Toolformer highlights the potential of LMs to autonomously utilize external tools through APIs, further expanding their capability to handle complex tasks with minimal supervision \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. These studies emphasize the importance of both the quantity and quality of instruction data, as well as the model's scale, in achieving robust zero-shot performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. The integration of such techniques into training frameworks is crucial for developing more versatile and efficient LLMs.

### <a id='category-benchmarking-and-evaluation-frameworks'></a>Benchmarking and Evaluation Frameworks: 7 papers found.
The category "Benchmarking and Evaluation Frameworks" encompasses a range of studies focused on developing and refining methods to assess the capabilities of large language models (LLMs). These works highlight the challenges in evaluating LLMs due to their broad functionalities and the limitations of existing benchmarks in capturing human preferences \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)\]. To address these issues, researchers have explored using strong LLMs, such as GPT-4, as judges to evaluate models on open-ended tasks, demonstrating that they can achieve over 80% agreement with human preferences \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)\]. Additionally, new benchmarks like MT-bench and Chatbot Arena have been introduced to provide more comprehensive and scalable evaluation frameworks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)\]. Another study proposes a comprehensive test covering 57 tasks, including subjects like mathematics, history, and law, revealing that even the largest models still struggle to reach expert-level accuracy on these tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300)\]. Furthermore, the development of specialized benchmarks, such as GSM8K for mathematical reasoning and HumanEval for code generation, has enabled more targeted assessments of model performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)\]. These efforts underscore the importance of diverse and robust evaluation methods to accurately measure LLM capabilities and identify areas for improvement \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18223)\].

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «We present QLoRA, an efficient finetuning approach that reduces memory usage enough to finetune a 65B parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance. QLoRA backpropagates gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model into Low Rank Adapters~(LoRA). Our best model family, which we name Guanaco, outperforms all previous openly released models on the Vicuna benchmark, reaching 99.3% of the performance level of ChatGPT while only requiring 21 hours of finetuning on a single GPU. QLoRA introduces a number of innovations to save memory without sacrificing performance: (a) 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), a new data type that is information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights (b) double quantization to reduce the average memory footprint by quantizing the quantization constants, and (c) paged optimizers to manage memory spikes. We use QLoRA to finetune more than 1,000 models, providing a detailed analysis of instruction following and chatbot performance across 8 instruction datasets, multiple model types (LLaMA, T5), and model scales that would be infeasible to run with regular finetuning (e.g. 33B and 65B parameter models). Our results show that QLoRA finetuning on a small high-quality dataset leads to state-of-the-art results, even when using smaller models than the previous SoTA. We provide a detailed analysis of chatbot performance based on both human and GPT-4 evaluations showing that GPT-4 evaluations are a cheap and reasonable alternative to human evaluation. Furthermore, we find that current chatbot benchmarks are not trustworthy to accurately evaluate the performance levels of chatbots. A lemon-picked analysis demonstrates where Guanaco fails compared to ChatGPT. We release all of our models and code, including CUDA kernels for 4-bit training.»
