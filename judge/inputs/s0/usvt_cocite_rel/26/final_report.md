## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy protection in data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for data privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, especially in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Specifically, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This issue has been explored in several works that examine the security and robustness of DP protocols. 

Several studies have focused on the vulnerabilities of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols, which are designed to protect user privacy by perturbing data before transmission \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\] highlights that LDP protocols are susceptible to data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users and send crafted data to skew the results. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\] demonstrates that LDP is highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can obscure the distribution of honest users' inputs. These findings underscore the need for more robust mechanisms to ensure the integrity of DP protocols.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed verifiable DP mechanisms that allow for the verification of the correctness of the noise added during the privatization process \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. The concept of verifiable differential privacy has been explored in \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\], where the authors introduce a framework that enables verification of DP guarantees. This aligns with the proposed Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP) definition, which incorporates additional verification mechanisms to ensure the correctness of DP execution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. 

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) have also been considered as a means to achieve verifiable DP. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as demonstrated in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. This finding is relevant to the discussion of VDDP, where ZKPs are not required for the protocol to function correctly. 

Several works have proposed mechanisms to improve the efficiency and accuracy of DP protocols. ### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-(ldp)-and-adversarial-manipulation'></a>Local Differential Privacy (LDP) and Adversarial Manipulation: 7 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has been widely studied for its ability to protect user privacy while enabling data analytics, but recent research highlights its vulnerabilities to adversarial manipulation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Several studies have shown that malicious data providers can manipulate their outputs before sending them to the data collector, exploiting the fact that estimations are normalized using the randomization probability defined in the LDP protocol \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This allows attackers to skew or even control the results, undermining the privacy guarantees \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. To counteract these threats, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms that enable the data collector to verify the completeness of the randomization process for each user, thereby preventing output manipulation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, some works have focused on improving the robustness of LDP protocols against data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users to distort the results \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These attacks are particularly effective when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large, as a small fraction of users can obscure the true distribution of honest users’ inputs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. To address these challenges, new protocols have been designed to be more resilient to manipulation, especially for tasks like frequency estimation and heavy hitter identification \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Furthermore, post-processing techniques have been explored to enhance the accuracy of LDP protocols by leveraging constraints such as non-negative frequencies and normalization \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. While these approaches improve robustness, they often come with trade-offs in terms of computational overhead or accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Overall, the field continues to evolve with a growing emphasis on both theoretical foundations and practical implementations of LDP protocols \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/770ad70607ce60d8c411916129a423b5aad45e93)\].

	Document 2: [Snippet]: «Differential Privacy (DP) is often presented as a strong privacy-enhancing technology with broad applicability and advocated as a de facto standard for releasing aggregate statistics on sensitive data. However, in many embodiments, DP introduces a new attack surface: a malicious entity entrusted with releasing statistics could manipulate the results and use the randomness of DP as a convenient smokescreen to mask its nefariousness. Since revealing the random noise would obviate the purpose of introducing it, the miscreant may have a perfect alibi. To close this loophole, we introduce the idea of Interactive Proofs For Differential Privacy, which requires the publishing entity to output a zero knowledge proof that convinces an efficient verifier that the output is both DP and reliable. Such a definition might seem unachievable, as a verifier must validate that DP randomness was generated faithfully without learning anything about the randomness itself. We resolve this paradox by carefully mixing private and public randomness to compute verifiable DP counting queries with theoretical guarantees and show that it is also practical for real-world deployment. We also demonstrate that computational assumptions are necessary by showing a separation between information-theoretic DP and computational DP under our definition of verifiability.»
