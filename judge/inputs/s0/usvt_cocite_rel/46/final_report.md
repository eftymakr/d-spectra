## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in recommender systems by leveraging user behavior across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Early approaches primarily relied on Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to model sequential patterns \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. While MCs are effective in sparse datasets, they fail to capture long-term dependencies, whereas RNNs, though more expressive, suffer from computational inefficiency and difficulty in handling long sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, self-attention based models like SASRec were introduced, which enable the capture of long-term semantics while maintaining computational efficiency by focusing on relevant items in the user's history \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\].

Several works have explored the use of domain-aware modules to enhance cross-domain recommendations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. For example, DA-GCN introduces a domain-aware graph convolution network to learn user-specific node representations and employs attention mechanisms to guide message passing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Similarly, DASL proposes a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains to improve cross-domain CTR predictions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. These methods often focus on transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain but may overlook the inter-sequence relationships and the potential for bidirectional knowledge exchange \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\].

Recent studies have also emphasized the importance of modeling inter-sequence and intra-sequence relationships in CDSR. C2DSR, for instance, leverages both intra- and inter-sequence item interactions to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. The model uses graph neural networks to mine collaborative relationships and sequential attentive encoders to capture sequential dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Additionally, the paper introduces a contrastive cross-domain infomax objective to enhance the correlation between single- and cross-domain user representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\].

Other approaches, such as π-Net and PSJNet, have focused on shared-account cross-domain scenarios, where multiple users share a single account and behaviors are available across domains \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. π-Net introduces a shared account filter unit and a cross-domain transfer unit to address the challenges of shared accounts and cross-domain settings \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. ### <a id='category-cross-domain-knowledge-transfer-mechanisms'></a>Cross-Domain Knowledge Transfer Mechanisms: 19 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research direction to address data sparsity and cold-start issues in sequential recommendation systems by leveraging interactions across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms within transformers, augmenting them with domain-specific modules to enhance performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. However, these methods typically overlook the inherent power of the self-attention module itself in capturing cross-domain correlations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. To address this, recent works have focused on improving the self-attention mechanism directly, such as introducing Pareto-optimal self-attention to model cross-domain learning as a multi-objective optimization problem \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. This approach dynamically minimizes cross-domain attention scores while optimizing the recommendation task, leading to automated knowledge transfer in CDSR, termed AutoCDSR \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. AutoCDSR not only mitigates negative transfer but also encourages complementary knowledge exchange between domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Furthermore, AutoCDSR+ has been proposed as a more performant variant with minimal additional computational overhead, enabling plug-and-play integration into existing transformer-based recommenders \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Several studies have explored alternative strategies for cross-domain knowledge transfer, such as using graph neural networks to mine inter-sequence item relationships \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\], dual learning mechanisms for bidirectional information transfer \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\], and contrastive learning objectives to enhance alignment between single- and cross-domain representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Additionally, some works have addressed data sparsity and class imbalance issues by incorporating supervised contrastive learning and focal loss \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. These methods collectively highlight the importance of leveraging both intra- and inter-domain interactions while addressing practical challenges like data distribution shifts and open-world assumptions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].

### <a id='category-shared-account-cross-domain-models'></a>Shared Account Cross-Domain Models: 5 papers found.
Shared-account cross-domain sequential recommendation (SCSR) is a challenging task that requires modeling sequential user behaviors across multiple domains while accounting for shared accounts, where multiple users share a single account \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. Existing approaches often rely on RNN-based models to mine sequential patterns, but these models struggle to capture complex relationships among multiple entities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Additionally, many methods focus on latent space knowledge transfer between domains, neglecting the explicit cross-domain graph structure \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\]. To address these limitations, recent works have proposed graph-based solutions such as DA-GCN, which constructs domain-specific graphs and employs domain-aware graph convolution networks to learn user-specific representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Other approaches, like π-Net, introduce parallel information-sharing mechanisms to handle shared accounts and cross-domain interactions by using shared account filter units and cross-domain transfer units \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. Similarly, PSJNet adopts a split-join framework to separate role-specific behaviors and then combine them for cross-domain recommendations \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. These methods highlight the importance of explicitly modeling both domain-specific and cross-domain interactions, as well as addressing the challenges of shared account identification and behavior filtering \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08088)\]. Despite these advances, there remains a need for more efficient and scalable solutions that can effectively handle the complexities of SCSR \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\].
