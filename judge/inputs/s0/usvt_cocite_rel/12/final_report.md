## Related Works

Event cameras have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional frame-based cameras due to their high temporal resolution and ability to capture dynamic scenes efficiently \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces challenges in processing and analyzing the information, particularly in terms of bandwidth, memory, and computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Prior work has attempted to address these challenges by exploring event downsampling techniques, which aim to reduce the volume of data while preserving essential visual information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Most existing approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit their adaptability to different applications and environments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. 

Several studies have investigated the use of event cameras for various vision tasks, including optical flow estimation, object classification, and visual-inertial odometry \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07165)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. For instance, EV-FlowNet introduces a self-supervised deep learning pipeline for optical flow estimation from event data, leveraging grayscale images captured at the same time as the events as a supervisory signal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Similarly, Event-Based Vision: A Survey provides a comprehensive overview of the field, highlighting the unique properties of event cameras and the challenges in processing their output \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. 

In addition to optical flow, event cameras have been used for depth estimation and egomotion prediction. Unsupervised Event-Based Learning of Optical Flow, Depth, and Egomotion proposes a framework that learns motion information directly from event streams without requiring labeled data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. This work demonstrates the potential of event-based learning for tasks that require understanding of scene dynamics \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. 

Event cameras have also been integrated with traditional computer vision techniques. Events-To-Video proposes a method to reconstruct videos from event data, enabling the use of off-the-shelf computer vision algorithms on event-based inputs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\]. This approach has been validated on tasks such as object classification and visual-inertial odometry, showing that reconstructions from event data can outperform algorithms specifically designed for event streams \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\]. 

Furthermore, the development of large-scale datasets has been crucial for advancing event-based vision. The Multivehicle Stereo Event Camera Dataset provides synchronized event streams, grayscale images, and IMU readings, enabling the evaluation of algorithms in diverse environments \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10202)\]. Similarly, Video to Events presents a method for converting existing video datasets into synthetic event data, allowing the use of large-scale labeled datasets for training event-based models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03095)\].

### <a id='category-vision-tasks-and-applications'></a>Vision Tasks and Applications: 4 papers found.
Event-based vision has garnered significant attention for its ability to capture dynamic scenes with high temporal resolution and robustness to challenging conditions such as high-speed motion and high dynamic range \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. Unlike traditional frame-based cameras, event cameras asynchronously record per-pixel brightness changes, resulting in a stream of events that encode time, location, and intensity changes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. This unique output has led to the development of specialized algorithms and deep learning models tailored for event-based data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. For instance, EV-FlowNet introduces a self-supervised deep learning pipeline for optical flow estimation, leveraging grayscale images captured alongside event streams as supervisory signals \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. Similarly, research has explored the use of event cameras in motion estimation tasks, such as predicting vehicle steering angles, where they outperform traditional cameras in challenging illumination and fast motion scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07165)\]. Additionally, unsupervised learning frameworks have been proposed to extract motion information directly from event streams, enabling tasks like motion blur reduction and egomotion estimation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in adapting vision algorithms to the unique characteristics of event-based data, paving the way for more efficient and adaptive computer vision systems \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\].

### <a id='category-dataset-development-for-event-cameras'></a>Dataset Development for Event Cameras: 4 papers found.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Event cameras are novel sensors that output brightness changes in the form of a stream of asynchronous "events" instead of intensity frames. They offer significant advantages with respect to conventional cameras: high dynamic range (HDR), high temporal resolution, and no motion blur. Recently, novel learning approaches operating on event data have achieved impressive results. Yet, these methods require a large amount of event data for training, which is hardly available due the novelty of event sensors in computer vision research. In this paper, we present a method that addresses these needs by converting any existing video dataset recorded with conventional cameras to synthetic event data. This unlocks the use of a virtually unlimited number of existing video datasets for training networks designed for real event data. We evaluate our method on two relevant vision tasks, i.e., object recognition and semantic segmentation, and show that models trained on synthetic events have several benefits: (i) they generalize well to real event data, even in scenarios where standard-camera images are blurry or overexposed, by inheriting the outstanding properties of event cameras; (ii) they can be used for fine-tuning on real data to improve over state-of-the-art for both classification and semantic segmentation.»
