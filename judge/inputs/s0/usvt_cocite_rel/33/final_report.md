## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a prominent approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These methods aim to mitigate the degradation of performance on previously learned tasks when new tasks are introduced. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is widely used, its optimality remains an open question \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. 

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of rehearsal strategies in CL. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\] examined the use of experience replay buffers for all past events, combining on- and off-policy learning, and found that this approach could reduce catastrophic forgetting in both Atari and DMLab domains. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\] proposed Deep Generative Replay, a framework inspired by the hippocampus's role in short-term memory, which enables the sampling and interleaving of past and new task data. This method has shown promising results in sequential learning settings involving image classification tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\].

The concept of sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data sequentially, has also been explored. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\] introduced a task-based hard attention mechanism that preserves previous tasks' information without affecting the current task's learning. This approach effectively reduces catastrophic forgetting by up to 80% \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. Additionally, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\] investigated the use of tiny episodic memories for continual learning and found that a simple baseline, which jointly trains on both current and memory examples, outperformed state-of-the-art CL approaches \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\].

In the context of online continual learning, \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\] proposed a method that retrieves the most interfered samples for replay, leading to improved performance and reduced forgetting. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where data is streamed continuously and cannot be revisited \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] introduced ExStream, a memory-efficient rehearsal algorithm that performs well with far less memory and computation compared to full rehearsal \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\].

Theoretical analysis of rehearsal-based CL has also gained attention. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\] provided the first explicit form of expected forgetting and generalization error under overparameterized linear models, showing that sequential rehearsal performs better when tasks are less similar \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\]. ### <a id='category-experience-replay-techniques'></a>Experience Replay Techniques: 7 papers found.
Experience replay techniques have emerged as a critical approach to mitigating catastrophic forgetting in continual learning, where models must retain knowledge from previous tasks while learning new ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These techniques typically involve storing past data in a buffer and reusing it during training to prevent overfitting to recent tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. Several studies have explored the effectiveness of experience replay, particularly in reinforcement learning settings, where agents encounter sequential tasks without explicit task boundaries \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. For instance, one approach involves using a buffer to store all past events and combining on- and off-policy learning with behavioral cloning to improve generalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Others have focused on optimizing the selection of samples from the replay buffer, proposing methods such as reservoir sampling or controlled sampling based on the impact of parameter updates \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. These strategies aim to enhance performance by ensuring that the replayed data is representative and minimizes interference with previously learned tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Additionally, generative replay, which involves reconstructing past experiences, has been shown to be effective in maintaining long-term memory and reducing forgetting \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. Some methods integrate generative models directly into the main model to improve efficiency and scalability \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Overall, experience replay remains a promising avenue for continual learning, with ongoing research focused on improving its effectiveness under various constraints and scenarios \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\].

Yet standard training methods for deep networks often suffer from catastrophic forgetting, where learning new tasks erases knowledge of earlier tasks. While catastrophic forgetting labels the problem, the theoretical reasons for interference between tasks remain unclear. Here, we attempt to narrow this gap between theory and practice by studying continual learning in the teacher-student setup. We extend previous analytical work on two-layer networks in the teacher-student setup to multiple teachers. Using each teacher to represent a different task, we investigate how the relationship between teachers affects the amount of forgetting and transfer exhibited by the student when the task switches. In line with recent work, we find that when tasks depend on similar features, intermediate task similarity leads to greatest forgetting. However, feature similarity is only one way in which tasks may be related. The teacher-student approach allows us to disentangle task similarity at the level of readouts (hidden-to-output weights) and features (input-to-hidden weights). We find a complex interplay between both types of similarity, initial transfer/forgetting rates, maximum transfer/forgetting, and long-term transfer/forgetting. Together, these results help illuminate the diverse factors contributing to catastrophic forgetting.»
