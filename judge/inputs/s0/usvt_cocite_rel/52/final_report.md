## Related Works

Dense retrievers have gained significant traction in information retrieval due to their ability to encode text into dense vector representations that can be efficiently compared using shallow similarity operations such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These models typically rely on pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) that are fine-tuned using contrastive learning to align queries with relevant documents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. However, recent research has questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may not significantly alter the underlying knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\].

This debate has primarily focused on BERT-based encoders using the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative dense retriever \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. The current study extends this analysis by testing different representation approaches, such as CLS token versus mean pooling, and different backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Additionally, we evaluate performance across multiple datasets, including MSMARCO and Natural Questions, to generalize findings beyond the original BERT-based setup \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

Our results confirm that in DPR tuning, pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance, with fine-tuning primarily adjusting neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. However, this pattern does not hold universally. For example, in mean-pooled models like Contriever, and in decoder-based models like LLaMA, fine-tuning plays a more significant role in shaping retrieval performance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\]. This suggests that the effectiveness of fine-tuning may depend on the architecture and representation strategy employed \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\].

Several prior works have explored the training of dense retrievers. For instance, the Condenser architecture was introduced to improve dense encoders by pre-training with a novel Transformer design that conditions on dense representations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. Similarly, the coCondenser model adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of pre-training strategies in achieving effective dense retrieval \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13602)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-approaches-in-retrieval'></a>Contrastive Learning Approaches in Retrieval: 11 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a pivotal approach in training dense retrievers, enabling models to learn effective text representations without relying heavily on labeled data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This method leverages self-supervised objectives to align queries and passages in a shared embedding space, improving retrieval performance through contrastive loss functions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Several studies have explored variations of contrastive learning to address specific challenges in dense retrieval, such as training data noise, negative instance representation, and computational efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. For instance, coCondenser introduces an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, ANCE constructs negative examples using an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, resolving the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Additionally, efficient training techniques like TAS-Balanced have been proposed to reduce computational requirements while maintaining high retrieval accuracy \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance dense retrievers through contrastive learning, emphasizing both theoretical insights and practical improvements in training mechanisms \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]\[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\].

### <a id='category-reproducibility-and-public-implementation'></a>Reproducibility and Public Implementation: 3 papers found.
The category "Reproducibility and Public Implementation" highlights the importance of transparent and replicable research practices in natural language processing (NLP). Several studies emphasize the challenges of comparing different training approaches due to variations in hyperparameters, dataset sizes, and computational resources \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. For instance, a replication study of BERT pretraining found that the model was significantly undertrained and could match or exceed the performance of later models, underscoring the impact of hyperparameter choices and training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Additionally, efforts to improve the scalability and efficiency of BERT through parameter-reduction techniques have been proposed, demonstrating how these methods can enhance training speed and model performance while maintaining competitive results on benchmarks like GLUE and SQuAD \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)\]. Furthermore, the development of Universal Language Model Fine-tuning (ULMFiT) showcases how transfer learning methods can be applied broadly across NLP tasks, achieving state-of-the-art results with minimal labeled data \[[29](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1e077413b25c4d34945cc2707e17e46ed4fe784a)\]. These works collectively stress the need for open-source implementations and rigorous experimental setups to ensure that research findings are both verifiable and reproducible. The availability of code and pre-trained models further supports the community in building upon prior work, fostering continued innovation in the field.

### <a id='category-dense-passage-retrieval-(dpr)-framework'></a>Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) Framework: 7 papers found.
In the context of dense passage retrieval (DPR), recent research has focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of retrieving relevant documents for open-domain question answering. Traditional methods such as BM25 and TF-IDF have been widely used, but they rely on sparse vector space models and lack the flexibility to adapt to different downstream tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. In contrast, dense retrievers leverage pre-trained language models to generate continuous, low-dimensional embeddings for both queries and documents, enabling more nuanced semantic matching through inner product or cosine similarity \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. However, training dense retrievers remains challenging due to issues such as the discrepancy between training and inference, the presence of unlabeled positives, and limited training data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed optimized training approaches like RocketQA, which introduces techniques such as cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives, and data augmentation to enhance retrieval performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that dense retrievers can be further improved by incorporating lexical matching capabilities, as seen in the Salient Phrase Aware Retriever (SPAR), which combines the strengths of dense and sparse models \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06918)\]. Moreover, the role of pre-training versus fine-tuning in dense retrievers has been explored, with findings indicating that pre-trained knowledge significantly underpins retrieval performance, although this pattern varies across different architectures and datasets \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of DPR frameworks toward more robust and adaptable retrieval systems.
