## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match speech content and emotion. Early approaches relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, but recent advances have shifted toward data-driven methods using deep learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. These methods typically involve mapping audio signals to facial animations, often using pre-trained self-supervised audio models as encoders \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. However, challenges persist, particularly in accurately capturing lip movements and facial expressions that align with speech content \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\].

One notable work, **VOCA**, introduced a neural network that factors identity from facial motion, enabling realistic animation of a wide range of adult faces based on any speech signal \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. This model was trained on a large 4D face dataset, allowing it to generalize to unseen subjects without retargeting \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Similarly, **FaceFormer** proposed a Transformer-based autoregressive model that encodes long-term audio context and autoregressively predicts a sequence of animated 3D face meshes, improving upon prior phoneme-level feature learning \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. The use of self-supervised pre-trained speech representations was also emphasized to address data scarcity issues \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\].

Another approach, **MeshTalk**, introduced a categorical latent space for facial animation that disentangles audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information using a novel cross-modality loss \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. This enabled highly accurate lip motion and plausible animation of non-speech-related facial features like eye blinks and brow movements \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. **EmoTalk** further extended this by proposing an end-to-end neural network to disentangle emotions from speech content, generating rich 3D facial expressions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\]. This work also addressed the scarcity of 3D emotional talking face data by leveraging facial blendshapes for plausible 3D face reconstruction \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

**Audio-Driven Emotional Video Portraits (EVP)** decomposed speech into two decoupled spaces—duration-independent emotion and duration-dependent content—enabling dynamic 2D emotional facial landmarks to be deduced \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09488)\]. This was then used to generate high-quality video portraits with vivid emotional dynamics \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09488)\]. **SelfTalk** introduced a self-supervised commutative training diagram to learn 3D talking faces by involving cross-modal features exchange between audio, text, and lip shape \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. This framework enabled more plausible lip shapes by leveraging knowledge from the lip-reading interpreter \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\].

### <a id='category-3d-facial-animation-techniques'></a>3D Facial Animation Techniques: 14 papers found.
Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in recent research, with various approaches aiming to improve realism, accuracy, and scalability. Early methods often relied on regression models, which faced challenges such as over-smoothing and poor generalization due to the ill-posed nature of audio-visual mapping \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed transformer-based models like Face-Former, which leverage long-term audio context and autoregressive prediction for more natural facial motion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Additionally, methods such as SelfTalk introduce self-supervised learning in cross-modal networks, enabling the system to learn intricate connections between audio, text, and lip shape \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. Another approach, Imitator, focuses on identity-specific details by learning from short reference videos, allowing for more personalized and realistic facial expressions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00023)\]. The use of 4D face datasets, such as those used in VOCA, has also been critical in training models that can generalize across different speakers and languages \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. More recently, diffusion-based models like FaceDiffuser have been introduced to generate non-deterministic facial animations, offering greater variability and realism \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11306)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to overcome limitations in data scarcity, cross-modal alignment, and identity-specific modeling, with a growing emphasis on end-to-end solutions and real-time applicability \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\].

### <a id='category-emotion-and-expression-modeling-in-facial-animation'></a>Emotion and Expression Modeling in Facial Animation: 8 papers found.
In the field of emotion and expression modeling in facial animation, recent research has focused on improving the disentanglement of emotional and content-based features in speech-driven 3D face animation. Several studies have highlighted the limitations of existing methods, which often neglect emotional facial expressions or fail to separate them from speech content \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. For instance, one approach introduces an emotion disentangling encoder (EDE) that decomposes speech into emotion and content spaces by cross-reconstructed speech signals with different emotion labels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. Another work proposes the Cross-Reconstructed Emotion Disentanglement technique, which separates speech into a duration-independent emotion space and a duration-dependent content space, enabling the deduction of dynamic 2D emotional facial landmarks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Similarly, the Emotion-Aware Motion Model (EAMM) incorporates an Implicit Emotion Displacement Learner to represent emotion-related facial dynamics as linearly additive displacements to motion representations, enhancing emotional expressiveness \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. Additionally, EMOTE introduces a content-emotion exchange mechanism to supervise different emotions on the same audio while maintaining lip motion synchronized with speech \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. These methods collectively aim to generate more natural and emotionally expressive 3D facial animations by addressing the challenges of emotional disentanglement and lip-sync accuracy. The proposed Wav2Sem module further contributes to this field by introducing a semantic decorrelation approach to improve the precision and naturalness of facial animations by decoupling audio features \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\].
