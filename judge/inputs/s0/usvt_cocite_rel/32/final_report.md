## Related Works

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have emphasized the importance of understanding how feature representations evolve across layers, as this knowledge is critical for improving interpretability and robustness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)\]. Several studies have explored the dynamics of representation learning in LLMs, focusing on how layers contribute to specific functions or behaviors. For example, some works have identified critical layers linked to particular tasks or behaviors through data-dependent analyses of fine-tuned models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. However, these approaches are typically limited to post-hoc settings, as they rely on fine-tuned models rather than pre-fine-tuned ones \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

In contrast, our work introduces a data-oblivious approach to identify intrinsic critical layers in pre-fine-tuned LLMs by analyzing representation dynamics using Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04366)\]. This method enables us to uncover patterns that are consistent across tasks for a given model, offering a more generalizable understanding of layer importance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13255)\]. We show that layers with significant shifts in representation space are also those most affected during fine-tuning—a pattern that holds consistently across tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10199)\].

Our spectral analysis further reveals that these shifts are driven by changes in the top principal components, which encode semantic transitions from rationales to conclusions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)\]. This finding aligns with prior work on the intrinsic dimensionality of language models, which suggests that pre-trained models have a low intrinsic dimension, allowing for effective fine-tuning with relatively few parameters \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)\]. Additionally, this insight connects with studies on parameter-efficient transfer learning, where methods such as LoRA and adapters have been shown to achieve strong performance by modifying only a small subset of parameters \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05365)\].

We apply these findings to two practical scenarios: efficient domain adaptation and backdoor defense. For domain adaptation, we demonstrate that fine-tuning critical layers leads to greater loss reduction compared to non-critical layers \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00751)\]. This aligns with prior work on efficient fine-tuning methods, such as BitFit, which shows that modifying only bias terms can be competitive with full fine-tuning \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1e077413b25c4d34945cc2707e17e46ed4fe784a)\]. ### <a id='category-parameter-efficient-transfer-learning'></a>Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning: 14 papers found.
Parameter-efficient transfer learning methods aim to fine-tune large pre-trained language models with minimal parameter updates, offering a balance between performance and computational efficiency \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. These approaches are crucial as full fine-tuning becomes impractical with increasing model size and task diversity \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04366)\]. For instance, LoRA introduces low-rank adaptation by injecting trainable matrices into each Transformer layer, reducing trainable parameters by up to 10,000 times while maintaining performance comparable to full fine-tuning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)\]. Similarly, BitFit modifies only bias terms, achieving competitive results with smaller datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10199)\]. Adapter modules add minimal parameters per task, enabling parameter sharing across tasks without retraining the entire model \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00751)\]. Prefix-tuning optimizes task-specific vectors, keeping parameters frozen and improving performance in low-data settings \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.00190)\]. These methods highlight the potential of sparse fine-tuning to enhance model adaptability without excessive resource consumption \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05687)\]. The effectiveness of these techniques is further supported by empirical studies showing that intrinsic dimensionality of pre-trained models is low, allowing efficient fine-tuning with fewer parameters \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13255)\]. Overall, parameter-efficient transfer learning represents a promising direction for scalable and cost-effective model adaptation \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01933)\].

### <a id='category-reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback-(rlhf)'></a>Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF): 4 papers found.
The Related Works section for the paper's category on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) highlights several approaches aimed at aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences. Existing methods often rely on complex and unstable procedures, such as first training a reward model to reflect human preferences and then fine-tuning the LLM using reinforcement learning to maximize this reward \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This process, known as RLHF, involves significant computational resources and hyperparameter tuning \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06767)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed alternative frameworks like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which simplifies the process by enabling the extraction of optimal policies in closed form using a simple classification loss, thereby eliminating the need for sampling from the LLM during fine-tuning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Similarly, Reward rAnked FineTuning (RAFT) introduces a method where high-quality samples are selected and used for fine-tuning, improving model performance in both reward learning and automated metrics \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06767)\]. These approaches underscore the ongoing efforts to develop more stable and efficient methods for aligning LMs with human preferences, emphasizing the importance of reducing computational overhead and enhancing model reliability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.06767)\].
