## Related Works

Calibration of machine learning models has been a critical area of research, particularly in ensuring that predicted probabilities reflect the true likelihood of correct predictions. Early work on calibration focused on traditional models, such as logistic regression, where calibration techniques like Platt scaling were introduced \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. However, modern neural networks, due to their complexity and training procedures, often exhibit poor calibration \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue has been attributed to factors such as depth, width, and normalization techniques \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. 

Recent studies have explored post-processing methods to improve calibration. Temperature scaling, a simple variant of Platt scaling, has been shown to be surprisingly effective in calibrating predictions across various architectures and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. However, popular recalibration methods like Platt scaling and temperature scaling have been found to be less calibrated than reported, and current techniques struggle to estimate the degree of miscalibration \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. An alternative, histogram binning, has also been proposed but suffers from sample inefficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. To address these limitations, the scaling-binning calibrator was introduced, combining parametric fitting with binning to achieve better calibration with fewer samples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\].

In the context of deep learning, Bayesian methods such as Bayesian neural networks have been proposed for uncertainty estimation, though they are computationally expensive and require significant modifications to training procedures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. A more scalable alternative, deep ensembles, has been shown to produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates that are as good or better than Bayesian methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. This approach is simple to implement and can be parallelized, making it suitable for large-scale applications \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\].

Another line of work focuses on the calibration of ranking models. Scale calibration has been identified as a critical issue in learning-to-rank systems, where ranking functions often lack scale calibration, leading to practical deployment challenges \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f6bb146b9c1504ec77100e640d624bdc204f0ac)\]. This problem is exacerbated by the translation-invariance of popular ranking losses, which can cause training instability \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f6bb146b9c1504ec77100e640d624bdc204f0ac)\]. To address this, several studies have proposed calibrated ranking losses that ensure both ranking quality and score scale calibration \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f6bb146b9c1504ec77100e640d624bdc204f0ac)\].

In addition, mixup training has been shown to improve calibration in deep neural networks by introducing label smoothing and feature mixing, which reduces overconfidence in predictions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\]. This approach leads to better-calibrated models, especially in out-of-distribution scenarios \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\].

Furthermore, the importance of uncertainty estimation in deep learning has been highlighted, with methods such as dropout being proposed as a Bayesian approximation to model uncertainty \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)\]. This approach allows for uncertainty estimation without sacrificing computational efficiency or test accuracy \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)\].

These advancements underscore the importance of developing flexible and effective calibration strategies to improve the reliability of neural network predictions in real-world scenarios.
