## Related Works

Video frame interpolation has been a focal point in video processing research, with numerous methods proposed to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones. Traditional approaches typically involve two stages: motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, these methods are often limited by the accuracy of optical flow estimation and the computational complexity of synthesizing intermediate frames \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent advances have shifted toward end-to-end deep learning approaches that combine motion estimation and synthesis into a single process \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\].

Several works have explored the use of adaptive convolutional networks for video frame interpolation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\] introduced a method that considers pixel synthesis as a local convolution over two input frames, allowing the network to capture both motion and synthesis in a single step. This approach eliminates the need for explicit optical flow estimation and enables robust handling of challenges like occlusion and blur \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\] proposed a separable convolution approach that reduces the number of parameters required to estimate motion and synthesis, leading to more efficient models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\].

The use of deep neural networks for video frame interpolation has also led to the development of models that can handle large motions and complex scenes. For example, \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\] introduced a method that formulates motion estimation as a classification task rather than regression, leading to improved performance on high-resolution datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12622)\]. Additionally, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\] proposed a dynamic architecture that assigns varying amounts of computation to different regions of the video, significantly reducing computational costs while maintaining performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\].

Another line of research focuses on the integration of perceptual loss functions to improve the visual quality of interpolated frames. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\] introduced a method that incorporates perceptual loss to train the neural network to produce visually pleasing frames, while \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00627)\] proposed a technique that uses a flow reversal layer to estimate flow fields starting from the unknown target frame to the source frame, improving the accuracy of interpolation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\].

Moreover, the use of deep learning for video compression and view synthesis has also been explored. \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3a6e72172b7e1fb3b45f53e9aaf7d42e427c26de)\] presented an end-to-end deep learning codec that treats video compression as repeated image interpolation, benefiting from recent advances in deep image interpolation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. Similarly, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\] proposed a motion estimation and compensation-driven neural network that integrates both optical flow and interpolation kernels to synthesize target frame pixels, leading to more efficient and accurate results \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05534)\].
