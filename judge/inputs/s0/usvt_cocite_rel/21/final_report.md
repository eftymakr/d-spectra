## Related Works

Video frame interpolation has been a focal point in video processing research, with numerous methods proposed to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones. Traditional approaches typically involve two stages: motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, these methods are often limited by the accuracy of optical flow estimation and the computational complexity of synthesizing intermediate frames \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent advances have shifted toward end-to-end deep learning approaches that combine motion estimation and synthesis into a single process \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\].

Several works have explored the use of adaptive convolutional networks for video frame interpolation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\] introduced a method that considers pixel synthesis as a local convolution over two input frames, allowing the network to capture both motion and synthesis in a single step. This approach eliminates the need for explicit optical flow estimation and enables robust handling of challenges like occlusion and blur \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\] proposed a separable convolution approach that reduces the number of parameters required to estimate motion and synthesis, leading to more efficient models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\].

The use of deep neural networks for video frame interpolation has also led to the development of models that can handle large motions and complex scenes. For example, \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\] introduced a method that formulates motion estimation as a classification task rather than regression, leading to improved performance on high-resolution datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12622)\]. Additionally, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\] proposed a dynamic architecture that assigns varying amounts of computation to different regions of the video, significantly reducing computational costs while maintaining performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\].

Another line of research focuses on the integration of perceptual loss functions to improve the visual quality of interpolated frames. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\] introduced a method that incorporates perceptual loss to train the neural network to produce visually pleasing frames, while \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00627)\] proposed a technique that uses a flow reversal layer to estimate flow fields starting from the unknown target frame to the source frame, improving the accuracy of interpolation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\].

Moreover, the use of deep learning for video compression and view synthesis has also been explored. \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3a6e72172b7e1fb3b45f53e9aaf7d42e427c26de)\] presented an end-to-end deep learning codec that treats video compression as repeated image interpolation, benefiting from recent advances in deep image interpolation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. Similarly, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\] proposed a motion estimation and compensation-driven neural network that integrates both optical flow and interpolation kernels to synthesize target frame pixels, leading to more efficient and accurate results \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05534)\].

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-techniques'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Techniques: 18 papers found.
Video frame interpolation techniques have evolved significantly, particularly with the integration of deep learning approaches that address traditional limitations such as motion estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation followed by frame synthesis, but recent advances have merged these steps into a single process, often using spatially adaptive convolution kernels \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. This approach allows for more efficient handling of complex motions, occlusions, and brightness changes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Some methods employ separable convolutions to reduce parameter counts and improve scalability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Others focus on unsupervised learning, leveraging cycle consistency and pseudo-supervised losses to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring ground-truth data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\]. Additionally, methods like IM-Net and PhaseNet have been developed to handle high-resolution and large motion scenarios, with IM-Net achieving better performance through classification-based motion estimation \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\], while PhaseNet improves robustness using phase-based motion representations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\]. Quadratic interpolation methods have also emerged, incorporating acceleration information to better model curvilinear trajectories \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00627)\]. Furthermore, depth-aware approaches have been introduced to explicitly detect occlusions and improve synthesis by leveraging depth information \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. These advancements collectively demonstrate a shift toward more efficient, scalable, and high-quality video frame interpolation techniques, often driven by end-to-end training and adaptive kernel estimation \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3a6e72172b7e1fb3b45f53e9aaf7d42e427c26de)\].

### <a id='category-end-to-end-deep-learning-approaches'></a>End-to-End Deep Learning Approaches: 8 papers found.
End-to-end deep learning approaches have significantly advanced video frame interpolation and neural rendering by integrating motion estimation, occlusion reasoning, and implicit scene representation. Early methods relied heavily on optical flow estimation, which introduced complexity and error propagation, especially in scenes with large motion or heavy occlusion \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. To address these limitations, researchers proposed end-to-end models that eliminate the need for explicit optical flow computation, instead using techniques like PixelShuffle and channel attention to extract motion information directly from pixel-level features \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. These models demonstrated superior performance in challenging scenarios, achieving high-quality frame interpolation without the need for additional motion estimation modules \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Furthermore, some approaches extended beyond video interpolation, incorporating implicit scene representations that encode both geometry and appearance in a continuous 3D space \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. These representations, trained end-to-end from 2D images and camera poses, enabled novel view synthesis and joint shape-appearance interpolation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. In parallel, deep learning has also been applied to video compression, where frame interpolation techniques were leveraged to improve the efficiency and quality of video codecs \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06919)\]. Additionally, methods like Scene Representation Networks (SRNs) and NeuS have further refined neural rendering by addressing issues such as self-occlusion and surface reconstruction accuracy \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]\[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]. These developments highlight the growing trend of using end-to-end deep learning to unify complex visual tasks, from motion modeling to 3D scene reconstruction, while reducing reliance on traditional, hand-crafted pipelines \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06919)\].

### <a id='category-dynamic-architectures-for-computational-efficiency'></a>Dynamic Architectures for Computational Efficiency: 2 papers found.
Dynamic architectures for computational efficiency focus on optimizing the computational resources required for complex tasks like neural rendering and video frame interpolation. These approaches aim to reduce the computational overhead by dynamically allocating resources based on the task's requirements. For instance, in neural rendering, the challenge lies in generating high-quality free-viewpoint renderings from limited input data, which often involves computationally intensive processes such as ray marching \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. To address this, Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF) introduce a sparse voxel octree structure that allows for efficient rendering by skipping irrelevant voxels, significantly improving inference speed while maintaining quality \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. Similarly, in video frame interpolation, the computational cost can be reduced by assigning varying amounts of computation to different regions based on their motion complexity \[[30](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4b2ff4acdbefe5a0aafa8b989f28f220f9c750bb)\]. This dynamic allocation ensures that static regions require less computation, while regions with significant motion are processed with greater detail, leading to a reduction in FLOPs by up to 50% without compromising performance \[[30](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4b2ff4acdbefe5a0aafa8b989f28f220f9c750bb)\]. These methods exemplify the broader trend of leveraging dynamic architectures to enhance computational efficiency in neural models, making them more scalable and practical for real-world applications.
