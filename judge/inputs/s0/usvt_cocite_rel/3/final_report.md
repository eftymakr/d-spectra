## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, particularly through the development of end-to-end models that generate high-quality speech directly from text \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\]. These models, such as Tacotron \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\], FastSpeech \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\], and Fastpitch \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\], have enabled parallel processing and faster inference compared to autoregressive models. However, these systems often lack fine-grained control over prosody, which is essential for generating expressive and contextually appropriate speech \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\]. 

Prosody control in TTS has been a major research focus, with several approaches focusing on explicit modeling of prosodic features such as pitch, energy, and duration \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. For example, the work by \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\] proposes a model that generates speech conditioned on these acoustic correlates, allowing for flexible and interpretable control over prosody. This approach enables the generation of multiple distinct renditions of a text by modifying the acoustic features during inference. However, these methods often require additional training or specialized modules, limiting their applicability in post-hoc scenarios \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\].

Traditional approaches to mispronunciation correction rely on grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) dictionaries, which are not practical in low-resource settings due to the lack of annotated data \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\]. Recent work has explored alternative methods, such as using neural prosody generators to model prosody from text \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. These models, however, often fail to capture the full variability of natural speech, resulting in flat and less expressive output \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04233)\].

To address these limitations, several studies have proposed controllable TTS systems that allow for post-hoc adjustments of prosody and pronunciation. For example, the work by \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\] introduces "global style tokens" (GSTs), which enable the modeling of acoustic expressiveness without explicit labels. These embeddings can be used to control synthesis in novel ways, such as varying speed and speaking style independently of the text content \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\]. Similarly, \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\] proposes a user-controllable, context-aware neural prosody generator that allows for the modification of prosody constraints during inference, enhancing the flexibility of TTS systems.

Another line of research focuses on the use of variational autoencoders (VAEs) to model the distribution of prosodic features, enabling the generation of varied intonation while maintaining naturalness \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04233)\]. This approach allows for the exploration of the tails of the prior distribution, leading to more idiosyncratic and varied renditions of speech

### <a id='category-tts-system-development'></a>TTS System Development: 10 papers found.
Recent advancements in text-to-speech (TTS) systems have focused on improving speech naturalness and enabling more flexible prosody control \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Early models like Tacotron \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\] and its successor Tacotron 2 \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05884)\] introduced end-to-end frameworks that directly generate speech from text, achieving high-quality outputs comparable to professional recordings. However, these models often rely on autoregressive architectures, which are slow and difficult to train \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. To address these limitations, parallel TTS models such as FastSpeech \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\] and Glow-TTS \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\] were developed, enabling faster inference and training by generating mel-spectrograms in parallel. FastSpeech, for instance, uses a teacher-student distillation approach to improve duration prediction and reduce information loss, though it still faces challenges in capturing speech variation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Later improvements like FastSpeech 2 and 2s further enhanced voice quality by incorporating more detailed speech features such as pitch and energy as conditional inputs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Additionally, research has explored methods to improve prosody control and multi-sentence coherence, such as using long context and multi-speaker training to enhance speech naturalness \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to make TTS systems more efficient, expressive, and adaptable to different use cases.

### <a id='category-prosody-control-methods'></a>Prosody Control Methods: 5 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have enabled high-quality speech synthesis, but controlling prosody remains a challenge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Traditional methods often rely on specialized modules or additional training for prosody manipulation, limiting post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. In contrast, several works have explored methods for improving prosody control without requiring explicit prosodic labels or additional training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. For instance, some approaches introduce prosody embeddings that enable fine-grained control of speaking style by learning temporal structures in the embedding networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. These embeddings can be adjusted to modify pitch, amplitude, and other prosodic features at both the frame and phoneme levels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]. Additionally, non-autoregressive TTS models conditioned on acoustic speech features such as pitch, duration, and energy have been proposed to enable intuitive control over prosody dimensions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. These models can generate a wide variety of speaking styles while maintaining speech quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Another approach involves conditioning TTS systems on learned prosody embeddings derived from reference signals, allowing for prosody transfer between speakers \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. This method enables synthesized speech to match the prosody of a reference signal even when the speakers differ \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. Furthermore, models that explicitly condition on acoustic correlates of prosody, such as $F_0$, energy, and duration, offer more interpretable and temporally-precise control over prosody \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in improving prosody control in TTS systems through embedding-based and conditioning-based methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\].

### <a id='category-user-controllable-tts-systems'></a>User-Controllable TTS Systems: 4 papers found.
Recent advances in text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis have focused on improving the naturalness of generated speech, but user control over prosody remains a challenge \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. Existing methods often rely on predefined modules or additional training data to adjust prosody, limiting their flexibility for post-hoc modifications \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\]. In contrast, several works have explored sequence-to-sequence models that allow for more intuitive control over prosody by conditioning on acoustic features such as pitch, duration, and energy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. These models learn a latent prosody space, enabling the generation of diverse speaking styles while maintaining high quality \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. Additionally, some approaches use hierarchical latent variables to disentangle attributes like speaking style, accent, and background noise, allowing for fine-grained control over these properties \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.07217)\]. Others introduce global style tokens, which are trained without explicit labels and enable style transfer across different texts and speakers \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\]. These methods collectively highlight the growing interest in user-controllable TTS systems that can adapt to various speaking styles and conditions while preserving speech quality \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.07217)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\].

### <a id='category-variational-autoencoder-applications'></a>Variational Autoencoder Applications: 5 papers found.
In the realm of Variational Autoencoder (VAE) applications for text-to-speech (TTS) systems, recent research has focused on improving prosody control, naturalness, and the ability to generate varied speech renditions. Several studies have explored the use of VAEs to model latent variables that capture prosodic features, enabling more flexible and interpretable speech synthesis \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\]. For instance, one approach introduces a hierarchical latent variable model that conditions finer-level prosody representations on coarser-level ones, enhancing multi-resolution modeling while maintaining model stability \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\]. Another work proposes a sequential prior in a discrete latent space, leveraging vector quantization (VQ) and autoregressive models to generate more natural speech samples \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03788)\]. These methods address the limitations of standard VAE priors, which often result in unnatural and discontinuous speech due to dramatic prosodic variations between tokens \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03788)\]. Additionally, research has been conducted on decoupling reference signal alignment from the overall TTS system, using phoneme-level time stamps and VAEs to enhance latent representations for robust prosody transfer across speakers \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02479)\]. Furthermore, studies have investigated the trade-off between naturalness and intonation variation, demonstrating that sampling from the tails of the VAE prior can produce more varied intonation while maintaining naturalness \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04233)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of VAEs in enabling more expressive and controllable TTS systems.
