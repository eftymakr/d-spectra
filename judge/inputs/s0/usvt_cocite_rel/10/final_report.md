## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike traditional next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts the next $k$ tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly for large models, by enabling more efficient training and faster inference \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior studies have indicated that smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in adapting to the MTP objective \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several works have explored alternative training strategies to address these challenges. For instance, curriculum learning has been proposed as a method to gradually increase the complexity of the training objective \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05808)\]. Specifically, a forward curriculum starts with NTP and gradually transitions to MTP, allowing SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05808)\]. This approach has been shown to improve downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05808)\]. In contrast, a reverse curriculum begins with MTP and gradually reduces the number of tokens predicted, leading to stronger NTP performance and output quality but without the self-speculative decoding benefits \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05808)\].

The concept of speculative decoding has also been explored as a means to accelerate inference in autoregressive models \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. This technique involves using a draft model to generate multiple tokens in parallel, followed by verification against the main model \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. While speculative decoding can significantly reduce inference time, it is often applied in conjunction with other training strategies to maximize performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. Similarly, Medusa introduces multiple decoding heads to predict multiple subsequent tokens in parallel, achieving substantial speedups without compromising generation quality \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. These methods highlight the potential of parallel prediction strategies in improving both training and inference efficiency.

In addition to training strategies, the importance of process supervision in improving reasoning capabilities has been emphasized \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. Process supervision provides feedback at each intermediate reasoning step, leading to better performance on tasks like mathematical reasoning compared to outcome supervision \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to the MATH dataset, where process-supervised models outperformed outcome-supervised models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. Furthermore, the use of active learning has been shown to enhance the efficacy of process supervision by selecting the most informative examples for training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\].

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Large Language Models (LLMs) employ auto-regressive decoding that requires sequential computation, with each step reliant on the previous one's output. This creates a bottleneck as each step necessitates moving the full model parameters from High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) to the accelerator's cache. While methods such as speculative decoding have been suggested to address this issue, their implementation is impeded by the challenges associated with acquiring and maintaining a separate draft model. In this paper, we present Medusa, an efficient method that augments LLM inference by adding extra decoding heads to predict multiple subsequent tokens in parallel. Using a tree-based attention mechanism, Medusa constructs multiple candidate continuations and verifies them simultaneously in each decoding step. By leveraging parallel processing, Medusa substantially reduces the number of decoding steps required. We present two levels of fine-tuning procedures for Medusa to meet the needs of different use cases: Medusa-1: Medusa is directly fine-tuned on top of a frozen backbone LLM, enabling lossless inference acceleration. Medusa-2: Medusa is fine-tuned together with the backbone LLM, enabling better prediction accuracy of Medusa heads and higher speedup but needing a special training recipe that preserves the backbone model's capabilities. Moreover, we propose several extensions that improve or expand the utility of Medusa, including a self-distillation to handle situations where no training data is available and a typical acceptance scheme to boost the acceptance rate while maintaining generation quality. We evaluate Medusa on models of various sizes and training procedures. Our experiments demonstrate that Medusa-1 can achieve over 2.2x speedup without compromising generation quality, while Medusa-2 further improves the speedup to 2.3-3.6x.»
