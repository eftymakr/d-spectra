## Related Works

Long-tailed recognition remains a challenging problem in computer vision due to the skewed class distribution in real-world datasets. Several approaches have been proposed to address this issue, primarily focusing on class-balancing strategies, contrastive learning, and decoupling representation and classifier learning. 

Early methods tackled class imbalance by employing re-weighting or re-sampling techniques, such as instance-balanced sampling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. These approaches aimed to mitigate the bias towards majority classes by adjusting the loss function or modifying the training data. However, these methods often led to suboptimal representation learning due to the lack of diversity in the training samples \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. 

More recently, contrastive learning has emerged as a promising approach for learning discriminative visual representations. Supervised contrastive learning (SCL) \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11362)\] has shown significant improvements over cross-entropy by pulling together samples from the same class and pushing apart those from different classes. However, SCL struggles with long-tailed data due to the imbalance in gradient contributions from positive and negative pairs \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11362)\]. This issue is further exacerbated by the lack of uniformity in feature distributions for minority classes \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13998)\]. 

To address these limitations, several works have proposed modifications to contrastive learning. Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\] introduces class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process, improving the representation quality for tail classes. Similarly, Balanced Contrastive Learning (BCL) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\] addresses the failure of SCL to form a regular simplex by introducing class-averaging and class-complement strategies. These methods aim to improve the uniformity of feature distributions and enhance class separability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. 

Decoupling representation and classifier learning has also been explored as a way to improve long-tailed recognition. The work by \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\] demonstrated that learning high-quality representations is less affected by data imbalance, and that a simple instance-balanced sampling can lead to strong long-tailed recognition performance when combined with an appropriately designed classifier. This idea was further extended in \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02413)\], where a Bilateral-Branch Network (BBN) was proposed to jointly optimize representation and classifier learning, leading to improved performance on benchmark datasets.

This approach modifies the logits based on label frequencies, encouraging a larger margin between rare and common classes. ### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-approaches'></a>Contrastive Learning Approaches: 7 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for representation learning, particularly in the context of long-tailed recognition tasks, where class imbalance significantly affects model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. While supervised contrastive learning (SCL) has shown promise in balanced settings, its effectiveness is limited in long-tailed distributions due to issues such as poor uniformity in feature space and gradient conflicts between positive and negative pairs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. This has led to the development of specialized variants like Balanced Contrastive Learning (BCL), which introduces class-averaging and class-complement strategies to improve gradient contribution balance and ensure uniform feature distribution across all classes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. Similarly, Targeted Supervised Contrastive Learning (TSC) addresses the poor separability of minority classes by generating uniformly distributed targets on a hypersphere and aligning features to these targets during training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13998)\]. These approaches aim to enhance the generalization of models by ensuring that all classes, including underrepresented ones, are effectively captured in the feature space. Additionally, methods like SimCLR have demonstrated the importance of data augmentation and normalized embeddings in contrastive learning, achieving state-of-the-art results on ImageNet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.05160)\]. The Momentum Contrast (MoCo) framework further advances unsupervised contrastive learning by introducing a dynamic dictionary and moving-averaged encoder, enabling consistent representation learning without requiring a memory bank \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. These contributions highlight the ongoing efforts to refine contrastive learning for both balanced and imbalanced data scenarios, with a focus on improving feature uniformity, gradient consistency, and model generalization \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14267)\].

### <a id='category-class-balancing-strategies'></a>Class-Balancing Strategies: 10 papers found.
The long-tailed recognition problem, where data is unevenly distributed across classes, has been extensively studied, with various approaches focusing on class-balancing strategies to improve model performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. Existing methods often tackle the issue by re-sampling data, re-weighting classes, or modifying the loss function to reduce bias toward common (head) classes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01809)\]. For instance, weight balancing techniques such as L2-normalization, weight decay, and MaxNorm have been explored to ensure that classifier weights are balanced across classes, leading to improved accuracy in long-tailed scenarios \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14197)\]. Similarly, the RIDE framework introduces a distribution-aware diversity loss and an expert routing module to enhance performance by dynamically assigning ambiguous instances to specialized classifiers \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01809)\]. Another approach, the dynamic meta-embedding method, integrates image and memory features to better handle imbalanced data by leveraging a learned metric that respects closed-world assumptions while acknowledging open-world novelty \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. Additionally, the Balanced Softmax and Balanced Meta-Softmax methods address the label distribution shift between training and testing by introducing unbiased extensions of the Softmax function \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10740)\]. Theoretical frameworks such as causal inference have also been applied to disentangle the effects of momentum in long-tailed classification, offering new insights into how to mitigate bias toward head classes \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.12991)\]. These diverse strategies highlight the ongoing efforts to address long-tailed recognition by balancing data distribution, loss functions, and model architecture \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05555)\].
