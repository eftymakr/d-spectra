## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advancements focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as ARIMA and exponential smoothing have been widely used, but they often struggle with capturing complex temporal dependencies and non-stationarity \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7b74990bff941aece41437ab9aa9c1f48a3fafca)\]. More recently, deep learning models have emerged as a powerful alternative, offering greater flexibility and performance in forecasting tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13278)\].

Several studies have explored the use of Transformers for time series forecasting, leveraging their ability to model long-range dependencies. However, Transformers face challenges in handling non-stationary data and large lookback windows, leading to performance degradation and computational inefficiency \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various modifications, such as the PatchTST model, which introduces a patching design to retain local semantic information and reduce computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, the Autoformer model incorporates an Auto-Correlation mechanism to improve efficiency and accuracy in long-term forecasting \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of Transformers when properly adapted for time series tasks.

In addition to Transformers, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and their variants, such as LSTMs and GRUs, have been extensively studied for time series analysis \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. The LSTNet framework, for example, combines CNNs and RNNs to capture both short-term and long-term patterns, achieving significant improvements over traditional methods \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. However, these models often require careful tuning and may not generalize well to diverse forecasting tasks without additional adaptations.

Probabilistic forecasting has also gained traction, with models like DeepAR providing accurate forecasts by estimating the probability distribution of future values \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. This approach is particularly useful in financial applications where uncertainty quantification is essential. Similarly, the Multi-Horizon Quantile Recurrent Forecaster (MQRF) extends probabilistic forecasting to multi-step predictions, accommodating both static and dynamic covariates \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.11053)\]. These models highlight the importance of incorporating uncertainty into forecasting frameworks, especially in high-stakes domains like finance.

Recent work has also explored the use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting, aiming to leverage their strong pattern recognition and reasoning capabilities \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. However, studies have shown that LLMs may not always provide significant advantages over traditional models, particularly when pre-trained without specific time series knowledge \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. The Chronos framework, which pretrains language models on time series data, has shown promising results in improving zero-shot forecasting performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07815)\]. ### <a id='category-transformer-based-time-series-forecasting'></a>Transformer-Based Time Series Forecasting: 6 papers found.
Recent advancements in Transformer-based time series forecasting have focused on improving long-term prediction accuracy and handling complex temporal dependencies. Models like PatchTST \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\] introduce a patching design that segments time series into subseries-level patches, enabling efficient computation and better long-term forecasting by retaining local semantic information. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\] combines recurrent layers with interpretable self-attention mechanisms to handle multi-horizon forecasting, offering both high performance and interpretability. The Non-stationary Transformers framework \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\] addresses the issue of over-stationarization by incorporating modules that preserve non-stationary characteristics of real-world data, leading to significant improvements in forecasting accuracy. The iTransformer \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\] restructures the Transformer architecture to better handle multivariate time series by applying attention and feed-forward networks on inverted dimensions, enhancing performance across different lookback windows. Additionally, the Autoformer \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\] introduces an Auto-Correlation mechanism that improves efficiency and accuracy in long-term forecasting by leveraging periodicity and sub-series level dependencies. These models collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance Transformer-based approaches for time series forecasting, addressing challenges such as non-stationarity, computational efficiency, and interpretability.

### <a id='category-deep-learning-approaches-for-time-series'></a>Deep Learning Approaches for Time Series: 8 papers found.
Time series forecasting has seen significant advancements through deep learning, with various architectures addressing the challenges of temporal variation and uncertainty. Traditional methods often struggle with capturing complex patterns and providing probabilistic outputs, whereas deep learning models offer more flexibility and accuracy. For instance, DeepAR leverages an autoregressive recurrent network to produce probabilistic forecasts, achieving notable improvements over classical approaches \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Similarly, recent models like TimesNet and TimeMixer have introduced innovative techniques to handle multi-periodicity and multiscale-mixing patterns, respectively, by transforming time series into 2D tensors or utilizing fully MLP-based architectures \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\]. These models not only enhance forecasting accuracy but also provide robustness against uncertainty, which is crucial for applications such as financial fund allocation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Furthermore, hybrid models that combine statistical methods with neural networks have been proposed to improve performance in both short- and long-term forecasting tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. The integration of foundation models and pre-trained architectures has also gained traction, enabling generalized knowledge transfer across diverse time series analysis tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. In the context of financial applications, models like LSTNet and STGCN have demonstrated effectiveness in capturing both short-term and long-term dependencies, as well as spatial-temporal correlations, which are essential for accurate predictions in dynamic environments \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. These developments highlight the evolving landscape of deep learning for time series, emphasizing the importance of adaptive and uncertainty-aware frameworks in real-world decision-making.

### <a id='category-large-language-models-in-time-series-forecasting'></a>Large Language Models in Time Series Forecasting: 5 papers found.
The category "Large Language Models in Time Series Forecasting" explores the application of large language models (LLMs) to time series forecasting, highlighting both their potential and limitations. Several studies demonstrate that LLMs can effectively integrate multi-modal signals, such as financial news and price data, to improve forecasting accuracy and explainability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. For instance, one study shows that LLMs like GPT-4 and Open LLaMA can reason over textual and numerical data, leveraging cross-sequence information to generate explainable forecasts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. However, other research challenges the effectiveness of LLMs, indicating that removing the LLM component or replacing it with a basic attention layer does not degrade forecasting performance, and in some cases, improves it \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. Additionally, pretrained LLMs often do not outperform models trained from scratch, and they struggle with capturing sequential dependencies in time series data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. To address these challenges, frameworks like Chronos and Time-LLM have been developed. Chronos tokenizes time series data and trains transformer-based models on this data, achieving strong performance on both seen and unseen datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Similarly, Time-LLM reprograms LLMs for time series forecasting by aligning modalities through text prototypes and prompt-based techniques, leading to superior performance in few-shot and zero-shot settings \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\]. These studies collectively suggest that while LLMs offer promising capabilities, their integration into time series forecasting requires careful design and adaptation to overcome existing limitations.

### <a id='category-traditional-time-series-forecasting-models'></a>Traditional Time Series Forecasting Models: 1 papers found.

Traditional time series forecasting models, such as exponential smoothing and ARIMA, have been widely used for automatic forecasting in various domains, including business and finance \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7b74990bff941aece41437ab9aa9c1f48a3fafca)\]. These models are particularly useful for handling both seasonal and non-seasonal data, and they form the basis for many forecasting algorithms implemented in tools like the forecast package for R \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7b74990bff941aece41437ab9aa9c1f48a3fafca)\]. However, recent advancements in financial applications, such as the Risk-aware Time-Series Predict-and-Allocate (RTS-PnO) framework, have introduced new approaches that address limitations in traditional methods, such as goal mismatch and forecasting uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. The RTS-PnO framework integrates end-to-end training with objective alignment, adaptive uncertainty calibration, and model-agnostic design, offering improvements over conventional prediction-only or predict-then-optimize approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. While traditional models remain foundational, modern frameworks like RTS-PnO demonstrate the potential for more robust and flexible solutions in complex financial scenarios.
