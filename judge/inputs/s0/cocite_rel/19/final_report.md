## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, with numerous approaches aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match input audio. Early methods often relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, but recent advances have shifted toward data-driven deep learning models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. These models typically encode audio signals into latent representations and map them to facial animations, often using regression or sequence-to-sequence architectures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. However, these approaches face challenges in capturing the fine-grained dynamics of facial movements, particularly in cases where phonetically similar syllables produce distinct lip shapes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\].

Several works have attempted to improve the realism and accuracy of speech-driven facial animation by incorporating more sophisticated modeling techniques. For instance, FaceFormer introduces a Transformer-based autoregressive model that encodes long-term audio context and autoregressively predicts a sequence of animated 3D face meshes \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. This approach addresses the limitations of prior methods that focus on short audio windows and limited context \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\]. Similarly, MeshTalk proposes a method that disentangles audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information using a cross-modality loss, enabling more natural and expressive facial animations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. These methods highlight the importance of modeling both the content and emotional aspects of speech to generate realistic facial expressions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

The issue of phonetically similar syllables leading to averaging effects in lip motion generation has been identified as a critical challenge. This problem arises because self-supervised audio models often fail to distinguish between phonetically similar syllables, resulting in averaged lip shapes that do not accurately reflect the intended speech \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. To address this, the proposed Wav2Sem module introduces a semantic decorrelation mechanism that extracts semantic features from the entire audio sequence, thereby improving the discriminability of audio encodings in the feature space \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\]. This approach is inspired by earlier works that emphasized the importance of semantic information in speech-driven animation, such as the use of phoneme-level features and cross-modal alignment \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\].

Other related works, such as EmoTalk, focus on disentangling emotional and content-related information in speech to generate more expressive facial animations \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. Similarly, EAMM introduces an emotion-aware motion model that enables one-shot emotional talking face generation by leveraging an emotion source video \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00023)\]. These methods demonstrate the growing interest in incorporating emotional and contextual information into speech-driven facial animation, aligning with the goals of the proposed Wav2Sem module \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\].

Speech-driven facial animation techniques have seen significant advancements, with a focus on improving lip synchronization, facial expression realism, and generalizability across different speakers and languages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Many prior works rely on phoneme-level features extracted from short audio windows, which can lead to inaccurate or overly smoothed lip movements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed Transformer-based models like Face-Former, which encode long-term audio context and use biased attention mechanisms to better align audio and motion modalities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Additionally, methods such as meshtalk introduce categorical latent spaces that disentangle audio-correlated and uncorrelated facial information, enabling more natural and expressive animations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. Another approach, VOCA, leverages a 4D face dataset to train a model that factors identity from facial motion, allowing realistic animation of unseen subjects without retargeting \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\]. Similarly, SelfTalk integrates self-supervision in a cross-modal network system, enabling the learning of 3D talking faces by exchanging features across audio, text, and lip shape \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. These methods highlight the importance of addressing the ill-posed nature of audio-visual mapping and the scarcity of 4D data, which are critical challenges in achieving realistic and scalable speech-driven facial animation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\].

### <a id='category-emotion-aware-facial-animation-models'></a>Emotion-Aware Facial Animation Models: 7 papers found.
In the realm of emotion-aware facial animation models, recent research has focused on disentangling emotional and content-related aspects of speech to generate more expressive 3D facial animations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. Techniques such as the Emotion Disentangling Encoder (EDE) and Cross-Reconstructed Emotion Disentanglement have been introduced to separate emotional and content features, enabling more natural and controllable facial expressions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. These methods often rely on advanced neural network architectures to decode and synthesize facial movements, leveraging both audio and visual data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Additionally, the development of large-scale datasets, such as 3D-ETF and 3D-TalkEmo, has been crucial in training models to handle the complexity of emotional facial expressions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\]. Furthermore, models like EMOTE incorporate decoupled losses for speech and emotion, allowing for better lip-sync and explicit emotional control \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. The use of implicit emotion displacement learners and geometry maps has also contributed to more realistic and emotionally expressive 3D facial animations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\]. Overall, these approaches highlight the growing emphasis on emotional realism and controllability in speech-driven facial animation systems.
