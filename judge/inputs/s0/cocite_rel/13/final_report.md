## Related Works

Long-tailed recognition remains a challenging problem in visual recognition due to the skewed class distribution in real-world datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Many existing methods have attempted to address this issue by either balancing the data, the loss, or the classifier \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. For instance, class-balancing strategies such as loss re-weighting, data re-sampling, or transfer learning from head to tail classes have been widely explored \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. However, these approaches often follow a joint learning scheme of representation and classification, which may not be optimal for long-tailed data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\].

Recent work has focused on contrastive learning as a promising approach to improve representation learning for long-tailed recognition. Supervised contrastive learning (SCL) has shown significant performance gains by pulling samples from the same class together and pushing apart samples from different classes in the embedding space \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11362)\]. However, SCL tends to bias toward high-frequency classes, increasing the difficulty of imbalanced learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. This bias is exacerbated by the long-tailed distribution of real-world data, where a few majority classes dominate the training process \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13998)\].

To address this issue, several methods have been proposed. Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) introduces class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process and adaptively enhance the intensity of pushing samples toward their corresponding centers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. Balanced Contrastive Learning (BCL) further improves upon SCL by introducing class-averaging and class-complement strategies to ensure a regular simplex formation in the embedding space \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. Targeted Supervised Contrastive Learning (TSC) improves the uniformity of the feature distribution on the hypersphere by generating targets uniformly distributed on a hypersphere and aligning features with these targets \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13998)\].

In addition to contrastive learning, other approaches have explored the use of class-balanced losses and weight balancing techniques. The Class-Balanced Loss based on the effective number of samples has been proposed to re-balance the loss by considering the impact of sample overlap \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05555)\]. ### <a id='category-long-tailed-recognition-challenges'></a>Long-tailed Recognition Challenges: 10 papers found.
The long-tailed recognition problem, where a few classes dominate the data distribution while most classes have few samples, poses significant challenges for deep learning models \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00321)\]. Existing approaches often focus on class re-balancing strategies such as re-weighting or re-sampling, which can improve recognition accuracy but may degrade the representational power of learned features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed integrated frameworks that simultaneously handle imbalanced classification, few-shot learning, and open-set recognition \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Additionally, the Self-supervised Aggregation of Diverse Experts (SADE) framework addresses test-agnostic long-tailed recognition by training diverse experts from a long-tailed dataset and aggregating them during inference \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09249)\]. These approaches highlight the need for unified solutions that address the full spectrum of long-tailed recognition challenges, from class imbalance to open-set recognition \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\].

### <a id='category-weight-balancing-techniques'></a>Weight Balancing Techniques: 1 papers found.
The long-tailed recognition (LTR) problem has been extensively studied due to the skewed class distributions commonly found in real-world data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Traditional approaches often suffer from bias toward common classes, as models are trained with naive methods that prioritize accuracy on frequent classes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. To address this, researchers have explored various weight balancing techniques, including L2-normalization, weight decay, and MaxNorm \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. These methods aim to mitigate the imbalance by constraining or penalizing the magnitude of class-specific weights. L2-normalization enforces unit norm per-class weights, but this can hinder the learning process by imposing a hard constraint \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Weight decay, on the other hand, penalizes large weights more heavily, leading to more balanced weight distributions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. MaxNorm operates by restricting weights within a norm ball, preventing them from exceeding a specified radius \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. Empirical studies have shown that both weight decay and MaxNorm significantly improve LTR accuracy, with weight decay proving particularly effective despite being underexplored in this domain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. These findings have led to the development of two-stage training paradigms that combine feature learning with class-balanced loss functions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. The proposed methods achieve state-of-the-art results on multiple benchmarks, establishing a new baseline for LTR research \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\].
