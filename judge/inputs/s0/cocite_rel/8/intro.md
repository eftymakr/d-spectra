## Related Works

The growing deployment of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in how they reflect and amplify societal biases \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, often overlooking the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. This limitation has been highlighted in prior work, which shows that conventional metrics fail to capture the nuanced ways in which models may exhibit bias, especially when they display high confidence in incorrect predictions \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. For instance, Mistral-7B has been found to exhibit suboptimal fairness due to its high confidence in incorrect predictions, a detail that conventional metrics like Equalized Odds overlook \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

Several studies have explored fairness in machine learning, emphasizing the need for more comprehensive definitions and metrics that account for both accuracy and fairness \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. For example, the paper "Fairness through awareness" introduces a framework for fair classification that considers both utility and fairness constraints, ensuring that similar individuals are treated similarly \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This approach has been extended in later work, such as "Fairness in Deep Learning: A Computational Perspective," which reviews recent progress in tackling algorithmic fairness from a computational standpoint \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08843)\]. These works underscore the importance of integrating fairness considerations into the design and evaluation of machine learning systems.

The issue of fairness in LLMs has also been addressed in the context of bias evaluation and mitigation. The paper "Bias and Fairness in Large Language Models: A Survey" provides a comprehensive overview of existing methods for evaluating and mitigating bias in LLMs, highlighting the need for more nuanced metrics that capture the complex ways in which models can reflect societal biases \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. Similarly, "BBQ: A hand-built bias benchmark for question answering" introduces a dataset designed to evaluate how biases manifest in model outputs for applied tasks like question answering \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08193)\]. This dataset highlights how models often rely on stereotypes when the context is under-informative, leading to the reproduction of harmful biases \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08193)\].

To address these challenges, the paper "MEDFAIR: Benchmarking Fairness for Medical Imaging" introduces a framework for benchmarking fairness in medical imaging, emphasizing the importance of diverse datasets and robust evaluation criteria \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725)\]. This work aligns with the need for more diverse and representative datasets to evaluate modern LLMs effectively.
