## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area in the context of privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in response to the "right to be forgotten" (RTBF) legislation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\]. This concept allows users to request the removal of their data from a trained model, a process that is especially challenging in federated learning (FL) due to the decentralized nature of data and model training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. Several recent works have explored methods for achieving this goal, each with varying approaches and trade-offs.

Early efforts in federated unlearning focused on retraining the model from scratch after removing the target data \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\]. While effective, this method is computationally expensive and impractical for large-scale systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed alternative strategies, such as leveraging historical parameter updates to reconstruct the unlearned model without full retraining \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06edb1f6eadb6c141d720fcca4c854578dc5d670)\]. This approach significantly reduces the time required for unlearning while maintaining model efficacy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. For instance, FedEraser \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\] introduces a novel calibration method to speed up the reconstruction of the unlearned model using retained updates, achieving a 4× speed-up compared to retraining from scratch.

Another line of work focuses on improving the efficiency and accuracy of unlearning in federated settings. For example, the paper \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\] proposes a method that first performs local unlearning at the client and then uses the locally unlearned model as initialization for a few rounds of federated learning. This approach avoids the need for global access to training data or storage of historical updates, making it more scalable \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\]. Similarly, the work \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\] introduces a framework that enables the removal of a client's contribution without requiring additional storage, by performing gradient ascent in an orthogonal space formed by other clients' gradients \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a5f39bcd6198b26997b77233ff81a14e6bc8a32d)\].

In addition to efficiency, verifiability has become an important consideration in federated unlearning. The VeriFi framework \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/efab3b30d6f67f55c68f9dc7c65b2b54b1789030)\] introduces a system that allows users to verify the effectiveness of unlearning, ensuring that the model no longer retains the influence of the removed data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11794)\]. This is crucial for compliance with privacy regulations, as it provides a mechanism for independently confirming that unlearning has occurred \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.09441)\]. Furthermore, the paper \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/70954dd11d1463bc665f803d84b15a4d83805ec0)\] explores the use of differential privacy to ensure that the unlearning process does not introduce new privacy risks, making it suitable for sensitive applications \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12448)\].

### <a id='category-unlearning-methods-in-federated-learning'></a>Unlearning Methods in Federated Learning: 23 papers found.
The category "Unlearning Methods in Federated Learning" explores the challenges and solutions for enabling users to remove their data influence from federated learning (FL) models, driven by the "right to be forgotten" and privacy concerns. Existing methods, such as FedEraser \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\], aim to reconstruct unlearned models by leveraging historical parameter updates, reducing the need for full retraining \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\]. Similarly, FRU \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\] introduces a rollback mechanism inspired by database transactions, allowing efficient unlearning in federated recommendation systems. However, these approaches often require storing historical updates, which can be resource-intensive \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. To address this, methods like SFU \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11794)\] propose subspace-based techniques that eliminate client contributions without additional storage, while Knot \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/efab3b30d6f67f55c68f9dc7c65b2b54b1789030)\] introduces clustered aggregation to limit retraining scope during data erasure. Additionally, research such as \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a5f39bcd6198b26997b77233ff81a14e6bc8a32d)\] focuses on removing client contributions by subtracting historical updates and using knowledge distillation to maintain model performance. Incentive mechanisms \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\] are also introduced to retain valuable users, while \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12448)\] explores unlearning to remove backdoors, enhancing security. Despite these advancements, challenges remain, including information leakage risks \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06edb1f6eadb6c141d720fcca4c854578dc5d670)\], the need for efficient evaluation metrics \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.04977)\], and the complexity of unlearning in distributed settings \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11018)\]. These studies collectively highlight the evolving landscape of federated unlearning, emphasizing the balance between privacy, efficiency, and model performance.

[Snippet]: «Federated learning enables thousands of participants to construct a deep learning model without sharing their private training data with each other. For example, multiple smartphones can jointly train a next-word predictor for keyboards without revealing what individual users type. We demonstrate that any participant in federated learning can introduce hidden backdoor functionality into the joint global model, e.g., to ensure that an image classifier assigns an attacker-chosen label to images with certain features, or that a word predictor completes certain sentences with an attacker-chosen word. 
We design and evaluate a new model-poisoning methodology based on model replacement. An attacker selected in a single round of federated learning can cause the global model to immediately reach 100% accuracy on the backdoor task. We evaluate the attack under different assumptions for the standard federated-learning tasks and show that it greatly outperforms data poisoning. Our generic constrain-and-scale technique also evades anomaly detection-based defenses by incorporating the evasion into the attacker's loss function during training.»
