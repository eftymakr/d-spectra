## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommender systems, evolving from basic matrix factorization techniques to more sophisticated deep learning approaches \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\]. Early methods such as matrix factorization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] and later neural network-based models like Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] have laid the foundation for modern recommendation systems. NCF introduces a multi-layer perceptron to model the user-item interaction function, enabling non-linear modeling of collaborative signals \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. However, these methods often rely on pre-defined features and do not explicitly encode the collaborative signal from user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Recent advances in graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly impacted CF. LightGCN \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] simplifies the design of GCNs by focusing on neighborhood aggregation, leading to a linear and efficient model that outperforms NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] by about 16.0% on average. Similarly, UltraGCN \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\] further simplifies GCNs by removing infinite layers of message passing, achieving a 10x speedup over LightGCN while maintaining performance. These models emphasize the importance of efficient and scalable graph-based approaches for recommendation.

Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have also been applied to large-scale recommendation systems, as demonstrated by the work at Pinterest \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. This approach leverages efficient random walks and graph convolutions to generate embeddings that incorporate both graph structure and node features, enabling high-quality recommendations on massive graphs. Another notable contribution is the Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\], which focuses on disentangling user intents by modeling distribution over intents for each user-item interaction. This approach iteratively refines intent-aware interaction graphs and representations, leading to more interpretable and effective recommendations. Additionally, researchers have explored the use of GNNs in social recommendation systems, where they can jointly model user-user social graphs and user-item interaction graphs to capture both interactions and opinions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. Furthermore, large-scale applications of GNNs, such as at Pinterest, have demonstrated their potential for handling massive graphs with billions of nodes and edges, leveraging efficient random walks and training strategies to generate high-quality recommendations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of GNNs in recommendation systems, focusing on improving model efficiency, capturing complex collaborative signals, and scaling to real-world applications.

### <a id='category-spectral-methods-for-graph-based-recommendations'></a>Spectral Methods for Graph-Based Recommendations: 4 papers found.
Graph-based recommendation methods have seen significant advancements through spectral approaches, which leverage the structural properties of user-item interaction graphs to enhance recommendation accuracy. Spectral methods operate in the spectral domain, enabling the extraction of both local and global patterns by considering the graph's connectivity and proximity information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This approach is particularly useful in addressing challenges such as the cold-start problem, where traditional collaborative filtering (CF) methods struggle due to insufficient user or item data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Recent work has focused on developing spectral convolution operations that directly process signals on graphs, allowing for more nuanced modeling of user-item relationships \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. For instance, SpectralCF introduces a novel spectral convolution operation that captures both the proximity and connectivity information within the graph, leading to improved performance over existing CF-based methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, PolyCF reframes the CF task as a graph-signal processing problem, using polynomial graph filters to capture spectral features across multiple eigenspaces and recover missing interactions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. These methods highlight the potential of spectral techniques in enhancing the expressiveness and effectiveness of graph-based recommendation systems. Additionally, research on graph contrastive learning (GCL) has explored the connection between graph augmentation strategies and spectral properties, revealing that effective augmentations should emphasize differences in high-frequency components \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. These findings underscore the importance of spectral analysis in improving the robustness and generalization of graph-based recommendation models.

[Snippet]: «We present graph attention networks (GATs), novel neural network architectures that operate on graph-structured data, leveraging masked self-attentional layers to address the shortcomings of prior methods based on graph convolutions or their approximations. By stacking layers in which nodes are able to attend over their neighborhoods' features, we enable (implicitly) specifying different weights to different nodes in a neighborhood, without requiring any kind of costly matrix operation (such as inversion) or depending on knowing the graph structure upfront. In this way, we address several key challenges of spectral-based graph neural networks simultaneously, and make our model readily applicable to inductive as well as transductive problems. Our GAT models have achieved or matched state-of-the-art results across four established transductive and inductive graph benchmarks: the Cora, Citeseer and Pubmed citation network datasets, as well as a protein-protein interaction dataset (wherein test graphs remain unseen during training).»
