## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as the de facto standard for achieving privacy in data analysis \[[20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4ce10063cd25447dcde75c2d9ce327446ced952)\]. However, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, particularly in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. For instance, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. This issue has been explored in several works that examine the vulnerabilities of DP in distributed environments. 

In the local differential privacy (LDP) model, where users perturb their data before sending it to a data collector, researchers have identified vulnerabilities to manipulation attacks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These attacks can significantly distort the results of the data collector by allowing a small fraction of users to obscure the true distribution of the data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Similarly, in the central model, where a trusted server collects and processes data, the risk of unfaithful execution by the server remains a critical concern \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. 

To address these issues, several works have proposed verifiable mechanisms that ensure the correctness of DP protocols. For example, the work by \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\] introduces interactive proofs for differential privacy, which require the publishing entity to output a zero-knowledge proof that convinces a verifier that the output is both differentially private and reliable. This approach ensures that the randomness used in DP is faithfully generated, preventing malicious manipulation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. 

Another line of research focuses on verifiable randomization mechanisms that prevent malicious data providers from compromising the LDP protocol \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These mechanisms allow the data collector to verify the completeness of the randomization process for each user, thereby ensuring that the output is not manipulated \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where users may attempt to skew the results by altering their responses \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\].

In addition, the shuffled model of differential privacy has been proposed as a middle ground between the local and central models, offering both high accuracy and reduced trust assumptions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. This model allows for the random permutation of user reports, which enhances privacy while maintaining utility \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. The shuffled model has been further explored in the context of distributed differential privacy, where it provides the benefits of the central model without requiring a trusted server \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\].

Furthermore, the work by \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1407.6981)\] introduces RAPPOR, a randomized aggregatable privacy-preserving ordinal response mechanism that enables the collection of statistics while preserving individual privacy. RAPPOR uses randomized response to ensure that individual data points cannot be linked to specific users, making it a robust solution for privacy-preserving data collection \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1407.6981)\].

Local Differential Privacy (LDP) protocols, which enable privacy-preserving data analytics by perturbing user data before collection, have been extensively studied for improving utility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. However, their security against adversarial manipulation remains a critical concern. Research has shown that LDP protocols are highly vulnerable to data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users to manipulate frequency estimates or heavy hitter identifications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These attacks exploit the fact that noninteractive LDP protocols can be manipulated to obscure the true data distribution, especially when privacy levels are high or domain sizes are large \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, malicious data providers can manipulate their outputs to skew estimations, as the data collector relies on already randomized data to infer background distributions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. While some countermeasures, such as verifiable randomization mechanisms, have been proposed to mitigate these vulnerabilities, they may not be universally effective \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These findings highlight the need for more robust defenses against adversarial manipulation in LDP protocols.

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-of-differential-privacy'></a>Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy: 4 papers found.
The shuffled model of differential privacy offers a middle ground between the local and central models, aiming to balance privacy guarantees with computational efficiency and accuracy. Unlike the local model, where each user individually randomizes their data before sending it to a central server, the shuffled model introduces an anonymous channel that permutes user messages before aggregation, thereby reducing the need for full trust in the server while maintaining higher accuracy than the local model \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model has been shown to provide the power of the central model without requiring a fully trusted server, as it allows for the collection and analysis of data in a way that preserves privacy through permutation-invariant mechanisms \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Additionally, recent work has demonstrated that the shuffled model can achieve error independent of domain size when estimating histograms, implying a significant gap in sample complexity compared to the local model \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. Furthermore, the shuffled model has been analyzed in terms of its privacy amplification properties, where certain algorithms satisfying local differential privacy can also achieve central differential privacy through general techniques, suggesting that existing LDP-based deployments may have lower privacy costs than previously thought \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These developments highlight the shuffled model's potential to improve both practicality and theoretical understanding of privacy-preserving computation in distributed settings.

However, LDP mechanisms often face challenges in repeated data collection scenarios, where privacy guarantees degrade over time \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. To address this, recent work has focused on developing mechanisms that maintain strong privacy guarantees even with long-term, repeated data collection \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. For example, new LDP mechanisms have been proposed for repeated collection of counter data, offering comparable accuracy to single-round mechanisms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Furthermore, the RAPPOR framework has been introduced to enable anonymous, high-utility data collection with strong privacy guarantees, particularly for categorical statistics \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. These methods are complemented by theoretical advancements in DP, including the development of order-optimal mechanisms like hashed K-ary Randomized Response (KRR) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\], which achieve competitive utility across different privacy regimes. The integration of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP) with DP has also been explored, demonstrating that while ZKPs can support DP under verifiability requirements, they are not strictly necessary \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d762c3b48d3949dc2cd803d955c3c983e7aa16ba)\].
