## Related Works

Recent research has explored the internal mechanisms of large language models (LLMs), particularly focusing on how they encode and express behaviors such as refusal and alignment. A key insight is that refusal behavior is mediated by a single direction in the model's activation space, which can be manipulated to alter model behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. This finding highlights the importance of understanding the internal structure of LLMs to develop effective methods for controlling their outputs.

Several studies have investigated methods for steering LLMs through activation space modifications. For instance, Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) introduces steering vectors by averaging activation differences between positive and negative examples of a behavior, enabling precise control over model outputs \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. This method is effective over and on top of traditional approaches like fine-tuning and system prompt design, and it minimally reduces model capabilities \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. Similarly, Inference-Time Intervention (ITI) shifts model activations during inference to improve truthfulness, achieving significant improvements on benchmarks like TruthfulQA \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\]. ITI is minimally invasive and data-efficient, requiring only a few hundred examples to locate truthful directions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\].

The ability of LLMs to reason has also been a focus of recent work. Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting has shown promise in eliciting complex reasoning, but recent studies have demonstrated that LLMs can also perform zero-shot reasoning by simply adding a "Let's think step by step" prompt \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. This approach significantly improves performance on arithmetic and symbolic reasoning tasks without requiring task-specific examples \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. Moreover, the ReAct framework synergizes reasoning and acting by interleaving reasoning traces with task-specific actions, improving interpretability and trustworthiness in decision-making tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\].

Multimodal reasoning capabilities have also been explored, with models like Video-LLaMA demonstrating the ability to understand and generate responses based on both visual and auditory content in videos \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\]. Similarly, X-LLM converts multimodal information into foreign languages and integrates it into a large language model, enabling multimodal chat capabilities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04160)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of LLMs to handle complex, real-world tasks by incorporating multiple modalities.

Adversarial attacks on aligned models have also been studied, with methods like adversarial suffixes being used to bypass safety mechanisms and generate objectionable content \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. These attacks are transferable across models and can be automatically generated using gradient-based search techniques \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. This underscores the challenges in aligning LLMs and the need for robust safety mechanisms.
