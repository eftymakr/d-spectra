## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling detectors to recognize objects based on user-defined vocabularies in natural language \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. This capability allows for greater flexibility compared to traditional object detection models, which are limited to predefined categories \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. However, the performance of these models can be hampered when the user-defined vocabulary is overly broad or mis-specified \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. To address this, researchers have explored various methods to improve the alignment between the detector and the vocabulary, often leveraging vision-language pre-training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\].

Several works have focused on open-vocabulary detection by using image-caption pairs to expand the vocabulary of detectors \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]. For instance, ViLD employs vision and language knowledge distillation to train a two-stage detector, achieving significant improvements on benchmarks like LVIS and COCO \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]. Similarly, Detic leverages image-level supervision to expand the vocabulary of detectors to tens of thousands of concepts, outperforming prior work on open-vocabulary and long-tail detection benchmarks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. This approach is particularly useful when dealing with large-scale datasets that are difficult to annotate \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\].

Another line of research focuses on using vision-language models to generate pseudo labels for object detection, especially when labeled data is scarce \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. For example, the method proposed in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\] generates pseudo labels by aligning word semantics in captions with visual features of object masks in images, enabling the detection of novel classes without mask annotations. This is a crucial advancement, as it reduces the reliance on expensive bounding-box annotations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\].

Additionally, methods like MDETR and Grounding DINO have demonstrated the effectiveness of integrating language supervision into object detection frameworks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12763)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05499)\]. MDETR uses a transformer-based architecture to reason jointly over text and image, achieving state-of-the-art results on various benchmarks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12763)\]. Grounding DINO, on the other hand, introduces language to a closed-set detector to enable open-set concept generalization, achieving strong performance on benchmarks like COCO and LVIS \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05499)\].

The work in \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\] further advances open-vocabulary detection by generating pseudo bounding-box labels from large-scale image-caption pairs, significantly improving performance on benchmarks like COCO and LVIS \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. This method demonstrates the potential of leveraging weak supervision to expand the vocabulary of detectors without requiring extensive manual annotation \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\].
