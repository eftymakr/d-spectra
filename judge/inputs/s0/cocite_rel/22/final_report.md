## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has attracted significant research interest due to its practical importance in real-world applications where labeled data in the target domain is scarce or unavailable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Traditional UDA methods typically assume that the category settings between the source and target domains are known, which is often not the case in real-world scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes (classes present in the target domain but not in the source) exist \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. To address this limitation, several works have explored methods that can handle unknown category settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].

One line of research focuses on improving the alignment between source and target domains by leveraging adversarial learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. For example, ADVENT \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] introduces an adversarial entropy minimization approach to reduce domain shift, achieving state-of-the-art results on synthetic-to-real benchmarks. Similarly, CyCADA \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\] proposes a cycle-consistent adversarial domain adaptation model that enforces cycle-consistency while leveraging a task loss, enabling domain adaptation without requiring aligned image pairs. These methods, however, still assume that the category sets are known \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\].

Another approach is to utilize self-training with pseudo-labels to improve the performance of UDA models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. For instance, Prototypical Pseudo Label Denoising \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\] introduces a method that uses representative prototypes to address noisy pseudo-labels and improve the alignment of target features. Similarly, BUDA \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\] defines a new problem called "Boundless Unsupervised Domain Adaptation," where the target domain may contain classes not present in the source domain. This work proposes a framework that combines domain adaptation and zero-shot learning to enable adaptation to unseen classes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\].

Recent methods have also focused on addressing class imbalance and improving the quality of pseudo-labels \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]. For example, DADA \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\] leverages depth information to improve the performance of UDA models by exploiting depth-aware features. Similarly, DAFormer \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13613)\] introduces a novel architecture based on Transformers and proposes training strategies to stabilize the training process and avoid overfitting to the source domain. These methods demonstrate the potential of leveraging additional information, such as depth or pre-trained models, to improve domain adaptation performance.

Furthermore, some works have explored curriculum learning and dual-level domain mixing to improve the adaptation process \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10349)\]. ### <a id='category-self-training-with-pseudo-labels'></a>Self-Training with Pseudo-Labels: 6 papers found.
Self-training with pseudo-labels has emerged as a key technique in domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, addressing the challenge of transferring knowledge from labeled source data to unlabeled target data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. This approach leverages pseudo-labels generated from the model's predictions on the target domain to further refine the model, although these labels can be noisy due to domain shifts \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. To mitigate this, various methods have been proposed, such as using representative prototypes to estimate the likelihood of pseudo-labels and aligning feature spaces for more compact representations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. Additionally, uncertainty-aware consistency regularization has been introduced to improve the reliability of pseudo-labels by incorporating latent uncertainty information during training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. These techniques aim to enhance the robustness of models in cross-domain scenarios, particularly when dealing with synthetic-to-real data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. Furthermore, methods like ClassMix have been developed to generate more effective data augmentations for semi-supervised semantic segmentation, improving performance through better utilization of unlabeled data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\]. The integration of self-training with pseudo-labels, combined with advanced regularization and feature alignment strategies, has led to significant improvements in domain adaptation tasks, as demonstrated in benchmarks such as GTA5 to Cityscapes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\].

### <a id='category-adversarial-learning-approaches'></a>Adversarial Learning Approaches: 11 papers found.
In the context of unsupervised domain adaptation for semantic segmentation, recent approaches focus on aligning feature distributions between source and target domains to improve generalization. Several methods employ adversarial learning to reduce domain shift, such as category-level adversarial networks that align local semantic features \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Others introduce separated semantic feature adaptation, using discriminators to independently adapt semantic features across domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. Multi-level adversarial networks are also proposed to enhance domain adaptation at different feature levels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\]. Additionally, entropy-based losses and adversarial losses are combined to achieve state-of-the-art performance in synthetic-to-real domain adaptation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. The use of cycle-consistent adversarial domain adaptation enables pixel-level and feature-level adaptation without requiring aligned image pairs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. Furthermore, methods like Contrastive Adaptation Networks explicitly model intra-class and inter-class domain discrepancies to improve generalization \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.08702)\]. A Fourier Transform-based approach simplifies domain alignment by swapping low-frequency spectra between domains \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\]. Finally, integrating GANs into FCN frameworks allows for structured domain adaptation by transforming synthetic features to real-like features \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the importance of adversarial learning, domain invariance, and structured adaptation in improving semantic segmentation performance across diverse domains.
