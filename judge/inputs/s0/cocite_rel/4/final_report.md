## Related Works

The problem of learning from noisy labels has been extensively studied in recent years, with a growing body of research focused on developing robust training strategies for deep neural networks. Early approaches primarily relied on modifying the loss function to mitigate the impact of label noise, such as using mean absolute error (MAE) as a noise-robust alternative to cross entropy (CE) \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07836)\]. However, MAE has been shown to perform poorly with deep neural networks and challenging datasets \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07836)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed a variety of loss functions that generalize both MAE and CE, offering improved robustness to label noise \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07836)\]. These loss functions are theoretically grounded and can be applied to any existing deep learning architecture \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07836)\].

Another line of research has focused on modifying the training process itself to better handle noisy labels. For example, Co-teaching \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06872)\] proposes a paradigm where two neural networks are trained simultaneously, with each network teaching the other by selecting data with potentially clean labels. This approach has been shown to significantly improve robustness against label noise, especially in cases where the noise ratio is high \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06872)\]. Similarly, Co-teaching+ \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04215)\] builds on this idea by introducing a strategy that leverages disagreement between models to improve generalization, further enhancing the robustness of the trained models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04215)\].

In addition to loss functions and training strategies, there has been growing interest in semi-supervised learning techniques for handling noisy labels. DivideMix \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\] introduces a framework that dynamically divides training data into labeled and unlabeled sets based on loss distribution, and trains the model in a semi-supervised manner. This approach helps to reduce confirmation bias and improve performance on noisy datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\]. Similarly, the work on CleanNet \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07131)\] explores transfer learning strategies that allow for the reduction of human supervision in label noise cleaning, demonstrating significant improvements in label noise detection and classification performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07131)\].

The problem of label noise has also been addressed from an information-theoretic perspective. [Snippet]: «Training accurate deep neural networks (DNNs) in the presence of noisy labels is an important and challenging task. Though a number of approaches have been proposed for learning with noisy labels, many open issues remain. In this paper, we show that DNN learning with Cross Entropy (CE) exhibits overfitting to noisy labels on some classes (``easy" classes), but more surprisingly, it also suffers from significant under learning on some other classes (``hard" classes). Intuitively, CE requires an extra term to facilitate learning of hard classes, and more importantly, this term should be noise tolerant, so as to avoid overfitting to noisy labels. Inspired by the symmetric KL-divergence, we propose the approach of Symmetric cross entropy Learning (SL), boosting CE symmetrically with a noise robust counterpart Reverse Cross Entropy (RCE). Our proposed SL approach simultaneously addresses both the under learning and overfitting problem of CE in the presence of noisy labels. We provide a theoretical analysis of SL and also empirically show, on a range of benchmark and real-world datasets, that SL outperforms state-of-the-art methods. We also show that SL can be easily incorporated into existing methods in order to further enhance their performance.»

### <a id='category-label-noise-mitigation-techniques'></a>Label Noise Mitigation Techniques: 11 papers found.
In the context of label noise mitigation techniques, several approaches have been proposed to improve the robustness of deep learning models when trained on noisy data. One such method is Co-teaching, which trains two neural networks simultaneously, allowing them to teach each other by selecting and backpropagating data with potentially clean labels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.06112)\]. Another approach involves loss correction techniques that account for class-dependent label noise, using matrix inversion and multiplication to adjust the loss function, while also estimating noise probabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06872)\]. Additionally, a framework has been introduced that leverages semi-supervised learning to dynamically divide training data into clean and noisy subsets, improving model performance through label co-refinement and co-guessing \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03683)\]. Furthermore, research has explored the use of noise-tolerant loss functions, such as mean absolute error, which enable robust risk minimization even in the presence of label noise \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\]. These methods collectively highlight the importance of addressing label noise through architectural modifications, loss function design, and semi-supervised learning strategies.

[Snippet]: «Despite their massive size, successful deep artificial neural networks can exhibit a remarkably small difference between training and test performance. Conventional wisdom attributes small generalization error either to properties of the model family, or to the regularization techniques used during training. 
Through extensive systematic experiments, we show how these traditional approaches fail to explain why large neural networks generalize well in practice. Specifically, our experiments establish that state-of-the-art convolutional networks for image classification trained with stochastic gradient methods easily fit a random labeling of the training data. This phenomenon is qualitatively unaffected by explicit regularization, and occurs even if we replace the true images by completely unstructured random noise. We corroborate these experimental findings with a theoretical construction showing that simple depth two neural networks already have perfect finite sample expressivity as soon as the number of parameters exceeds the number of data points as it usually does in practice. 
We interpret our experimental findings by comparison with traditional models.»
