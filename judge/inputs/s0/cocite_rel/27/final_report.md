## Related Works

Recent advancements in information retrieval have seen a shift from traditional sparse retrieval methods to dense retrieval frameworks that leverage deep learning models for improved accuracy. Early work on dense retrieval, such as BM25 and TF-IDF, relied on keyword matching and bag-of-words representations \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d69c0ed04ecc852e8c921900d3e7967f74f81263)\]. However, these methods are limited in their ability to capture semantic relationships between queries and documents \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. To address this, dense retrieval methods have emerged, where both queries and documents are encoded into dense vectors, and similarity is computed based on these representations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

The use of pre-trained language models, such as BERT, has significantly improved the performance of dense retrieval systems. These models are fine-tuned on specific tasks, enabling them to capture complex semantic relationships \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. ColBERT, for instance, introduces a late interaction architecture that independently encodes queries and documents and then computes their similarity through a cheap yet powerful interaction step, which allows for efficient retrieval \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. This approach enables pre-computation of document representations and offline indexing, which is crucial for large-scale retrieval tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\].

Another line of research focuses on differentiable search indices (DSIs), which encode the entire corpus into the parameters of a single model, allowing direct query-to-document retrieval without an explicit index \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. DSI models have shown strong generalization capabilities, outperforming BM25 in zero-shot settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. However, these models face challenges when the corpus evolves over time, as retraining is required to incorporate new documents \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. DSI++ addresses this by introducing a continual learning framework that allows incremental indexing of new documents while maintaining retrieval performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\].

In the context of table retrieval, several approaches have been proposed to improve semantic alignment between queries and tables. One such approach is the use of semantic similarity measures, where queries and tables are represented in multiple semantic spaces, and various similarity metrics are used to match them \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06159)\]. This method has shown significant improvements over traditional baselines \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06159)\]. Additionally, the use of query generation models to create synthetic queries for tables has been explored to enhance the interaction between queries and tables \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. This approach allows the model to learn more robust representations by considering a broader range of query-table interactions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

Furthermore, the use of multimodal representations has been explored for web table retrieval, where both textual and structural information are considered \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/373588873bef360e3ecad2e091ac17895eb863af)\]. ### <a id='category-differentiable-search-indices'></a>Differentiable Search Indices: 4 papers found.
Differentiable Search Indices (DSIs) represent a paradigm shift in information retrieval by integrating indexing and retrieval into a single model, typically a transformer-based architecture \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. This approach eliminates the need for separate indexing and retrieval stages, allowing models to directly map queries to document identifiers using only their parameters \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Early work on DSIs demonstrated their potential to outperform traditional methods, such as dual encoder models, by simplifying the retrieval pipeline and enabling more direct query-document interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. However, challenges remain, particularly in handling dynamic corpora and mitigating data distribution mismatches between indexing and retrieval phases \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. For instance, during indexing, DSI models often learn connections between long documents and their identifiers, while retrieval queries are typically shorter, leading to performance degradation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed enhancements such as DSI-QG, which generates relevant queries during indexing to better align with retrieval conditions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Additionally, continual learning challenges have emerged, as retraining models for incremental indexing is computationally expensive \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. Solutions like DSI++ have been introduced to enable continual indexing while minimizing forgetting of previously indexed documents, using techniques such as flatter minima optimization and generative memory \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. These advancements highlight the evolving landscape of DSIs, emphasizing their potential for improving retrieval accuracy and adaptability in real-world scenarios.
