## Related Works

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have emphasized the importance of understanding how feature representations evolve across layers, as this knowledge is critical for improving interpretability and robustness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)\]. Several studies have explored the dynamics of representation learning in LLMs, focusing on how fine-tuning affects model behavior. For example, Llama 2 introduces a collection of fine-tuned chat models optimized for dialogue use cases, demonstrating that fine-tuning can significantly enhance performance on specific tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)\]. Similarly, the work on instruction tuning shows that fine-tuning language models on a collection of tasks described via instructions can substantially improve zero-shot performance on unseen tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. This aligns with the broader trend of using fine-tuning to adapt pre-trained models to downstream applications.

The effectiveness of fine-tuning methods has also been extensively studied. Techniques such as Child-Tuning, which updates a subset of parameters during fine-tuning, have shown promising results, outperforming vanilla fine-tuning by a significant margin \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05687)\]. Additionally, methods like BitFit, which only modify the bias-terms of the model, have demonstrated that small-to-medium training data can be sufficient for achieving competitive performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10199)\]. These findings highlight the importance of parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies, especially when dealing with large-scale models where full fine-tuning is computationally prohibitive.

Moreover, the role of fine-tuning in aligning models with human preferences has gained attention. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) introduces a new parameterization of the reward model in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), enabling the extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach simplifies the fine-tuning process and improves the alignment of models with human preferences, as evidenced by its performance on sentiment control and dialogue tasks. Similarly, the work on preference learning for LLMs emphasizes the importance of human feedback in shaping model behavior and improving alignment with user intent \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11191)\].

This suggests that the choice of fine-tuning method and data size is critical for achieving optimal performance.

### <a id='category-parameter-efficient-fine-tuning-methods'></a>Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning Methods: 8 papers found.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods have emerged as a critical area of research in adapting large pre-trained language models to downstream tasks with limited resources. These methods aim to reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining strong performance, addressing the computational and memory constraints of full fine-tuning. For instance, Child-Tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)\] introduces a technique where only a subset of parameters, referred to as the child network, is updated by masking gradients of the remaining parameters, leading to significant performance improvements over vanilla fine-tuning. Similarly, BitFit \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\] focuses on modifying only the bias terms of the model, demonstrating competitiveness with full fine-tuning, especially on small-to-medium datasets. LoRA \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)\] further advances this field by injecting low-rank decomposition matrices into each Transformer layer, drastically reducing trainable parameters while preserving model quality. Additionally, prefix-tuning \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.00190)\] proposes a lightweight alternative by optimizing a sequence of task-specific vectors, allowing the model to generate outputs based on these prefixes without modifying the original parameters. Adapter modules \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17193)\] also provide a compact solution by adding only a few trainable parameters per task, enabling efficient parameter sharing across multiple tasks. These methods collectively highlight the potential of parameter-efficient approaches in achieving effective fine-tuning with minimal computational overhead, offering insights into the underlying mechanisms of model adaptation and generalization.

### <a id='category-human-preference-alignment-in-llms'></a>Human Preference Alignment in LLMs: 4 papers found.
The category "Human Preference Alignment in LLMs" explores methods to align large language models with human preferences, a critical aspect of ensuring their safe and effective deployment. Research in this area highlights the challenges of steering LMs to produce desired outputs, often relying on reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, RLHF is complex and unstable, requiring the training of a reward model and subsequent fine-tuning of the LM using reinforcement learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. To address these issues, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has been introduced, offering a simpler and more stable alternative by enabling closed-form policy extraction through a classification loss \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Additionally, surveys have categorized alignment strategies into four components—model, data, feedback, and algorithm—providing a unified framework to understand and compare different approaches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05687)\]. These works emphasize the importance of human feedback in shaping LLM behavior, with methods varying in their data requirements and implementation complexity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05687)\]. Furthermore, reward learning has been applied to natural language tasks, demonstrating the potential of human judgment in defining reward signals for reinforcement learning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Overall, these studies underscore the need for efficient and reliable methods to align LMs with human preferences, while also highlighting the limitations and opportunities for future research in this area \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05687)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\].
