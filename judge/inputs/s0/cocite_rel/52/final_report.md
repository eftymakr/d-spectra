## Related Works

Dense retrievers have become a cornerstone in information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) to encode text into dense vector representations that can be compared via simple similarity operations such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. These models typically employ contrastive learning during fine-tuning to improve retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. However, recent research has questioned the relative contributions of pre-training versus fine-tuning in acquiring retrieval knowledge, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily gained during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may only adjust neuron activations rather than reorganize knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This claim was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative dense retriever \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\].

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of different pre-training and fine-tuning strategies for dense retrieval. For instance, BERT has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art results on various NLP tasks, including question answering and language inference, through its bidirectional training approach \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, BERT's performance can be further optimized through techniques such as RoBERTa, which introduces improvements in training data size and hyperparameter choices \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Additionally, models like Condenser and coCondenser have been proposed to address the limitations of standard language models by pre-training toward dense encoders \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. These models aim to better aggregate text information into dense representations, improving retrieval performance on tasks such as MS-MARCO and Natural Questions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\].

The role of fine-tuning in dense retrieval has also been scrutinized. For example, the use of contrastive learning during fine-tuning has been shown to significantly improve retrieval performance, particularly when combined with large-scale pre-training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. However, the extent to which fine-tuning can acquire new knowledge remains debated. Some studies suggest that fine-tuning may not be sufficient to acquire knowledge not already present in the pre-trained model \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. This is supported by findings that in models such as Contriever, which uses mean pooling, and LLaMA-based decoder-only models, the pattern of pre-training dominance over fine-tuning does not hold universally \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)\].

In addition to architectural choices, the effectiveness of dense retrievers is influenced by training strategies. For example, the use of hard negatives during training has been shown to improve retrieval performance by focusing on the most challenging examples \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15466)\]. ### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-in-retrieval'></a>Pre-trained Language Models in Retrieval: 4 papers found.
Recent advancements in pre-trained language models (PLMs) have significantly impacted the field of dense retrieval, with researchers exploring novel pre-training strategies to enhance retrieval performance. Prior work has focused on fine-tuning deep PLMs to generate dense vector representations for efficient text comparison and retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. However, dense encoders often require substantial data and sophisticated training techniques, which can be challenging in low-resource scenarios \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed specialized architectures such as Condenser, which pre-trains a novel Transformer structure tailored for dense encoding \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Additionally, the RetroMAE framework introduces an asymmetric MAE workflow, combining a full-scale BERT-like encoder with a one-layer decoder and varying masking ratios to improve retrieval performance \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\]. Another line of research, such as PROP, leverages the query likelihood model to construct a representative words prediction task, achieving state-of-the-art results on ad-hoc retrieval benchmarks \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10137)\]. Building on this, B-PROP further enhances retrieval by incorporating a contrastive method based on BERT’s self-attention mechanism to sample representative words from documents \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09791)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to refine pre-training strategies for dense retrieval, emphasizing the importance of both architectural innovations and task-specific objectives in improving retrieval effectiveness.

### <a id='category-training-strategies-for-retrieval-models'></a>Training Strategies for Retrieval Models: 14 papers found.
The category "Training Strategies for Retrieval Models" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the effectiveness and efficiency of dense retrieval systems. These strategies often involve pre-training models on large corpora to capture rich semantic representations, followed by fine-tuning for specific tasks such as open-domain question answering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Techniques like Retrieval-Augmented Language Model pre-training (REALM) integrate a latent knowledge retriever to allow models to access and attend over documents during inference, enhancing interpretability and modularity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Additionally, methods such as Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE) address the challenge of training dense retrievers by generating more realistic negative examples, thereby improving the alignment between training and testing data distributions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. The use of embedding-based retrieval models, particularly those leveraging Transformer architectures, has also gained traction, with pre-training tasks like Inverse Cloze Task (ICT), Body First Selection (BFS), and Wiki Link Prediction (WLP) significantly enhancing performance over traditional IR methods like BM-25 \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Furthermore, the development of optimized training approaches such as RocketQA, which incorporates cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives, and data augmentation, has led to substantial improvements in dense passage retrieval \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Contrastive learning has also been explored as a way to train unsupervised dense retrievers, showing strong performance across various retrieval settings and even enabling cross-lingual retrieval \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11942)\]. The integration of self-supervised objectives, such as those used in DeCLUTR, has further advanced the field by enabling the learning of high-quality sentence embeddings without the need for labeled data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Overall, these strategies highlight the importance of both pre-training and fine-tuning in dense retrieval, with a growing emphasis on efficient and scalable training mechanisms that can handle large-scale document corpora \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

### <a id='category-dense-passage-retrieval-(dpr)-framework'></a>Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) Framework: 5 papers found.
The Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework has been extensively studied for its ability to encode text into dense vectors for efficient retrieval. Recent work highlights the effectiveness of fine-tuned language models (LM) for dense retrieval, though training dense retrievers remains challenging due to their sensitivity to data noise and reliance on large batches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed architectures like Condenser, which condenses information through LM pre-training, and coCondenser, which introduces an unsupervised contrastive loss to improve passage embedding robustness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Additionally, the framework has been extended to jointly train retrievers and readers without relying on traditional IR systems, using tasks like the Inverse Cloze Task for pre-training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. This approach has shown significant improvements, outperforming BM25 by up to 19 points in exact match on certain datasets \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Furthermore, dense retrievers have been shown to outperform sparse models like BM25 and TF-IDF, achieving better performance with minimal fine-tuning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Pre-training on large-scale datasets, such as synthetically generated questions or Reddit conversations, has also been explored to enhance retrieval performance, demonstrating substantial improvements over supervised baselines \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Theoretical and empirical analyses suggest limitations in fixed-length encodings for long documents, leading to proposals of hybrid models combining the efficiency of dual encoders with the expressiveness of attentional architectures \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These advancements underscore the evolving landscape of DPR, emphasizing the importance of pre-training, fine-tuning strategies, and architectural innovations.
