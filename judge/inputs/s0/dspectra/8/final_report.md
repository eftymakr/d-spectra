The growing deployment of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in the context of biased or discriminatory outcomes \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. Traditional fairness metrics often rely on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, which fail to account for the implicit impact of model uncertainty, such as higher confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more nuanced fairness measures that incorporate uncertainty-aware evaluation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

Several studies have explored fairness in machine learning, particularly in areas such as hiring, lending, and predictive policing, where biased data can lead to discriminatory outcomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. For instance, the concept of counterfactual fairness has been introduced to ensure that decisions remain consistent across different demographic groups \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This approach is grounded in causal inference and aims to capture the intuition that a decision should be fair regardless of an individual's membership in a particular group \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. However, these methods often focus on binary classification tasks and may not generalize well to complex models like LLMs \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

The importance of fairness in algorithmic decision-making has also been highlighted in the context of recommendation systems, where biased data can lead to unfair predictions against minority groups \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\].
