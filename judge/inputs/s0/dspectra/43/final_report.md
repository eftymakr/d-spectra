## Related Works

Large language models (LLMs) have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in their ability to perform complex tasks through instruction tuning and fine-tuning. Instruction tuning, which involves training models on a collection of tasks described via natural language instructions, has been shown to improve zero-shot performance on unseen tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to a wide range of models, including PaLM and T5, leading to substantial improvements in benchmark performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416)\]. For example, FLAN, an instruction-tuned model, outperforms zero-shot GPT-3 on 20 out of 25 tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Similarly, instruction tuning has been extended to multimodal settings, where models like LLaVA are trained using generated instruction data to enhance visual and language understanding \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\].

The effectiveness of instruction tuning is further supported by studies that highlight the importance of diverse instruction datasets and the use of chain-of-thought (CoT) and program-of-thought (PoT) rationales in improving mathematical reasoning \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05653)\]. MAmmoTH, a series of models trained on a hybrid instruction dataset, demonstrates significant gains in mathematical reasoning tasks, achieving performance that surpasses existing open-source models and even competes with GPT-4 \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05653)\]. These findings underscore the importance of diverse and structured instruction data in enhancing model capabilities.

Efforts to scale LLMs have also been a focal point in recent research. The PaLM model, with 540 billion parameters, demonstrates the benefits of scaling in few-shot learning, achieving state-of-the-art results on various benchmarks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. Additionally, the training of large models is often constrained by memory and computational limitations, leading to the development of efficient training methods such as model parallelism \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053)\]. Megatron-LM, for instance, enables the training of models with up to 8.3 billion parameters using model parallelism, demonstrating the feasibility of training very large models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053)\].

In the context of inference, techniques such as "windowing" and "row-column bundling" have been introduced to optimize the use of limited memory, allowing models up to twice the size of available DRAM to be run efficiently \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11514)\]. These methods are crucial for deploying LLMs on devices with constrained resources.

### <a id='category-instruction-tuning-techniques'></a>Instruction Tuning Techniques: 5 papers found.
Instruction Tuning Techniques have gained significant attention for enhancing the performance and generalization of large language models (LLMs) across various tasks and setups. Instruction tuning involves fine-tuning models on datasets phrased as instructions, which has been shown to improve zero-shot and few-shot performance on unseen tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to a wide range of models, including PaLM, T5, and U-PaLM, with notable improvements in benchmarks such as MMLU, BBH, and TyDiQA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416)\]. The effectiveness of instruction tuning is further supported by studies demonstrating that increasing the number of tasks, model size, and the use of chain-of-thought (CoT) data can significantly enhance performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416)\]. Additionally, methods like Evol-Instruct have been proposed to generate complex instruction data automatically, reducing the reliance on manual creation and improving the quality of instruction datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. These techniques have also been extended to multimodal settings, where instruction-tuned models like LLaVA demonstrate impressive capabilities in handling visual and language tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. Furthermore, specialized instruction datasets such as MathInstruct have been developed to improve mathematical reasoning, combining chain-of-thought and program-of-thought rationales to achieve state-of-the-art results \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05653)\]. Overall, instruction tuning represents a versatile and effective strategy for improving LLMs across diverse domains and tasks.

### <a id='category-model-scaling-and-parameter-optimization'></a>Model Scaling and Parameter Optimization: 5 papers found.
Model scaling and parameter optimization have been central to advancing large language models (LLMs) in recent years. Researchers have demonstrated that increasing model size significantly improves performance across a wide range of natural language tasks, particularly in few-shot learning scenarios, where models can adapt to new tasks with minimal training data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. For instance, the Pathways Language Model PaLM, with 540 billion parameters, achieved state-of-the-art results on numerous benchmarks, outperforming both fine-tuned models and human performance on some tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. Similarly, training models with billions of parameters, such as the 8.3 billion parameter transformer model, has led to significant improvements in tasks like language understanding and generation, with notable gains in perplexity and accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053)\]. These findings highlight the importance of scaling in enhancing model capabilities, although challenges remain, particularly in mathematical reasoning, where even large models struggle to achieve high accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03874)\]. Additionally, the development of open-access models like BLOOM, with 176 billion parameters, underscores the growing trend of democratizing access to large language models through collaborative efforts \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05100)\]. The ability to train and optimize such models efficiently is further supported by advances in distributed training systems, enabling the use of thousands of GPUs and TPUs to scale models effectively \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053)\]. These efforts collectively emphasize the critical role of model scaling in pushing the boundaries of natural language processing.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Large language models (LLMs) are central to modern natural language processing, delivering exceptional performance in various tasks. However, their substantial computational and memory requirements present challenges, especially for devices with limited DRAM capacity. This paper tackles the challenge of efficiently running LLMs that exceed the available DRAM capacity by storing the model parameters in flash memory, but bringing them on demand to DRAM. Our method involves constructing an inference cost model that takes into account the characteristics of flash memory, guiding us to optimize in two critical areas: reducing the volume of data transferred from flash and reading data in larger, more contiguous chunks. Within this hardware-informed framework, we introduce two principal techniques. First,"windowing"strategically reduces data transfer by reusing previously activated neurons, and second,"row-column bundling", tailored to the sequential data access strengths of flash memory, increases the size of data chunks read from flash memory. These methods collectively enable running models up to twice the size of the available DRAM, with a 4-5x and 20-25x increase in inference speed compared to naive loading approaches in CPU and GPU, respectively. Our integration of sparsity awareness, context-adaptive loading, and a hardware-oriented design paves the way for effective inference of LLMs on devices with limited memory.»

### <a id='category-benchmarking-and-evaluation-frameworks'></a>Benchmarking and Evaluation Frameworks: 3 papers found.
The category "Benchmarking and Evaluation Frameworks" focuses on the development and use of standardized benchmarks to assess the performance and capabilities of natural language understanding (NLU) models and large language models (LLMs). These frameworks aim to evaluate models across diverse tasks and domains, ensuring robustness and generalization. For instance, the General Language Understanding Evaluation (GLUE) benchmark provides a suite of nine NLU tasks, along with auxiliary datasets for probing specific linguistic phenomena, to assess models' ability to understand language beyond superficial correspondences between inputs and outputs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07461)\]. Similarly, the HumanEval benchmark evaluates code-writing capabilities of models like Codex, demonstrating that fine-tuned models can solve a significant percentage of programming problems, although challenges remain with complex docstrings and long chains of operations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)\]. Additionally, the use of LLMs as judges to evaluate chat assistants on open-ended questions is explored, with benchmarks like MT-bench and Chatbot Arena showing that strong models like GPT-4 can align well with human preferences, achieving high agreement levels \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)\]. These frameworks highlight the importance of evaluating models not only on task-specific performance but also on their ability to generalize across domains and handle real-world challenges, emphasizing the need for comprehensive and adaptable evaluation methodologies.
