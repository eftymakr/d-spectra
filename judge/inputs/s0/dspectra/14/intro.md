## Related Works

Video super-resolution (VSR) has been a focal point in computer vision, with recent advances leveraging deep learning to address the challenges of temporal consistency and artifact reduction in high-resolution video generation. Early methods, such as those based on single-image super-resolution (SR), often failed to capture temporal dependencies, leading to inconsistent results across frames \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1501.00092)\]. To address this, researchers introduced multi-frame SR approaches that incorporated motion estimation and compensation, such as optical flow-based methods \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.05250)\]. However, these approaches were often computationally intensive and prone to errors due to inaccurate motion estimation \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/755227d7ea41b1d90df31f411f9ecbc998c20d26)\].

More recently, deep learning has enabled significant improvements in VSR. The introduction of deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for SR, such as the Enhanced Deep Residual Networks (EDSR) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02921)\], demonstrated that residual learning could enhance performance by allowing deeper networks to train more effectively. These models were later extended to video restoration tasks, where temporal information from multiple frames was exploited using sliding window strategies or recurrent architectures \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12288)\]. However, these methods often suffered from high computational costs and limited temporal coherence \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a2c50f93f6d0892918e0ff653750d198ccab48d0)\].

To further improve temporal consistency, researchers proposed recurrent architectures, such as the Bidirectional Recurrent Convolutional Network (BRCN) \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a2c50f93f6d0892918e0ff653750d198ccab48d0)\], which modeled long-term dependencies using convolutional operations. This approach reduced parameter counts and improved efficiency while maintaining performance. Similarly, the Frame-Recurrent Video Super-Resolution (FR-VSR) framework \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04590)\] used previously inferred high-resolution (HR) estimates to super-resolve subsequent frames, leading to more temporally consistent results. This method also reduced computational costs by warping only one image per step \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04590)\].

Another line of research focused on incorporating attention mechanisms into VSR models. The Video Super-Resolution Transformer (VSR-Transformer) \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06847)\] introduced spatial-temporal convolutional self-attention layers to exploit locality and feature alignment, improving performance on benchmark datasets. Similarly, the Temporal Group Attention (TGA) method \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10595)\] divided input sequences into groups based on frame rates and used attention modules to integrate complementary information, achieving favorable performance against state-of-the-art methods \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10595)\].

In addition to attention-based models, diffusion-based approaches have gained traction for their ability to generate high-quality, temporally consistent videos. The Stable Video Diffusion (SVD) model \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\] demonstrated that latent diffusion models could be adapted for video generation by introducing temporal layers and finetuning on high-quality datasets.
