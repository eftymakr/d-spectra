## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area to address the "right to be forgotten" in federated learning (FL) systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. This concept allows users to remove their data contributions from the global model while preserving the model's utility \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Early approaches to federated unlearning primarily relied on retraining the model from scratch, which is computationally expensive \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. To improve efficiency, several methods have been proposed that aim to remove a client's influence without full retraining \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\].

One notable approach is FedEraser, which leverages historical parameter updates to reconstruct the unlearned model \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. This method trades server storage for faster unlearning by calibrating retained updates \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. Similarly, the SISA training framework reduces the computational overhead associated with unlearning by strategically limiting the influence of a data point during training \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\]. These methods demonstrate that unlearning can be achieved more efficiently than full retraining \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f2f8f7a2ec1b2ede48cbcd189b376ab9fa0735ef)\].

The importance of verifiable unlearning has also been emphasized, particularly in ensuring that the unlearning effect can be independently verified \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\]. VeriFi is a framework that enables systematic analysis and quantification of unlearning effects through marking and checking mechanisms \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\]. This approach ensures that the unlearning process is transparent and verifiable \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]. Additionally, the concept of differential privacy has been integrated into unlearning algorithms to enhance privacy guarantees \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\].

In the context of federated online learning to rank (FOLTR), unlearning capabilities are essential to comply with privacy regulations such as the "right to be forgotten" \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. Existing unlearning methods often rely on single evaluation metrics, which may not provide a comprehensive assessment of unlearning effectiveness \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. To address this, recent studies have proposed multiple evaluation metrics to better capture the nuances of unlearning \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08096)\]. These metrics help in assessing both under-unlearning and over-unlearning scenarios, ensuring a balanced approach to data privacy and model performance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\].

Furthermore, the challenge of unlearning in federated settings has led to the development of novel techniques such as the use of influence functions and knowledge distillation \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06edb1f6eadb6c141d720fcca4c854578dc5d670)\]. These methods aim to remove a client's contribution while maintaining the model's accuracy \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]. Additionally, the use of backdoor attacks during training has been proposed as a way to evaluate the effectiveness of unlearning \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.03575)\].

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a critical framework for collaborative machine learning under privacy constraints, enabling multiple parties to jointly train models without sharing raw data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. Recent work has focused on improving FL by introducing techniques such as clustered FL (CFL), which leverages geometric properties of the loss surface to group clients with similar data distributions, enhancing model performance without requiring prior knowledge of the number of clusters \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. Additionally, federated transfer learning (FTL) has been proposed to address the challenge of knowledge sharing across organizations while preserving privacy, allowing target-domain parties to benefit from source-domain data without compromising user confidentiality \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03337)\]. Despite these advancements, privacy risks remain a concern, as model updates can inadvertently leak information about participants' training data, leading to membership inference attacks \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04049)\]. To mitigate these issues, researchers have explored privacy-preserving techniques such as homomorphic encryption and secret sharing, enabling secure multi-party learning in heterogeneous settings \[[28](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c17aa3da073a95de8510788099249c04839ffe1)\]. Furthermore, the development of federated learning systems has been driven by the need for scalable and efficient infrastructure, with recent surveys highlighting the importance of system design, communication efficiency, and privacy mechanisms in deploying FL at scale \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.09693)\]. These efforts underscore the growing interest in FL as a solution to privacy challenges in machine learning, while also highlighting the ongoing need for robust evaluation methods and secure implementation strategies.

### <a id='category-efficient-unlearning-techniques'></a>Efficient Unlearning Techniques: 4 papers found.
Efficient unlearning techniques have emerged as critical research directions to address the challenges posed by the "right to be forgotten" under data privacy regulations like GDPR \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]. This principle requires machine learning models to remove specific data points or classes without necessitating full retraining, which is computationally expensive \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. To meet this demand, researchers have proposed various methods, such as linear filtration \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\], SISA training \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\], and zero retrain forgetting (ZRF) \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08096)\], each aiming to reduce the overhead of unlearning while preserving model performance. Linear filtration offers a computationally efficient approach by sanitizing models through linear operations, while SISA training introduces a framework that strategically limits the influence of specific data points during training, improving unlearning speed for stateful algorithms like stochastic gradient descent \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. Additionally, ZRF provides a novel metric for evaluating unlearning effectiveness without relying on retrained models, enabling post-deployment analysis \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08096)\]. However, existing methods often overlook the impact of unlearning on fairness and other critical model properties \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. Recent work has begun addressing these limitations by proposing fair machine unlearning methods that maintain both unlearning accuracy and fairness performance \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. These advancements highlight the growing focus on developing efficient, reliable, and ethically sound unlearning strategies for real-world applications.

However, OLTR often requires the collection of sensitive user data, raising privacy concerns \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. To mitigate these issues, Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) integrates OLTR within a Federated Learning (FL) framework, enabling collaborative model training across decentralized data sources without sharing raw user data \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. While FOLTR enhances privacy, it faces challenges in ranking effectiveness, robustness to heterogeneous data distributions, security against attacks, and the ability to unlearn specific client interactions or data \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. These challenges highlight the need for further research into improving the performance and reliability of FOLTR systems, particularly in the context of emerging privacy regulations like the "right to be forgotten," which require machine learning systems to support user-driven data removal \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\].
