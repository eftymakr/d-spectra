## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to improve recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge transfer across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. However, existing methods often face challenges such as domain conflict and the negative transfer problem (NTP), where transferring information from poor-quality or conflicting domains degrades performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. This section reviews relevant works that address these issues and contribute to the field of MDR.

Early approaches in cross-domain recommendation (CDR) focused on transferring information from a rich domain to a sparse one, often using matrix factorization or simple neural networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/0902.3430)\]. More recently, deep learning-based methods have been proposed to better capture complex user-item interactions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. For example, CoNet introduces a deep transfer learning approach using neural networks, enabling dual knowledge transfer across domains through cross connections \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Similarly, DDTCDR leverages dual learning to iteratively transfer information between domains, improving recommendation accuracy \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. These methods, however, typically assume a fixed set of domains and do not dynamically select the most relevant ones for transfer \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\].

To address domain conflicts and improve generalization, several works have explored domain adaptation techniques. Domain Separation Networks (DSN) aim to learn representations that are both domain-invariant and domain-specific, enabling better adaptation to new domains \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/884f8a29a7979a1f05223f432d9a149ac4dcc6b4)\]. Similarly, MADAN proposes a multi-source adversarial domain aggregation network to align features across domains while preserving domain-specific characteristics \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. These methods are crucial for MDR, as they help mitigate the negative effects of domain mismatch and improve the robustness of recommendation models \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e776b35d50f07998e0c4fdca6bfe1f21c364fddf)\].

In the context of MDR, recent works have focused on disentangling domain-specific and domain-shared knowledge. For instance, EDDA introduces embedding disentangling and domain alignment techniques to enhance knowledge transfer between domains with limited overlap \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.03357)\]. HAMUR proposes a hyper-network to dynamically generate parameters for domain-specific adapters, improving flexibility and reducing interference between domains \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. These methods highlight the importance of modeling domain-specific and shared knowledge separately to avoid conflicts and improve performance \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\].

Furthermore, the problem of negative transfer has been extensively studied in cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR).
