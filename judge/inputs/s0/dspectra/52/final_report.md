## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a powerful alternative to traditional sparse retrieval methods, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to generate dense vector representations that can be compared via simple similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. The effectiveness of dense retrievers is closely tied to the pre-training phase, where models learn to encode semantic relationships between text segments \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Recent studies have questioned the role of fine-tuning in dense retrieval, arguing that much of the retrieval knowledge is acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning primarily adjusts neuron activations rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. This idea was initially explored using BERT-based encoders with the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. However, the generalizability of this claim has not been thoroughly evaluated across different architectures, representation strategies, and datasets.

Several works have explored the pre-training and fine-tuning dynamics in dense retrieval. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\] introduced the Condenser architecture, which pre-trains a Transformer model to condense information into dense vectors, improving retrieval performance over standard LM baselines. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\] proposed coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training. These approaches highlight the importance of pre-training in shaping the quality of dense representations \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\].

The role of fine-tuning in dense retrieval has also been investigated in the context of different backbone architectures. For instance, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\] compared the use of CLS tokens versus mean pooling in dense retrieval, showing that mean pooling can lead to better performance in certain settings. Additionally, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13602)\] explored the use of decoder-only models like LLaMA, demonstrating that decoder-based architectures can achieve competitive results in dense retrieval tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\]. These findings suggest that while pre-training is crucial, the choice of representation strategy and architecture can significantly impact the effectiveness of fine-tuning.

Moreover, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\] introduced the concept of contrastive learning for dense retrieval, showing that unsupervised contrastive methods can achieve strong performance without requiring labeled data. This aligns with the findings of \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\], which demonstrated that contrastive learning can improve the quality of dense representations by learning to distinguish between relevant and irrelevant documents. The use of contrastive learning has also been extended to multi-lingual settings, where it enables cross-lingual retrieval between different scripts \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-for-dense-retrieval'></a>Contrastive Learning for Dense Retrieval: 11 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a critical approach in dense retrieval, enabling models to learn effective text representations without requiring extensive labeled data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. This technique involves training models to distinguish between relevant and irrelevant documents by contrasting positive and negative examples, which helps in aligning query and document embeddings in the embedding space \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\]. Several studies have explored variations of contrastive learning to improve retrieval performance. For instance, coCondenser introduces an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, DANCE proposes a dual training objective inspired by query likelihood, leading to more tailored and uniform embeddings that enhance ranking performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]. The SimLM model employs a bottleneck architecture with a replaced language modeling objective to improve sample efficiency and reduce input distribution mismatch between pre-training and fine-tuning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\]. Additionally, ANCE constructs negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, aligning training data distribution with testing to improve retrieval accuracy \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of contrastive learning in overcoming limitations of traditional dense retrievers, such as fragility to training noise and dependency on large batches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Furthermore, contrastive learning has been combined with other strategies, such as pseudo-query generation and data augmentation, to enhance generalization across different retrieval tasks and languages \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.07452)\]. Overall, contrastive learning continues to drive advancements in dense retrieval by enabling more efficient and effective training paradigms \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07773)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

### <a id='category-impact-of-architecture-on-fine-tuning-effectiveness'></a>Impact of Architecture on Fine-tuning Effectiveness: 9 papers found.
The category "Impact of Architecture on Fine-tuning Effectiveness" highlights how the design of pre-trained language models significantly influences their performance when fine-tuned for downstream tasks. Several studies emphasize that standard Transformer architectures may not be optimal for dense retrieval, as their attention mechanisms are not inherently suited for aggregating text into compact representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed novel architectures like Condenser, which pre-trains models to condition on dense representations, leading to improved retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, the use of autoencoder-based models has been explored, where the encoder is trained to produce high-quality embeddings that can reconstruct input texts, though challenges arise from potential shortcuts in the decoder \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. The UniLM model further demonstrates the importance of architecture by integrating bidirectional and sequence-to-sequence tasks into a unified framework, achieving state-of-the-art results on multiple NLP benchmarks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. BERT, a bidirectional encoder, has also been foundational in this space, enabling fine-tuning for tasks like question answering with minimal architectural changes \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. However, the effectiveness of fine-tuning versus pre-training remains a topic of debate, with some studies suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and fine-tuning mainly adjusts activation patterns rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12035)\]. This insight has been extended to evaluate different representation methods, backbone architectures, and datasets, revealing that the relationship between pre-training and fine-tuning varies across models like BERT, LLaMA, and Contriever \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. These findings underscore the critical role of architectural design in determining how well models can leverage pre-trained knowledge during fine-tuning.
