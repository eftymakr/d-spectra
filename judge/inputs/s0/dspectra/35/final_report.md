## Related Works

Protein dynamics and conformational ensembles play a critical role in biological function, necessitating advanced computational methods to study and generate realistic protein structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Traditional approaches rely on molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which are computationally expensive and limited in throughput \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling, particularly denoising diffusion models, have enabled efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven approaches remains challenging, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\].

Several studies have explored energy-based models (EBMs) for protein conformation generation. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\] proposes an EBM that operates at atomic scale and is trained solely on crystallized protein data, achieving performance comparable to the Rosetta energy function. This model captures physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy and is evaluated on the rotamer recovery task \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\] introduces techniques to improve contrastive divergence training of EBMs, highlighting the importance of a neglected gradient term and demonstrating improved performance on benchmarks such as image generation and OOD detection \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\]. These efforts underscore the potential of EBMs in capturing physical properties of proteins.

Diffusion models have also been extensively applied to protein structure prediction. \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\] presents a diffusion-based generative model that generates protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state towards a stable folded structure. This approach mirrors the natural folding process and leverages the inherent shift and rotational invariance of the representation to avoid complex equivariant networks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\] proposes a latent diffusion model that reduces the complexity of protein modeling while flexibly capturing the distribution of natural protein structures in a condensed latent space. This model uses an equivariant protein autoencoder to embed proteins into a latent space and then applies an equivariant diffusion model to learn the distribution of the latent representations \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\].

Moreover, \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\] introduces EvoDiff, a diffusion framework that combines evolutionary-scale data with the conditioning capabilities of diffusion models for controllable protein generation in sequence space. ### <a id='category-protein-dynamics-and-conformational-ensembles'></a>Protein Dynamics and Conformational Ensembles: 5 papers found.
Protein dynamics and conformational ensembles are central to understanding biological functions, as proteins often operate through dynamic structural ensembles rather than static conformations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling have enabled efficient prediction and sampling of protein conformations, with methods such as AlphaFlow, ESMFlow, and P2DFlow leveraging flow-based approaches to capture conformational flexibility and higher-order ensemble observables \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]\[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. The concept of conformational selection, which posits that ligands select pre-existing protein conformations, has also gained traction, challenging the classical induced fit hypothesis and influencing drug design strategies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. ### <a id='category-energy-based-models-(ebms)-for-protein-conformation'></a>Energy-Based Models (EBMs) for Protein Conformation: 5 papers found.
Energy-based models (EBMs) have gained significant attention for their ability to model complex molecular systems, particularly in the context of protein conformation and molecular graph generation. These models operate by defining energy functions that capture the physical and chemical properties of molecular systems, allowing for the generation of realistic structures without explicit normalization \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03288)\]. Unlike traditional approaches that rely on physical principles and extensive tuning, recent EBMs are trained directly on experimental data, such as crystallized protein structures, enabling them to capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. The training of EBMs remains challenging due to the unknown normalizing constant, but advances in training methods, such as contrastive divergence and MCMC-free approaches like Score Matching and Noise Contrastive Estimation, have improved their feasibility and performance \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03288)\]. Furthermore, techniques like data augmentation, multi-scale processing, and reservoir sampling have been introduced to enhance model robustness and generation quality \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\]. In molecular graph generation, EBMs have been adapted to ensure permutation invariance, a critical property for generating chemically valid molecules, through permutation-invariant energy functions \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00546)\]. The Rosetta energy function, a state-of-the-art method, has also been widely used in protein structure prediction and design, demonstrating the importance of energy-based scoring in biological modeling \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b08a8e202e71cb4a9c10332dbff55f7448ebff2)\]. These developments highlight the growing role of EBMs in bridging data-driven approaches with physical supervision, offering promising avenues for applications in structural biology and drug discovery.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-protein-structure-generation'></a>Diffusion Models in Protein Structure Generation: 7 papers found.
Recent advances in diffusion models have significantly enhanced the ability to generate and predict protein structures, addressing the limitations of traditional methods such as molecular dynamics simulations, which are computationally expensive and suffer from rare event sampling issues \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. These models, including EigenFold \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\], ConfDiff \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\], and Genie \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12485)\], leverage diffusion processes to sample conformational ensembles and capture the flexibility essential for biological function. EigenFold, for instance, models proteins as harmonic oscillators, enabling a cascading-resolution generative process that improves model uncertainty estimation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Similarly, ConfDiff integrates physical prior knowledge through a force-guided network, ensuring generated conformations remain close to the equilibrium distribution \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. Genie, on the other hand, employs denoising diffusion probabilistic models and equivariant neural networks to generate diverse and designable protein backbones \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12485)\]. These models are further supported by other approaches such as the latent diffusion model proposed in \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\], which reduces modeling complexity while preserving the distribution of natural protein structures. Additionally, methods like the one described in \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\] mimic the natural folding process by generating protein backbones through denoising from an unfolded state, enhancing structural realism. The integration of physical supervision, as seen in the Energy-based Alignment (EBA) method, further improves the physical plausibility of generated structures by aligning generative models with feedback from physical models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These developments collectively highlight the growing role of diffusion models in advancing protein structure prediction and design, offering more efficient and accurate alternatives to conventional methods.

Recent advances in sequence-based protein generation frameworks have significantly enhanced the capabilities of deep learning in protein design and structure prediction. These frameworks, such as ProteinMPNN \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\], ProGen2 \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13517)\], and EvoDiff \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\], leverage large-scale training on diverse protein sequences to generate biologically plausible proteins with high accuracy. ProteinMPNN, for instance, demonstrates superior performance in sequence recovery and structural robustness compared to traditional physically based approaches like Rosetta, achieving a sequence recovery rate of 52.4% on native protein backbones \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\]. Similarly, ProGen2, with its 6.4B parameters, captures the distribution of evolutionary sequences and generates novel viable sequences without additional fine-tuning \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13517)\]. EvoDiff further expands the scope by integrating evolutionary data with diffusion models, enabling the generation of high-fidelity, diverse, and structurally plausible proteins, including those with disordered regions \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\]. These models also benefit from training on extensive datasets, such as over a billion proteins from genomic and immune repertoire databases, which enhances their ability to produce sequences with fine-grained control over functional and structural properties \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13517)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03497)\]. Additionally, models like the one described in \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/deee48c5e0ac0407a1e002905caaf2b174bdb0e6)\] combine unsupervised learning with multiple sequence alignments to improve parameter efficiency and performance in structure learning. These developments highlight the growing importance of large-scale data and advanced architectures in advancing protein design, offering new possibilities for applications in structural biology and drug discovery.

Recent advances in generative modeling have significantly impacted protein structure prediction, with denoising diffusion models emerging as a powerful tool for learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These models enable efficient and accurate prediction of protein conformations by iteratively denoising random noise into plausible structures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into data-driven approaches remains a challenge, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. To address this, Energy-based Alignment (EBA) was introduced, aligning generative models with feedback from physical models to balance conformational states based on energy differences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. This approach improves the physical plausibility of generated structures, enhancing model predictions for applications in structural biology and drug discovery \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. Additionally, generative models based on the flow-matching paradigm over $ \text(SE)(3) $, such as FoldFlow, have been developed to model protein backbones more accurately and efficiently \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\]. These models leverage Riemannian optimal transport and simulation-free training to achieve stable and fast training, offering advantages over diffusion-based approaches \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\]. Furthermore, the ability to map any invariant source distribution to any invariant target distribution over $ \text(SE)(3) $ highlights the flexibility and robustness of these models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. Experimental validations have demonstrated that FoldFlow can generate high-quality, diverse, and novel protein backbones up to 300 amino acids in length \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These developments underscore the growing importance of integrating physical principles with generative modeling to advance protein structure prediction \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\].
