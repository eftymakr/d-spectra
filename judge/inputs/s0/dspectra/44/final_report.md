## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods relied on matrix factorization to learn low-dimensional embeddings, but recent advances have shifted toward deep learning-based approaches that better capture complex interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Among these, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling user-item interactions through graph structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, the inherent complexity of GNNs, particularly in terms of computational overhead and over-smoothing, has motivated the development of simplified variants such as LightGCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which focuses on linear propagation and avoids unnecessary nonlinear operations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This approach has shown substantial improvements over more complex models like NGCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

The use of binary representations for recommendation has also gained traction, as it enables efficient online inference by reducing memory usage and computational costs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Prior work on binarized collaborative filtering, such as Binarized Collaborative Filtering with Distilling Graph Convolutional Networks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\], has explored the use of binary codes to represent users and items, leveraging distillation techniques to preserve ranking information. However, these methods often suffer from information loss due to the quantization process, leading to performance degradation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. To mitigate this, recent studies have proposed self-supervised learning approaches that enhance the robustness of graph-based recommendation models by incorporating auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. These methods aim to minimize information loss during the binarization process, improving both accuracy and robustness.

Furthermore, the integration of contrastive learning into graph collaborative filtering has been explored to address data sparsity issues, with methods such as Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\] explicitly incorporating structural and semantic neighbors into contrastive pairs. This approach enhances the model's ability to capture meaningful relationships by leveraging both local and global graph structures \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]. Similarly, the use of graph convolution networks (GCNs) for recommendation has been extensively studied, with models like DeepWalk \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\] and LightGCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] demonstrating the effectiveness of learning latent representations from graph structures. However, these models often struggle with scalability and over-smoothing, prompting further research into simplification and efficiency improvements \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\].

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-for-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Neural Networks for Collaborative Filtering: 5 papers found.
Recent advancements in graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly impacted collaborative filtering by enabling more effective modeling of user-item interactions. Early methods, such as matrix factorization, relied on pre-defined features to generate embeddings, but these approaches often failed to capture the collaborative signal inherent in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. To address this limitation, NGCF was introduced to integrate the bipartite graph structure into the embedding process, allowing for the propagation of embeddings and capturing high-order connectivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, GCN-based models like NGCF were found to include unnecessary components such as feature transformation and nonlinear activation, which hindered training and degraded performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. In response, LightGCN was proposed as a simplified model that focuses solely on neighborhood aggregation, achieving substantial improvements over NGCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, Multi-GCCF extended this idea by incorporating multiple graphs—user-user and item-item—to better model proximal information and account for the differences between user and item nodes \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Despite these advances, challenges such as over-smoothing and scalability remained, prompting further innovations like SimRec, which combines knowledge distillation and contrastive learning to improve efficiency while maintaining performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to refine GNN-based collaborative filtering, emphasizing the importance of simplification, scalability, and effective modeling of complex interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\].

Despite the effectiveness, these methods suffer from data sparsity in real scenarios. In order to reduce the influence of data sparsity, contrastive learning is adopted in graph collaborative filtering for enhancing the performance. However, these methods typically construct the contrastive pairs by random sampling, which neglect the neighboring relations among users (or items) and fail to fully exploit the potential of contrastive learning for recommendation. To tackle the above issue, we propose a novel contrastive learning approach, named Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning, named NCL, which explicitly incorporates the potential neighbors into contrastive pairs. Specifically, we introduce the neighbors of a user (or an item) from graph structure and semantic space respectively. For the structural neighbors on the interaction graph, we develop a novel structure-contrastive objective that regards users (or items) and their structural neighbors as positive contrastive pairs. In implementation, the representations of users (or items) and neighbors correspond to the outputs of different GNN layers. Furthermore, to excavate the potential neighbor relation in semantic space, we assume that users with similar representations are within the semantic neighborhood, and incorporate these semantic neighbors into the prototype-contrastive objective. The proposed NCL can be optimized with EM algorithm and generalized to apply to graph collaborative filtering methods. Extensive experiments on five public datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed NCL, notably with 26% and 17% performance gain over a competitive graph collaborative filtering base model on the Yelp and Amazon-book datasets, respectively.
