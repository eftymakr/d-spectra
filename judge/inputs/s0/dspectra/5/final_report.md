## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area in natural language processing (NLP), aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. This task is particularly relevant for applications requiring real-time adaptation, such as information retrieval, semantic similarity, and task-specific embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Existing approaches, however, often suffer from significant computational overhead due to the need to re-encode the entire corpus for each new instruction \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more efficient methods that can adapt pre-computed embeddings in real time \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)\].

Several works have explored the use of pre-trained models for instruction-following tasks. BERT, for instance, is a bidirectional transformer model that pre-trains on unlabeled text and can be fine-tuned for various tasks, including question answering and language inference \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08773)\]. Similarly, the Transformer architecture, introduced in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\], has become a foundational model for sequence transduction tasks, offering superior performance and parallelizability compared to recurrent and convolutional models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. These models, while powerful, typically require retraining or re-encoding for new tasks, which is computationally expensive \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\].

Recent efforts have focused on instruction tuning, where pre-trained models are fine-tuned using instruction-following data to improve their ability to generalize to new tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03277)\]. For example, Self-Instruct introduces a framework for improving instruction-following capabilities by bootstrapping off the model's own generations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06726)\]. Similarly, the Flan Collection provides a comprehensive set of instruction-tuning methods and datasets, demonstrating that task balancing and enrichment techniques are critical for effective instruction tuning \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09689)\]. These approaches rely on human-generated instruction data, which is often limited in quantity and diversity \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\].

To address these limitations, Unnatural Instructions was introduced, a large dataset of creative and diverse instructions generated with minimal human labor \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\]. This dataset has been used to train models that perform well on various benchmarks, surpassing the performance of models trained on manually-curated datasets \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13688)\]. Similarly, the INSTRUCTOR method introduces a single embedder that can generate text embeddings tailored to different downstream tasks and domains without additional training \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05250)\]. This approach is particularly promising for real-time applications where re-encoding the entire corpus is impractical \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07316)\].

In addition to instruction tuning, there has been growing interest in in-context learning, where models perform new tasks by conditioning on a few input-label pairs \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14327)\]. This approach has shown promise in reducing the need for extensive training data

### <a id='category-instruction-tuning-techniques'></a>Instruction Tuning Techniques: 8 papers found.
Instruction tuning techniques have seen significant advancements in recent years, with researchers exploring ways to improve the instruction-following capabilities of large language models (LLMs) without relying heavily on human-annotated data. Several approaches have been proposed to generate instruction data automatically, such as Self-Instruct \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\], which bootstraps instruction data from a model's own generations, and Evol-Instruct \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\], which iteratively evolves instructions to increase complexity. These methods aim to reduce the reliance on labor-intensive human annotation while improving the quality and diversity of instruction data. Similarly, GPT-4 has been used to generate high-quality instruction-following data for LLM finetuning, leading to superior zero-shot performance on new tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03277)\]. In the multimodal domain, instruction tuning has also been explored, with models like LLaVA being trained on generated visual instruction data to enhance their ability to understand and respond to multimodal inputs \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\]. Additionally, methods such as Dynosaur \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14327)\] leverage existing annotated datasets to automatically curate instruction-tuning data, offering cost-effective and high-quality data generation. These approaches collectively highlight the growing trend of using machine-generated instruction data to improve the instruction-following abilities of LLMs, reducing the need for extensive human annotation while enhancing model performance across diverse tasks.

### <a id='category-large-scale-instruction-datasets'></a>Large-scale Instruction Datasets: 5 papers found.
The category "Large-scale Instruction Datasets" encompasses several efforts to create extensive resources for training and evaluating models that follow natural language instructions. These datasets aim to improve the generalization ability of AI systems across diverse tasks by providing structured, human-authored instructions alongside input-output pairs. For instance, NATURAL INSTRUCTIONS introduces a dataset of 61 distinct tasks with 193k task instances, derived from crowdsourced instructions and mapped to a unified schema \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08773)\]. This dataset enables models to learn from task-specific instructions and generalize to unseen tasks, showing a 19% improvement when instructions are utilized \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08773)\]. Similarly, Unnatural Instructions leverages model-generated data to expand instruction sets, collecting 240,000 examples with minimal human labor, which rivals the effectiveness of manually-curated datasets \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09689)\]. Meanwhile, the Stanford Question Answering Dataset (SQuAD) focuses on reading comprehension, offering a benchmark for evaluating models on complex reasoning tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05250)\]. The Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) further extends the scope by evaluating text embeddings across 58 datasets and 112 languages, highlighting the need for versatile embedding methods that perform well across diverse tasks \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07316)\]. These datasets collectively underscore the importance of instruction-based learning and the challenges of generalization, providing foundational resources for advancing instruction-following systems.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Large language models (LMs) are able to in-context learn—perform a new task via inference alone by conditioning on a few input-label pairs (demonstrations) and making predictions for new inputs. However, there has been little understanding of how the model learns and which aspects of the demonstrations contribute to end task performance. In this paper, we show that ground truth demonstrations are in fact not required—randomly replacing labels in the demonstrations barely hurts performance on a range of classification and multi-choice tasks, consistently over 12 different models including GPT-3. Instead, we find that other aspects of the demonstrations are the key drivers of endtask performance, including the fact that they provide a few examples of (1) the label space, (2) the distribution of the input text, and (3) the overall format of the sequence. Together, our analysis provides a new way of understanding how and why in-context learning works, while opening up new questions about how much can be learned from large language models through inference alone.»
