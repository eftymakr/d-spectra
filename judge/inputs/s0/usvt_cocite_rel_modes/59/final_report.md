## Related Works

Fairness in recommendation systems has garnered significant attention in recent years, with a growing body of research addressing the trade-offs between recommendation accuracy and fairness. Prior work has identified that fairness constraints often lead to a decline in recommendation accuracy, particularly when fairness is enforced at the item level \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10671)\]. This trade-off is analogous to economic principles, where fairness considerations in re-ranking can be likened to a commodity tax on suppliers, ultimately translating into a cost passed on to consumers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. The extent to which such fairness constraints impact accuracy is formalized using the concept of elasticity, which measures how changes in fairness affect user-side performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. 

Several studies have explored fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] introduces a framework for evaluating fairness in rankings through pairwise comparisons, offering a tractable means to reason about fairness in rankings. Similarly, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\] proposes a two-sided fairness-aware recommendation model that considers both customer and provider fairness, ensuring that neither side is disadvantaged. The work in \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15781)\] extends this idea by using Lorenz dominance to generate rankings that are fair towards both users and item producers, maximizing utility for the worse-off individuals. 

The concept of fairness through awareness, introduced in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\], provides a foundational framework for fair classification, which has been adapted to recommendation systems. This approach ensures that similar individuals are treated similarly, which is crucial for maintaining fairness in personalized recommendations. Additionally, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08804)\] presents new fairness metrics for collaborative filtering, addressing different forms of unfairness that arise due to biased historical data. These metrics are optimized by adding fairness terms to the learning objective, effectively reducing unfairness in recommendations.

In the context of two-sided platforms, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\] investigates the problem of fair recommendation, considering both customer and producer fairness. The work proposes a novel mapping of the fair recommendation problem to a constrained version of the problem of fairly allocating indivisible goods, ensuring that both sides are treated fairly. Similarly, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09024)\] introduces a two-sided fairness-aware recommendation model that balances recommendation quality, customer fairness, and provider fairness. The model is designed to maintain a high level of personalization while ensuring fairness for both sides.

The challenge of achieving fairness in rankings while maintaining ranking quality has been addressed in several works. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\] introduces a constrained ranking maximization problem, where fairness or diversity constraints are imposed alongside the traditional ranking objectives. The work provides fast exact and approximation algorithms, along with complementary hardness results, to solve this problem efficiently. \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06660)\] further explores the issue of provider fairness

### <a id='category-fairness-in-recommendation-systems'></a>Fairness in Recommendation Systems: 10 papers found.
Fairness in recommendation systems has been a growing area of research, focusing on balancing accuracy with equitable treatment of users and providers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. Prior work has highlighted the trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness, noting that fairness constraints can lead to a decline in user-side accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. This trade-off is often compared to economic concepts, such as commodity tax transfer, where fairness considerations on the item side translate into a cost for users \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. Several studies have proposed methods to address this challenge, including fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms that balance accuracy and provider-side fairness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. These approaches often incorporate user tolerance for diversity in recommendations, ensuring that fairness is personalized to individual preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Additionally, some research has explored fairness from a two-sided perspective, considering both customer and producer fairness in recommendation systems \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]. For instance, the FairRec algorithm ensures fairness for both sides by guaranteeing exposure for producers and maintaining customer satisfaction \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]. Other studies have introduced dynamic fairness learning frameworks that adapt to changing item popularity over time, ensuring long-term fairness in recommendations \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03584)\]. Moreover, the concept of elasticity has been applied to evaluate the fairness-accuracy trade-off, leading to the development of the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) as an evaluation framework \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. This approach enables a more comprehensive assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms by considering different elasticity levels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]. Overall, the field has seen significant advancements in addressing fairness concerns, with a growing emphasis on multi-sided fairness and dynamic adaptation to ensure equitable outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.00780)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10764)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03584)\].

As ranking positions influence the amount of attention the ranked subjects receive, biases in rankings can lead to unfair distribution of opportunities and resources such as jobs or income. This paper proposes new measures and mechanisms to quantify and mitigate unfairness from a bias inherent to all rankings, namely, the position bias which leads to disproportionately less attention being paid to low-ranked subjects.
