## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy protection in data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for data privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, especially in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Specifically, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This issue has been explored in several works that examine the security and robustness of DP protocols. 

Several studies have focused on the vulnerabilities of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols, which are designed to protect user privacy by perturbing data before transmission \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\] highlights that LDP protocols are susceptible to data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users and send crafted data to skew the results. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\] demonstrates that LDP is highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can obscure the distribution of honest users' inputs. These findings underscore the need for more robust mechanisms to ensure the integrity of DP protocols.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed verifiable DP mechanisms that allow for the verification of the correctness of the noise added during the privatization process \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. The concept of verifiable differential privacy has been explored in \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\], where the authors introduce a framework that enables verification of DP guarantees. This aligns with the proposed Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP) definition, which incorporates additional verification mechanisms to ensure the correctness of DP execution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. 

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) have also been considered as a means to achieve verifiable DP. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as demonstrated in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. This finding is relevant to the discussion of VDDP, where ZKPs are not required for the protocol to function correctly.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-security-mechanisms'></a>Differential Privacy Security Mechanisms: 3 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy Security Mechanisms" explores various approaches to enhance the security and reliability of differential privacy (DP) protocols, particularly in the face of malicious data providers or unfaithful server execution. Existing studies highlight that local differential privacy (LDP) mechanisms, such as randomized response, are vulnerable to output manipulation by malicious participants who can distort the data collector's estimation of background distributions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. To counteract this, researchers have proposed verifiable randomization mechanisms that allow the data collector to verify the correctness of randomization performed by data providers, thereby preventing output manipulation and ensuring the integrity of the protocol \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, there is growing interest in systems like Cryptε, which aim to bridge the gap between the central and local models of DP by enabling accurate and expressive DP programs without requiring a trusted data collector \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. This is achieved through the use of two non-colluding untrusted servers that process encrypted data, ensuring that only the final noisy output is revealed \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]. Another line of research introduces interactive proofs for differential privacy, which require the publishing entity to provide a zero-knowledge proof that confirms the output is both differentially private and reliable \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. This approach leverages public and private randomness to verify DP computations while maintaining privacy guarantees \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. These developments collectively emphasize the need for robust verification mechanisms to ensure the faithful execution of DP protocols in distributed environments, addressing the limitations of traditional approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]\[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ac8be9c934d6ff9ae095ebde81c8d3a57f7b918d)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\].

### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-research'></a>Local Differential Privacy Research: 8 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has been extensively studied for its ability to protect user privacy during data collection and analysis. Researchers have focused on improving the utility of LDP protocols while ensuring strong privacy guarantees, but significant challenges remain, particularly regarding security vulnerabilities. For instance, attackers can exploit LDP protocols by injecting fake users or manipulating data to distort statistical results, such as estimating high frequencies or identifying heavy hitters \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This highlights a critical gap in the security of LDP protocols, which has been less explored compared to their utility improvements \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Additionally, LDP protocols are highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, especially when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large, allowing a small fraction of users to obscure the true distribution of data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. To address these issues, some studies have proposed countermeasures, though their effectiveness varies depending on the scenario \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Furthermore, LDP protocols often suffer from high noise and overhead due to their stronger privacy guarantees in the central model, which can lead to suboptimal performance in practical deployments \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Recent work has also focused on optimizing LDP mechanisms for multidimensional data and repeated data collection, aiming to improve accuracy and efficiency while maintaining privacy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. These efforts underscore the ongoing challenges and advancements in the field of LDP research.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-techniques'></a>Differential Privacy Techniques: 3 papers found.
The shuffled model has emerged as a promising approach in differential privacy, offering a middle ground between the local and central models. Unlike the local model, where each user individually randomizes their data before sending it to a server, and the central model, which requires a trusted server to collect and process data, the shuffled model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes user messages before aggregation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This allows for greater accuracy than the local model while avoiding the need for a fully trusted server, as seen in the central model \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. Recent work has demonstrated that the shuffled model can achieve the same level of accuracy as the central model for sum queries, without requiring cryptographic secure function evaluation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. However, there are limitations; for certain tasks, such as the selection problem, the shuffled model may require exponentially higher sample complexity compared to the central model under specific constraints \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. These findings highlight the trade-offs and potential of the shuffled model in distributed differential privacy settings. The model's simplicity and scalability make it a valuable direction for future research, especially in scenarios where trust in a central server is limited \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\].
