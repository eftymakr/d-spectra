## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area in the context of privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in response to the "right to be forgotten" (RTBF) legislation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. This concept allows users to request the removal of their data from a trained model, a necessity in distributed learning frameworks like Federated Learning (FL) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Early works focused on centralized unlearning, where full access to training data is available for the server to conduct the unlearning process \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\]. However, FL introduces unique challenges due to the decentralized nature of data and the lack of global access to training data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\].

Several studies have explored unlearning in FL by proposing methods that either retrain the model from scratch or use historical parameter updates to reconstruct the unlearned model \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. For instance, FedEraser leverages the historical parameter updates stored at the central server to efficiently reconstruct the unlearned model, achieving a 4× speed-up compared to retraining from scratch \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06edb1f6eadb6c141d720fcca4c854578dc5d670)\]. Similarly, the paper "Federated Unlearning: How to Efficiently Erase a Client in FL?" proposes a two-step approach: local unlearning at the client and then a few rounds of federated learning with the remaining clients to obtain the unlearned global model \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\].

The importance of verifiable unlearning has also been emphasized, as unlearning itself may not be sufficient without independent verification \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. VeriFi introduces a framework that enables users to verify the unlearning effect through marking and checking mechanisms, ensuring the unlearning process is both effective and transparent \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\]. Additionally, the paper "Communication Efficient and Provable Federated Unlearning" introduces a framework that guarantees the unlearned model is statistically indistinguishable from the one trained without the deleted data, ensuring both efficiency and accuracy \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\].

Privacy-preserving techniques have also been explored, such as the use of differential privacy in FedRecovery to ensure the unlearned model and retrained model are statistically indistinguishable \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.11018)\]. Furthermore, the paper "Federated Unlearning with Knowledge Distillation" proposes a method that leverages knowledge distillation to restore model performance without requiring any data from the clients \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\].

In addition to these methods, recent works have focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of unlearning in FL by addressing specific challenges such as data poisoning and backdoor attacks \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/efab3b30d6f67f55c68f9dc7c65b2b54b1789030)\]. ### <a id='category-federated-unlearning-research'></a>Federated Unlearning Research: 26 papers found.
The category "Federated Unlearning Research" explores the challenges and solutions for enabling data removal from federated learning (FL) models, driven by the "right to be forgotten" and privacy concerns. This research area has gained traction as existing machine unlearning techniques, designed for centralized learning, are not directly applicable to FL due to its distributed nature and lack of global data access \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. Several studies have proposed novel approaches to address this gap, such as FedEraser, which leverages historical parameter updates to reconstruct unlearned models efficiently \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\], and SFU, which eliminates a target client's contribution without requiring additional storage by performing gradient ascent in an orthogonal subspace \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12448)\]. Additionally, methods like VeriFi introduce verifiable federated unlearning frameworks that allow clients to independently verify the unlearning effect through marking and checking mechanisms \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. Other works, such as FAST, focus on eliminating malicious clients' contributions while maintaining model performance and efficiency \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2f69d7e9a7428a7e31de83b993a2142d0c2cde67)\], while Knot introduces a clustered aggregation mechanism to limit retraining costs during data erasure \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/70954dd11d1463bc665f803d84b15a4d83805ec0)\]. Furthermore, Starfish enhances privacy by using Two-Party Computation (2PC) techniques to protect client data during unlearning \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09724)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the growing interest in developing efficient, privacy-preserving, and verifiable federated unlearning methods that align with regulatory requirements and practical FL constraints.

[Snippet]: «The increasing data privacy concerns in recommendation systems have made federated recommendations attract more and more attention. Existing federated recommendation systems mainly focus on how to effectively and securely learn personal interests and preferences from their on-device interaction data. Still, none of them considers how to efficiently erase a user's contribution to the federated training process. We argue that such a dual setting is necessary. First, from the privacy protection perspective, "the right to be forgotten (RTBF)" requires that users have the right to withdraw their data contributions. Without the reversible ability, federated recommendation systems risk breaking data protection regulations. On the other hand, enabling a federated recommender to forget specific users can improve its robustness and resistance to malicious clients' attacks. To support user unlearning in federated recommendation systems, we propose an efficient unlearning method FRU (Federated Recommendation Unlearning), inspired by the log-based rollback mechanism of transactions in database management systems. It removes a user's contribution by rolling back and calibrating the historical parameter updates and then uses these updates to speed up federated recommender reconstruction. However, storing all historical parameter updates on resource-constrained personal devices is challenging and even infeasible. In light of this challenge, we propose a small-sized negative sampling method to reduce the number of item embedding updates and an importance-based update selection mechanism to store only important model updates. To evaluate the effectiveness of FRU, we propose an attack method to disturb federated recommenders via a group of compromised users. Then, we use FRU to recover recommenders by eliminating these users' influence. Finally, we conduct extensive experiments on two real-world recommendation datasets (i.e. MovieLens-100k and Steam-200k) with two widely used federated recommenders to show the efficiency and effectiveness of our proposed approaches.»

### <a id='category-federated-learning-mechanisms'></a>Federated Learning Mechanisms: 2 papers found.
The category "Federated Learning Mechanisms" encompasses research aimed at improving privacy and efficiency in distributed machine learning systems. Recent work has focused on federated unlearning, which seeks to remove the influence of specific user data from global models while maintaining model performance \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]. This is particularly relevant in scenarios like Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR), where user privacy is paramount due to the use of implicit feedback such as clicks \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]. Researchers have developed incentive mechanisms to retain valuable users by balancing the trade-offs between training losses and privacy costs \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]. Additionally, frameworks like federated random forest (RevFRF) have been introduced to address participant revocation, ensuring secure removal of revoked users' data while preserving model accuracy \[[27](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f07b5043bd6fbb41f85ed66cd73b8a7a1ba9a4f5)\]. These approaches highlight the growing emphasis on both technical and economic aspects of federated learning, aiming to align user privacy with system efficiency \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]\[[27](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f07b5043bd6fbb41f85ed66cd73b8a7a1ba9a4f5)\].
