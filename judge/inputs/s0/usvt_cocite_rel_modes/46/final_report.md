## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in recommender systems by leveraging user behavior across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Early approaches primarily relied on Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to model sequential patterns \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. While MCs are effective in sparse datasets, they fail to capture long-term dependencies, whereas RNNs, though more expressive, suffer from computational inefficiency and difficulty in handling long sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, self-attention based models like SASRec were introduced, which enable the capture of long-term semantics while maintaining computational efficiency by focusing on relevant items in the user's history \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\].

Several works have explored the use of domain-aware modules to enhance cross-domain recommendations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. For example, DA-GCN introduces a domain-aware graph convolution network to learn user-specific node representations and employs attention mechanisms to guide message passing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Similarly, DASL proposes a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains to improve cross-domain CTR predictions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. These methods often focus on transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain but may overlook the inter-sequence relationships and the potential for bidirectional knowledge exchange \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\].

Recent studies have also emphasized the importance of modeling inter-sequence and intra-sequence relationships in CDSR. C2DSR, for instance, leverages both intra- and inter-sequence item interactions to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. The model uses graph neural networks to mine collaborative relationships and sequential attentive encoders to capture sequential dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Additionally, the paper introduces a contrastive cross-domain infomax objective to enhance the correlation between single- and cross-domain user representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\].

Other approaches, such as π-Net and PSJNet, have focused on shared-account cross-domain scenarios, where multiple users share a single account and behaviors are available across domains \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]\[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. π-Net introduces a shared account filter unit and a cross-domain transfer unit to address the challenges of shared accounts and cross-domain settings \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. ### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation'></a>Cross Domain Sequential Recommendation: 20 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research area to address data sparsity and cold-start problems in sequential recommendation systems by leveraging cross-domain user interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing approaches often rely on domain-specific modules or knowledge transfer mechanisms, such as graph neural networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\], attention mechanisms \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\], or dual learning frameworks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\], to capture cross-domain preferences. However, these methods typically neglect the inherent power of the self-attention mechanism in transformers, which can naturally model complex correlations among sequential behaviors \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Recent works have begun to explore enhancing self-attention for CDSR, such as introducing Pareto-optimal self-attention to dynamically balance cross-domain attention scores \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\], or formulating cross-domain learning as a multi-objective optimization problem \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These approaches aim to improve recommendation performance while mitigating negative transfer between domains \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. Additionally, some studies have proposed adaptive mechanisms for open-world environments, such as the Adaptive Multi-Interest Debiasing framework \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05027)\], which addresses data distribution shifts and class imbalance issues \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6dd0605f7de7581c69b4acf0ab78000748a09f3e)\]. Others have focused on modeling mixed information flow between domains, incorporating both behavioral and knowledge transfer units to enhance sequence representations \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Despite these advances, challenges remain in effectively capturing inter-sequence relationships and ensuring robust knowledge transfer across domains \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\]. Overall, the field is moving toward more integrated and adaptive models that leverage the strengths of both self-attention and cross-domain learning \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\].

### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-models'></a>Sequential Recommendation Models: 5 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models have evolved significantly, with approaches ranging from traditional Markov Chains (MCs) to advanced recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and self-attention-based models. MCs assume that a user's next action depends only on their last few actions, making them suitable for sparse datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. In contrast, RNNs capture longer-term dependencies but often struggle with computational efficiency and sparse data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. More recently, self-attention mechanisms have emerged as a powerful alternative, enabling models like SASRec to balance long-term semantics with efficient prediction by focusing on relevant actions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Similarly, BERT4Rec introduces bidirectional self-attention, allowing for richer contextual understanding by considering both left and right contexts in user behavior sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. These models address limitations of unidirectional architectures, such as restricted hidden representations and rigid sequence assumptions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Additionally, S3-Rec leverages self-supervised learning and mutual information maximization to enhance data representation, particularly in sparse settings \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. Meanwhile, session-based recommendation models like NARM incorporate attention mechanisms to capture user intent within short sessions, improving performance on both short and long sessions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These developments highlight a shift toward more flexible and context-aware models that better handle the complexities of sequential recommendation.

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 5 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research direction to address data sparsity in recommender systems by leveraging sequential behaviors across multiple domains \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08272)\]. However, existing methods often rely on overlapping users or items between domains, limiting their applicability in practical scenarios \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08272)\]. To mitigate this, recent works have explored cross-domain sequential recommendation frameworks that integrate multi-domain data without requiring explicit user or item overlap \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206)\]. For instance, the multi-view graph contrastive learning (MGCL) framework introduces a contrastive mechanism to capture both intra-domain sequential patterns and inter-domain user preferences, enhancing recommendation performance by leveraging complementary information across domains \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206)\]. Similarly, the FedDCSR framework combines federated learning with cross-domain sequential recommendation to preserve data privacy while utilizing domain knowledge, addressing the challenge of data sharing restrictions under regulations like GDPR \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08420)\]. Additionally, the Shared-account Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (SCSR) task focuses on scenarios where multiple users share a single account, requiring accurate user-specific representation and domain filtering to improve cross-domain recommendations \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08088)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in developing robust and privacy-preserving CDSR models that can effectively handle domain-specific and cross-domain sequential patterns without relying on overlapping entities \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08272)\]\[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1c7b8be5f3849a3fd4af780b58eb28355641206)\]\[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08088)\]\[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.08420)\].
