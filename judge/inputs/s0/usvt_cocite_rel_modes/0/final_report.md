## Related Works

Traditional approaches to tax policy design often rely on static models that fail to capture the complexity of taxpayer behavior and economic heterogeneity. For instance, the U.S. federal income tax system, while effective in reducing inequality, lacks adaptability in response to changing economic conditions \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a065b1629ff85e2a0db928bd8ce115b2f5e2a80f)\]. Similarly, models like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework, though dynamic, do not adequately address taxpayer heterogeneity or irrational decision-making \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. This limitation has prompted researchers to explore alternative methodologies, such as agent-based modeling (ABM), which can simulate heterogeneous agent behaviors and social interactions \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a065b1629ff85e2a0db928bd8ce115b2f5e2a80f)\].

Agent-based models have been widely used to study tax compliance and evasion. For example, the Ising model has been adapted to simulate tax evasion dynamics, incorporating social interactions and enforcement mechanisms \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/0806.0344)\]. These models allow for the simulation of how tax evasion may vary over time and how policy interventions, such as audits, can influence behavior \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. Furthermore, the majority-vote model has been applied to study tax evasion on various network structures, demonstrating the robustness of such models across different topologies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/0910.1964)\]. These studies highlight the importance of social and network structures in shaping tax compliance decisions.

Recent advances in computational methods have also enabled the integration of machine learning techniques into economic modeling. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has been proposed as a tool for solving complex macroeconomic models, allowing agents to learn optimal strategies through interaction with the environment \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. This approach has been applied to monetary policy design, where DRL agents outperform traditional adaptive learning methods in solving dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. Similarly, the use of large language models (LLMs) in conjunction with ABM has been proposed as a novel approach to designing adaptive tax policies \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. This integration allows for the simulation of heterogeneous taxpayer behaviors while enabling the government to iteratively optimize tax rates based on real-time data.

The use of multi-agent systems to model economic behavior has also gained traction, particularly in the context of tax credit distribution and household decision-making \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]. These models incorporate reinforcement learning agents of different types, allowing for the simulation of complex interactions within economic systems. The inclusion of heterogeneous agents enables a more accurate representation of real-world economic conditions, including disparities in income and access to resources \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\].

In summary, while traditional tax policy models have limitations in capturing heterogeneity and irrational behavior, emerging methodologies such as agent-based modeling and deep reinforcement learning offer promising alternatives. ### <a id='category-tax-compliance-and-evasion-models'></a>Tax Compliance and Evasion Models: 11 papers found.
Tax compliance and evasion models have been extensively explored using statistical mechanics frameworks, particularly the Ising model, to simulate the dynamics of taxpayer behavior and the impact of enforcement mechanisms. Researchers have incorporated tax evasion into the Ising model, allowing for the analysis of how social interactions, trust in institutions, and external factors like audit rates influence compliance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16977)\]. These models demonstrate that even minimal enforcement, such as audit rates, can significantly reduce tax evasion \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that the behavior of tax evaders can be influenced by the actions of others, leading to complex patterns of compliance and evasion that vary over time \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/0801.2980)\]. The use of agent-based simulations has further enabled the examination of tax evasion on different network structures, including Barabasi-Albert and Erdos-Renyi graphs, revealing that the Zaklan model is robust across various topologies and dynamic settings \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/0910.1964)\]. Moreover, the integration of social and economic factors, such as fairness and conformity, has been explored to better understand taxpayer decision-making and the role of social norms in shaping compliance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1112.0233)\]. These findings highlight the importance of considering both individual and collective behaviors when designing effective tax policies.

### <a id='category-economic-models-and-reinforcement-learning'></a>Economic Models And Reinforcement Learning: 4 papers found.
The integration of reinforcement learning (RL) into economic modeling has gained traction as a means to address the limitations of traditional methods in capturing heterogeneous agent behavior and dynamic policy interactions. Recent studies demonstrate that RL offers a flexible framework for solving dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models by enabling agents to learn optimal strategies through interaction with the environment \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. This approach is particularly valuable in scenarios where agents exhibit bounded rationality, as RL allows for the modeling of adaptive learning and policy responses without assuming perfect foresight \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. For instance, RL has been applied to classical macroeconomic models to simulate the interaction between monetary and fiscal policies, showing that artificial agents can outperform traditional adaptive learning methods in diverse policy regimes \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09368)\]. Additionally, multi-agent RL models have been developed to incorporate a broader range of economic actors, including households, firms, and the government, while accounting for the complex interactions within a real-world economic system \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]. These models also emphasize the role of government policy in addressing economic inequality, such as through the strategic distribution of tax credits to reduce disparities and improve social welfare \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17252)\]. Furthermore, RL has been used to compute optimal interest rate reaction functions, incorporating nonlinear economic structures and asymmetric preferences, with results showing significant improvements in policy performance compared to conventional methods \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/faa50da104ab3b55075f39e1f416ab5b9fc2725e)\]. These advancements highlight the growing potential of RL in economic modeling, particularly in its ability to handle heterogeneity, adaptability, and policy optimization.
