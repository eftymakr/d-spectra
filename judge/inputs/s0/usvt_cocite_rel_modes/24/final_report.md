## Related Works

Diffusion models have become a cornerstone in generative modeling, particularly for image and video synthesis, due to their ability to produce high-quality outputs through iterative noise estimation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. However, the computational and memory demands of these models hinder their deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising technique to reduce model size and accelerate inference without retraining \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]. PTQ methods aim to compress models by reducing the bit-width of weights and activations, often achieving significant efficiency gains \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. 

Early PTQ approaches for diffusion models, such as Q-Diffusion, focused on addressing the challenges posed by the multi-timestep pipeline and the bimodal activation distribution of the noise estimation network \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. These methods introduced techniques like timestep-aware calibration and split shortcut quantization to maintain performance during quantization \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Similarly, PTQ4DiT was developed to handle the unique challenges of diffusion transformers (DiTs), including salient channels and temporal variability in activations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. This work proposed Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's $\rho$-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) to improve quantization accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. 

For video generation, the challenges of quantization are further compounded by the need to maintain spatiotemporal coherence across frames \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03270)\]. Existing quantization methods for image generation models often fail to generalize to video tasks, leading to loss of information and misalignment between optimization objectives and video-specific requirements \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. To address these issues, recent works such as ViDiT-Q and HQ-DiT have explored tailored quantization schemes for DiT models, achieving W8A8 and W4A8 precision with minimal performance degradation \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. These methods focus on dynamic quantization intervals and efficient calibration strategies to preserve visual quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. 

The Q-VDiT framework proposed in this work builds upon these advancements by introducing Token-aware Quantization Estimator (TQE) and Temporal Maintenance Distillation (TMD) to specifically address the challenges of video generation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. QE compensates for quantization errors in both token and feature dimensions, while TMD preserves spatiotemporal correlations between frames \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. These innovations enable the framework to achieve a scene consistency of 23.40, outperforming current state-of-the-art quantization methods by 1.9$\times$ \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\].

### <a id='category-diffusion-model-quantization-techniques'></a>Diffusion Model Quantization Techniques: 18 papers found.
Diffusion model quantization techniques have emerged as critical for enabling efficient deployment of these models on resource-constrained devices. Several approaches have been proposed to address the unique challenges posed by diffusion models, such as the temporal variation in activation distributions and the multi-time-step pipeline. For instance, PTQ methods tailored for diffusion models have been developed to handle these issues, such as timestep-aware calibration and split shortcut quantization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Similarly, for Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), challenges like salient channel magnitudes and temporal activation variability have been tackled through methods like Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. Additionally, methods such as PTQ4DiT and Q-DiT have been introduced to improve quantization accuracy by dynamically adjusting quantization intervals and incorporating sample-wise activation quantization \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. These techniques aim to maintain high generation quality while reducing computational and memory demands. Furthermore, data-free and parameter-efficient fine-tuning frameworks like EfficientDM have been proposed to achieve QAT-level performance with PTQ-like efficiency \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03270)\]. Overall, these approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to balance performance and efficiency in diffusion model quantization.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-and-quantization'></a>Diffusion Models and Quantization: 7 papers found.
Diffusion models have gained significant attention for their ability to generate high-quality images and videos, but their computational demands pose challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\]. To address this, researchers have explored training diffusion models in latent spaces, which reduces computational requirements while preserving visual fidelity \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. This approach has been extended to video generation, where models are trained with temporal layers to enable high-resolution, temporally coherent video synthesis \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\]. However, quantization of diffusion models remains challenging due to the sensitivity of both weights and activations, especially at low bit-widths \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05007)\]. Existing methods often struggle with maintaining quality and consistency during quantization, leading to the development of specialized techniques such as SVDQuant, which uses a low-rank branch to handle outliers and improve efficiency \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05007)\]. Additionally, for natural language models, post-training quantization approaches like ZeroQuant have been introduced to reduce memory and computational costs without significant accuracy loss \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01861)\]. These efforts highlight the ongoing research into optimizing diffusion models for both image and video generation, balancing quality with efficiency \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]\[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\]\[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05007)\].

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-for-text-to-image-synthesis'></a>Diffusion Models for Text to Image Synthesis: 3 papers found.
CogVideoX \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06072)\] represents a significant advancement in text-to-video generation by addressing key limitations of previous models, such as limited movement, short duration, and poor narrative coherence. It introduces a 3D Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to enhance video compression and fidelity, along with an expert transformer with adaptive LayerNorm to improve text-video alignment. Additionally, the model employs progressive training and multi-resolution frame packing to generate long, coherent videos with significant motion. These innovations contribute to state-of-the-art performance, as evidenced by superior results in both machine metrics and human evaluations. Meanwhile, diffusion models for image synthesis \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05233)\] have also seen improvements through classifier guidance, which balances diversity and fidelity, achieving competitive FID scores on ImageNet datasets. Furthermore, classifier-free guided diffusion models \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03142)\] have been optimized for efficiency, enabling faster inference by distilling models to require fewer sampling steps while maintaining high-quality outputs. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of diffusion-based generative models across both image and video domains.
