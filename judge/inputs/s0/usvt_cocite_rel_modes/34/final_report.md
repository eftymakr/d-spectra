## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advances focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as prediction-only or predict-then-optimize (PnO) methods suffer from goal mismatch, where the forecasting and allocation objectives are not aligned \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. This issue is further exacerbated by the introduction of state-of-the-art forecasting models, which introduce additional uncertainty into the predicted results \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.09363)\]. To address these challenges, this paper introduces the Risk-aware Time-Series Predict-and-Allocate (RTS-PnO) framework, which is designed to be agnostic to the underlying forecasting model and incorporates adaptive uncertainty calibration \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\].

Several recent works have explored the use of deep learning for time series forecasting. Transformers have gained significant traction due to their ability to capture long-range dependencies, although they face challenges in non-stationary data and computational efficiency \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. The PatchTST model, for instance, addresses these issues by introducing a segmentation-based approach and channel-independent design, improving long-term forecasting accuracy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. Similarly, the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) combines recurrent and self-attention layers to enable interpretable multi-horizon forecasting \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. These models, however, often require extensive tuning and are not inherently designed for integration with allocation strategies.

The use of large language models (LLMs) for time series forecasting has also been explored, with studies showing that models like GPT-3 and LLaMA-2 can perform zero-shot forecasting comparable to specialized models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. This approach, however, relies on encoding time series as strings of numerical digits and may not generalize well to complex financial data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. The Time-LLM framework attempts to bridge this gap by reprogramming LLMs for time series forecasting while preserving the backbone of the language model \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13408)\].

In the context of financial applications, the DeepAR model has demonstrated improvements in probabilistic forecasting, particularly in retail and demand prediction \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. However, it is limited to autoregressive recurrent networks and does not address the integration of forecasting with allocation decisions. The Deep Factors model, on the other hand, combines classical statistical models with deep neural networks to handle uncertainty and scalability in large collections of time series \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\].

The recent development of the Autoformer model introduces an Auto-Correlation mechanism, which enhances the efficiency and accuracy of long-term forecasting by capturing periodic dependencies \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12417)\]. ### <a id='category-time-series-forecasting-with-transformers'></a>Time Series Forecasting with Transformers: 9 papers found.
The category "Time Series Forecasting with Transformers" encompasses a range of research efforts aimed at enhancing the performance and adaptability of Transformer-based models for time series analysis. These studies address key challenges such as handling non-stationarity, long-term forecasting, and the limitations of traditional attention mechanisms. For instance, PatchTST introduces an efficient design that segments time series into subseries-level patches, enabling better local semantic retention and reduced computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, Non-stationary Transformers tackle the issue of over-stationarization by incorporating a De-stationary Attention module that recovers intrinsic non-stationary information, leading to significant improvements in forecasting accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. Meanwhile, the LTSF-Linear model challenges the dominance of complex Transformer-based architectures by demonstrating that simple linear models can outperform them on real-world datasets, highlighting the need for re-evaluation of model complexity \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. The TimesNet framework extends temporal variation modeling by transforming 1D time series into 2D tensors, allowing for more effective modeling of intraperiod and interperiod variations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. iTransformer reimagines the Transformer architecture by inverting dimensions to better capture multivariate correlations, achieving state-of-the-art results on real-world datasets \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. Autoformer introduces an Auto-Correlation mechanism that improves efficiency and accuracy in long-term forecasting by leveraging periodicity \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. SparseTSF offers a lightweight solution for long-term forecasting by focusing on cross-period trend prediction, achieving competitive performance with minimal parameters \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00946)\]. Lastly, TSMixer demonstrates that stacking multi-layer perceptrons can effectively extract information across time and features, outperforming specialized models on large-scale benchmarks \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06053)\]. These works collectively highlight the evolving landscape of Transformer-based time series forecasting, emphasizing adaptability, efficiency, and performance across diverse applications.

### <a id='category-time-series-forecasting-with-llms'></a>Time Series Forecasting with LLMs: 8 papers found.
Time series forecasting has seen significant advancements with the integration of large language models (LLMs), particularly in addressing the challenges of accuracy, uncertainty calibration, and adaptability across diverse applications. Recent studies have demonstrated that LLMs, such as GPT-3, LLaMA-2, and GPT-4, can achieve zero-shot or few-shot performance comparable to or exceeding that of specialized time series models \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\]. This is attributed to their ability to naturally represent multimodal distributions and handle repeated patterns, such as seasonal trends, which are common in time series data \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\]. Additionally, LLMs can manage missing data without imputation and incorporate textual side information, enhancing their interpretability and explanatory power \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\]. The development of frameworks like Time-LLM has further enabled the reprogramming of LLMs for time series forecasting by aligning the input modalities through text prototypes and techniques like Prompt-as-Prefix (PaP) \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. These approaches have shown promising results in both supervised and unsupervised settings, often outperforming traditional models such as ARMA-GARCH and gradient-boosting trees \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. Moreover, pre-trained models from NLP and computer vision, when adapted for time series analysis, have also demonstrated competitive performance, especially when fine-tuned on relevant tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11939)\]. The success of these models is further supported by their ability to generalize across different forecasting horizons and temporal granularities, making them versatile tools for real-world applications \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10688)\]. However, challenges such as tokenization biases and uncertainty calibration remain, as seen in cases where models like GPT-4 may underperform due to alignment interventions \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\]. Overall, the integration of LLMs into time series forecasting represents a paradigm shift, offering new opportunities for accurate, interpretable, and adaptable forecasting solutions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07820)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10688)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11939)\].
