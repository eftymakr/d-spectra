## Related Works

Deep-unrolling and plug-and-play (PnP) approaches have become the de-facto standard solvers for single-pixel imaging (SPI) inverse problems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. PnP approaches, a class of iterative algorithms where regularization is implicitly performed by an off-the-shelf deep denoiser, are flexible for varying compression ratios (CRs) but are limited in reconstruction accuracy and speed \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02862)\]. This limitation is due to the reliance on pre-trained denoisers that may not adapt well to different noise levels or measurement conditions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05399)\]. Conversely, unrolling approaches, a class of multi-stage neural networks where a truncated iterative optimization process is transformed into an end-to-end trainable network, typically achieve better accuracy with faster inference but require fine-tuning or even retraining when CR changes \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03264)\]. This makes them less flexible for applications where CR varies frequently \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06625)\].

Several works have explored the integration of deep learning with traditional iterative methods to improve both flexibility and performance. For instance, MoDL introduces a model-based image reconstruction framework with a CNN-based regularization prior, reducing the demand for training data and training time \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. Similarly, Learned D-AMP mimics the behavior of the denoising-based approximate message passing (D-AMP) algorithm, offering a principled approach to compressive image recovery \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03679)\]. These methods demonstrate that unrolling iterative algorithms into deep networks can lead to improved performance while maintaining interpretability \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10557)\].

The concept of algorithm unrolling has also been applied to image restoration tasks. For example, TNRD (Trainable Nonlinear Reaction Diffusion) uses a flexible learning framework based on nonlinear reaction diffusion models, achieving state-of-the-art results in tasks like Gaussian image denoising and super-resolution \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. Similarly, Deep Unfolding Network for Image Super-Resolution leverages both learning-based and model-based methods to create an end-to-end trainable network that can handle different scale factors and noise levels \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02177)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of unrolling to combine the strengths of both paradigms.

Recent works have also focused on improving the flexibility and generalizability of unrolling methods. For example, the paper on Deep Unfolding for Blind Deblurring proposes a network that can handle varying blur kernels and motion patterns while maintaining interpretability \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10428)\]. This aligns with the goal of the current work, which aims to design an efficient deep image restorer (DIR) that can flexibly handle varying CRs with a single model \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03981)\].

### <a id='category-inverse-imaging-deep-learning-methods'></a>Inverse Imaging Deep Learning Methods: 14 papers found.
In the field of inverse imaging deep learning methods, recent research has focused on integrating deep learning with traditional optimization techniques to improve both flexibility and performance in solving inverse problems. Plug-and-play (PnP) methods, which utilize off-the-shelf deep denoisers as implicit regularization, offer flexibility across varying compression ratios but often struggle with reconstruction accuracy and speed \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. Conversely, unrolling approaches, which transform iterative optimization algorithms into end-to-end trainable networks, typically achieve higher accuracy and faster inference but require retraining when the compression ratio changes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02862)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed hybrid frameworks that combine the strengths of both paradigms. For instance, the use of deep neural networks to approximate proximal operators in optimization algorithms, such as those based on half-quadratic splitting (HQS) or alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), has shown promise in achieving flexible and accurate reconstructions without the need for retraining \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05399)\]. These methods leverage learned denoisers as implicit priors while maintaining the structure of traditional optimization algorithms, enabling them to handle varying conditions with a single model \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03264)\]. Additionally, the integration of variable splitting techniques allows discriminative learning models to be plugged into model-based optimization frameworks, enhancing their applicability to a wide range of inverse problems \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06625)\]. The development of unrolled networks, such as LDAMP, which mimic the behavior of iterative algorithms like approximate message passing (AMP), has further demonstrated the potential of combining deep learning with traditional signal processing techniques to achieve both high accuracy and computational efficiency \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1508.02848)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to bridge the gap between model-based optimization and deep learning, leading to more versatile and effective solutions for inverse imaging problems.

Image restoration techniques have seen significant advancements with the integration of deep learning methods, particularly through deep-unrolling and plug-and-play (PnP) approaches \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. These methods leverage neural networks to solve inverse problems in imaging, such as single-pixel imaging (SPI), by either implicitly applying regularization via pre-trained denoisers or explicitly unrolling iterative optimization processes into end-to-end trainable networks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. While PnP approaches offer flexibility across varying compression ratios (CRs), they often suffer from limited reconstruction accuracy and speed \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. In contrast, unrolling methods, such as those based on half-quadratic splitting (HQS) or alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), typically achieve higher accuracy and faster inference but require retraining when CRs change \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. Recent work has focused on combining the strengths of both approaches, leading to hybrid models that maintain flexibility while improving performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. For instance, the proposed deep image restorer (DIR) integrates HQS and ADMM unrolling with a general proximal trajectory (PT) loss function, enabling the network to approximate the proximal operator of an ideal explicit restoration regularizer \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\]. This approach allows the model to handle varying CRs with a single network, outperforming previous CR-specific unrolling networks in both accuracy and speed \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.06001)\].
