## Related Works

Dense retrievers have gained significant traction in information retrieval due to their ability to encode text into dense vector representations that can be efficiently compared using shallow similarity operations such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. These models typically rely on pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) that are fine-tuned using contrastive learning to align queries with relevant documents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. However, recent research has questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers, suggesting that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning may not significantly alter the underlying knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\].

This debate has primarily focused on BERT-based encoders using the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative dense retriever \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. The current study extends this analysis by testing different representation approaches, such as CLS token versus mean pooling, and different backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Additionally, we evaluate performance across multiple datasets, including MSMARCO and Natural Questions, to generalize findings beyond the original BERT-based setup \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

Our results confirm that in DPR tuning, pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance, with fine-tuning primarily adjusting neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. However, this pattern does not hold universally. For example, in mean-pooled models like Contriever, and in decoder-based models like LLaMA, fine-tuning plays a more significant role in shaping retrieval performance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\]. This suggests that the effectiveness of fine-tuning may depend on the architecture and representation strategy employed \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\].

Several prior works have explored the training of dense retrievers. For instance, the Condenser architecture was introduced to improve dense encoders by pre-training with a novel Transformer design that conditions on dense representations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. Similarly, the coCondenser model adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of pre-training strategies in achieving effective dense retrieval \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13602)\].

### <a id='category-dense-retrieval-model-pretraining'></a>Dense Retrieval Model Pretraining: 20 papers found.
Dense retrieval models have seen significant advancements through pre-training strategies that aim to improve their ability to encode text into meaningful representations for efficient retrieval. Research has shown that pre-training plays a crucial role in capturing retrieval knowledge, as it enables models to learn rich semantic representations that can be leveraged for downstream tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. For instance, the Condenser architecture was introduced to pre-train models to condense information into dense vectors, improving retrieval performance without the need for large batch training or extensive data engineering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, the use of contrastive learning has been explored to enhance dense retrievers by aligning relevant documents in the embedding space \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. This approach has been further refined with methods like coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, leading to comparable performance with heavily engineered systems like RocketQA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Additionally, the effectiveness of dense retrieval has been demonstrated in open-domain question answering, where models like Retrieval-Augmented Language Models (REALM) combine pre-training with retrieval mechanisms to improve performance on benchmarks such as Open-QA \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions, which has led to the development of training mechanisms like Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE) to generate more realistic negative samples \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These efforts highlight the ongoing research into optimizing dense retrieval through pre-training and fine-tuning strategies, with a focus on improving efficiency, performance, and generalization across different tasks and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-methods'></a>Contrastive Learning Methods: 2 papers found.
Contrastive learning methods have gained significant attention in the field of self-supervised representation learning, with approaches such as DeCLUTR and SimCLR leading the way in developing effective unsupervised textual and visual representations \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. DeCLUTR introduces a self-supervised objective for learning universal sentence embeddings without requiring labeled data, closing the performance gap between unsupervised and supervised pretraining for universal sentence encoders \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. SimCLR, on the other hand, simplifies contrastive self-supervised learning by eliminating the need for specialized architectures or memory banks, and demonstrates that data augmentation, learnable nonlinear transformations, and larger batch sizes significantly enhance the quality of learned representations \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. These methods highlight the potential of contrastive learning to achieve competitive performance with supervised approaches, particularly in scenarios where labeled data is scarce. Additionally, they emphasize the importance of architectural design and training strategies in maximizing the effectiveness of contrastive learning frameworks. The success of these methods has spurred further research into the role of pre-training versus fine-tuning in dense retrievers, as seen in studies that explore how retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\].
