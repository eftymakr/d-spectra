## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommendation systems, with recent advancements leveraging graph-based models to better capture user-item interactions \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\]. Early approaches such as matrix factorization \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cf6f83fcce274606bf0264c59d1c78a30c9c9d18)\] and neural collaborative filtering (NCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] have laid the groundwork for modern methods that incorporate deep learning. However, these models often rely on simplistic assumptions about user-item relationships and fail to fully exploit the rich structure of interaction graphs \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\].

Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling such interactions, enabling more expressive and accurate representations \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2240f9231920ebfaee305537281d9fe0643779b8)\]. The success of GCNs in recommendation systems has been demonstrated by models like LightGCN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which simplifies the GCN architecture by removing non-linearities and focusing on neighborhood aggregation. This approach has shown significant improvements over earlier models, including Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\], by reducing training complexity and improving performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

Despite these advancements, existing GCN-based methods face challenges in modeling high-order interactions and handling large-scale graphs. For instance, models like NGCF \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] and RippleNet \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] attempt to integrate graph structures and side information, but they often struggle with over-smoothing and scalability issues. Additionally, the use of non-linear activation functions in GCNs can complicate training and degrade performance, especially in sparse interaction scenarios \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. To address these limitations, recent works such as LightGCN \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] and Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\] have proposed simplified architectures that focus on linear aggregation and disentangled representations, respectively.

Moreover, the integration of knowledge graphs into recommendation systems has gained traction, with models like Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\] and RippleNet \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] demonstrating the potential of leveraging semantic relationships between items. These models enhance recommendations by incorporating richer contextual information, although they may still face challenges in efficiently capturing complex interactions \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\].

In addition to GCN-based methods, other approaches such as variational autoencoders \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\] and self-supervised learning \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\] have been explored to improve the robustness and accuracy of recommendation systems. Variational autoencoders provide a probabilistic framework for modeling user preferences, while self-supervised learning techniques aim to enhance node representations by introducing auxiliary tasks that improve the model's ability to generalize \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\].

### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Based Collaborative Filtering: 9 papers found.
Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements with the integration of deep learning techniques, particularly through the use of graph convolutional networks (GCNs) and neural architectures. Early methods relied on matrix factorization and inner products to model user-item interactions, but these approaches were limited in capturing complex collaborative signals \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. More recent work, such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), has introduced frameworks that explicitly incorporate the bipartite graph structure of user-item interactions, enabling the propagation of embeddings to model high-order connectivity and inject collaborative signals directly into the embedding process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, the NCF framework replaces the inner product with a multi-layer perceptron to learn non-linear interactions between users and items, demonstrating the potential of deep learning in collaborative filtering \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. However, GCNs face challenges in large-scale user-item graphs, including training difficulties due to non-linear activations and the over-smoothing problem that limits the modeling of deeper layers \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. To address these issues, LightGCN simplifies GCN by retaining only the essential neighborhood aggregation component, leading to improved performance and scalability \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Additionally, models like Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) focus on disentangling user intents by modeling distributions over intents for each interaction, leading to more interpretable and effective representations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. Furthermore, the application of self-supervised learning in recommendation systems, as seen in Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL), enhances the robustness of GCNs by incorporating auxiliary tasks that improve the accuracy and resilience of node representations \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of graph-based collaborative filtering, driven by the need for more expressive, scalable, and interpretable models.

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-research'></a>Graph Neural Networks Research: 7 papers found.
Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have been widely adopted in collaborative filtering for recommendations, yet they face challenges in capturing complex user-item interactions and leveraging diverse preference patterns \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. Early GCN models, such as those based on spectral graph theory, operate by approximating graph convolutions through localized first-order approximations, enabling efficient computation on graph-structured data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. However, these models often suffer from over-smoothing when multiple layers are applied, limiting their ability to capture high-order relations between nodes \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed variants that incorporate attention mechanisms, such as Graph Attention Networks (GATs), which allow nodes to weigh their neighbors dynamically without requiring costly matrix operations \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]. Additionally, methods like Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) explicitly model high-order relations by propagating embeddings through a node's neighborhood and using attention to prioritize informative neighbors \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. These approaches improve the ability to generalize to unseen nodes and capture nuanced interactions, making them more suitable for large-scale recommendation tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Furthermore, efforts to enhance scalability and efficiency, such as the development of data-efficient GCN algorithms with MapReduce inference, have enabled the training of embeddings on graphs with billions of nodes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. Overall, these advancements highlight the evolving landscape of graph neural networks in recommendation systems, emphasizing the importance of both structural and semantic information in improving recommendation quality \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]\[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\].

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 10 papers found.
The related works in collaborative filtering (CF) and graph neural networks (GNNs) highlight key challenges and solutions in recommendation systems. Traditional CF methods face issues like the cold-start problem and data sparsity, which limit their effectiveness in capturing complex user-item interactions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. To address these, recent approaches leverage graph-based models that integrate both node information and topological structures, enabling more nuanced recommendations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07243)\]. For instance, SpectralCF introduces a spectral convolution operation that captures both proximity and connectivity information in user-item bipartite graphs, improving recommendation accuracy by learning from the spectral domain \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. Similarly, GraphRec proposes a framework that jointly models user-user social graphs and user-item graphs, addressing challenges related to heterogeneous strengths and multi-graph interactions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07243)\]. In contrast, item-based collaborative filtering (ICF) has also seen advancements, with DeepICF modeling higher-order item interactions using nonlinear neural networks to better capture user decision-making processes \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04392)\]. Additionally, methods like Wide & Deep combine memorization and generalization capabilities by integrating wide linear models with deep neural networks, enhancing recommendation performance in sparse scenarios \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07792)\]. These approaches reflect a growing trend toward integrating graph-based learning and deep learning techniques to overcome the limitations of traditional CF methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\].

### <a id='category-recommender-systems-research'></a>Recommender Systems Research: 4 papers found.
To address the sparsity and cold start problem in collaborative filtering, researchers have explored the use of side information such as social networks or item attributes to improve recommendation performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. One notable approach is RippleNet, which integrates knowledge graphs into recommender systems by simulating the propagation of user preferences over knowledge entities \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\]. This method iteratively extends a user's potential interests along links in the knowledge graph, forming a preference distribution for predicting item clicks \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\]. Similarly, matrix completion models have been enhanced by incorporating pairwise relationships between users and items in the form of graphs, along with smoothness priors \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\]. However, these methods often fail to fully exploit local stationarity structures in user/item graphs and require a linear number of parameters relative to the number of users and items \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\]. To overcome these limitations, geometric deep learning on graphs has been proposed, combining graph convolutional neural networks with recurrent neural networks to learn meaningful patterns and non-linear diffusion processes \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\]. Additionally, collaborative deep learning (CDL) has been introduced to jointly perform deep representation learning for content information and collaborative filtering for ratings, addressing the sparsity issue by leveraging non-i.i.d. input \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance collaborative filtering through advanced modeling techniques and the integration of auxiliary information.
