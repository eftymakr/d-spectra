## Related Works

The growing deployment of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in contexts where decisions can have profound social implications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Traditional fairness metrics often rely on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, which fail to account for the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This limitation has led researchers to explore alternative fairness definitions that consider not only prediction correctness but also the confidence with which predictions are made \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\].

Several studies have examined fairness in machine learning, focusing on the tension between minimizing error disparity across different population groups while maintaining calibrated probability estimates \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. For instance, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\] shows that calibration is compatible only with a single error constraint, such as equal false-negative rates across groups. This highlights the inherent trade-offs between fairness and model performance that must be carefully considered.

The concept of fairness through awareness has also been explored, where fairness is defined in terms of treating similar individuals similarly \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This approach has been applied to various domains, including hiring and lending, and has been extended to include fairness through affirmative action, ensuring statistical parity while maintaining utility \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. These frameworks provide a foundation for understanding how fairness can be operationalized in different contexts.

In the context of fairness in classification, the notion of disparate mistreatment has been introduced, which is defined in terms of misclassification rates across different demographic groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. This concept has been used to evaluate the fairness of predictive models, particularly in domains such as recidivism prediction, where the use of biased models can have severe consequences \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\]. The paper \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\] demonstrates that certain fairness criteria cannot all be simultaneously satisfied when recidivism prevalence differs across groups, highlighting the complexity of achieving fairness in practice.

Recent work has also focused on the challenges of auditing fairness in machine learning systems, particularly in the context of subgroup fairness \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. The paper \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\] proposes a framework for auditing subgroup fairness that requires statistical notions of fairness across exponentially many subgroups, which is computationally challenging. This work underscores the need for robust methods to evaluate fairness in complex, real-world scenarios.

In addition to fairness metrics, the paper \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\] discusses the intrinsic bias present in word embeddings and how these biases can be mitigated. This work is relevant to the broader discussion of fairness in language models, as it highlights the importance of addressing biases at the representation level. Similarly, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00133)\] introduces the CrowS-Pairs dataset, which is used to evaluate social biases in masked language models, providing a benchmark for fairness in NLP tasks.

### <a id='category-fairness-in-machine-learning'></a>Fairness in Machine Learning: 12 papers found.
The category "Fairness in Machine Learning" encompasses a broad range of research focused on mitigating bias and ensuring equitable outcomes in automated decision-making systems. Existing work highlights the challenges of defining and measuring fairness, particularly in scenarios where historical data may encode systemic biases \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Researchers have proposed various fairness criteria, such as statistical parity, equalized odds, and individual fairness, each with distinct implications for how fairness is operationalized \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. However, these criteria often conflict, making it difficult to satisfy all simultaneously \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. For instance, achieving equal false positive rates across groups may compromise calibration, and vice versa \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. To address these tensions, some studies advocate for frameworks that integrate causal inference to model fairness in a way that accounts for counterfactual scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Others propose subgroup fairness definitions that require fairness across exponentially many subgroups, though this introduces computational challenges \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. Additionally, there is growing interest in fairness-aware learning algorithms that incorporate fairness constraints directly into the training process, such as methods that balance utility and fairness by modifying the loss function \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. These approaches aim to reduce disparate mistreatment by ensuring that misclassification rates are equitable across social groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. Furthermore, the relationship between fairness and privacy has been explored, with some researchers suggesting that differential privacy techniques could be leveraged to enhance fairness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. Overall, the field emphasizes the need for nuanced, context-sensitive fairness metrics that go beyond simple parity measures to capture the complex interplay between model behavior, data distribution, and societal impact \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\].

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 4 papers found.
The related works section focuses on the growing body of research addressing fairness in AI systems, particularly in the context of machine learning and deep learning applications. Researchers have increasingly recognized the importance of fairness in AI, especially as these systems are deployed in sensitive domains where decisions can have significant impacts on individuals and communities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This has led to the development of various fairness definitions and metrics aimed at mitigating biases in AI outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. However, traditional fairness measures often fail to account for more nuanced aspects of bias, such as robustness bias, where certain subgroups may be disproportionately affected by adversarial attacks or model vulnerabilities \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. This issue is further compounded by the limitations of commonly used datasets, such as the UCI Adult dataset, which, while widely adopted, may not fully represent real-world complexities or capture the full scope of fairness challenges \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. As a result, there is a need for more comprehensive and diverse datasets that can better reflect the real-world conditions under which AI systems operate \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. Additionally, recent work has highlighted the importance of considering model uncertainty in fairness evaluations, as conventional metrics often overlook the implicit biases that arise from differences in model confidence across groups \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. These findings underscore the ongoing efforts to refine fairness metrics and improve the accountability of AI systems in practical settings.
