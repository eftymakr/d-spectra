## Related Works

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have emerged as a promising direction in artificial intelligence, enabling models to understand and generate content across multiple modalities, including text, images, and video. Early efforts in this field focused on integrating vision and language, often using pre-trained models for either vision or language and then combining them in a modular fashion \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. However, these approaches typically lacked the ability to perform in-context learning and lacked robustness in handling complex, real-world tasks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\].

Recent advances have introduced models that can perform multimodal reasoning and generate responses grounded in both visual and textual information. For instance, Video-ChatGPT combines a video-adapted visual encoder with a large language model (LLM) to enable detailed conversations about videos, using a large-scale dataset of 100,000 video-instruction pairs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\]. Similarly, Video-LLaMA extends this idea by integrating both visual and auditory signals, addressing challenges such as temporal coherence and cross-modal alignment through specialized components like the Video Q-former and Audio Q-former \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of MLLMs to understand and generate content that is both visually and audibly grounded \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\].

Another important development is the introduction of models that can perform grounding, i.e., aligning text with specific regions in images or videos. Kosmos-2, for example, introduces a novel way to represent referential expressions using Markdown links, enabling models to ground text to visual content through a large-scale dataset called GrIT \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14824)\]. This capability is crucial for tasks such as referring expression comprehension and phrase grounding, which require precise alignment between textual descriptions and visual elements \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14824)\].

The ability to perform instruction-following and in-context learning has also been a focus of recent research. Flamingo, for example, is a visual language model that enables few-shot learning by training on large-scale multimodal web corpora containing interleaved text and images \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. This approach allows models to adapt to new tasks with minimal supervision, which is a significant advantage in real-world applications \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. Similarly, LLaVA leverages instruction tuning using machine-generated instruction-following data to improve zero-shot performance on new tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\].

To evaluate the performance of MLLMs, researchers have introduced comprehensive benchmarks such as MME, which measures both perception and cognition abilities across 14 subtasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\]. ### <a id='category-multimodal-language-models-research'></a>Multimodal Language Models Research: 2 papers found.
Recent advancements in multimodal language models (MLLMs) have focused on integrating diverse modalities to enhance understanding and generation of nonverbal cues, such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language, alongside text \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. These models leverage large language models (LLMs) as a foundation, enabling emergent capabilities like story generation from images and math reasoning without optical character recognition \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. Researchers have also explored extending MLLMs to support more modalities, languages, and scenarios, with techniques such as multimodal in-context learning and chain of thought reasoning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. Notably, embodied language models have been developed to incorporate real-world sensor data, allowing for tasks like robotic manipulation planning and visual question answering by integrating visual, textual, and continuous state estimation inputs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03378)\]. These models demonstrate positive transfer across domains, improving performance on tasks like OK-VQA while retaining generalist language capabilities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.03378)\]. Such developments highlight the growing emphasis on creating multimodal systems that can effectively bridge the gap between human and machine understanding in complex, real-world interactions.

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 3 papers found.
Video-LLaMA \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\] and Qwen-VL \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12966)\] represent significant advancements in multimodal large language models (LLMs) by integrating visual and auditory information with textual understanding. Video-LLaMA specifically addresses the challenges of capturing temporal changes in visual scenes and integrating audio-visual signals, using a Video Q-former and an Audio Q-former to align with LLMs' embedding space \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\]. Similarly, Qwen-VL extends LLMs with visual perception through a visual receptor, input-output interface, and a 3-stage training pipeline, enabling tasks beyond image description and question-answering, such as visual grounding and text-reading \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12966)\]. These models highlight the growing focus on multimodal understanding, where models are trained on large-scale datasets to better comprehend and generate content grounded in both visual and auditory cues. The development of such frameworks reflects a broader trend in LLM research toward creating more immersive and context-aware conversational agents \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12966)\].
