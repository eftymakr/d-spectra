## Related Works

Deep sequential recommendation models have seen significant advancements, with various approaches addressing the challenges of modeling user behavior sequences. Early methods such as Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) were widely used to capture sequential patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. MCs assume that a user's next action is based on their last few actions, while RNNs allow for longer-term semantics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, MC-based methods often struggle with dense datasets, while RNNs can be computationally expensive \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, attention-based models such as SASRec were proposed, which combine the efficiency of MCs with the flexibility of RNNs by using self-attention to identify relevant items in a user's history \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. This approach allows for capturing long-term semantics while maintaining computational efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\].

Recent works have also explored self-supervised learning for sequential recommendation, such as S3-Rec, which leverages mutual information maximization to enhance data representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. This method introduces auxiliary objectives to learn correlations between items, attributes, and subsequences, improving performance, especially with limited training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. Similarly, BERT4Rec extends the bidirectional self-attention mechanism from the Transformer model to sequential recommendation, allowing for bidirectional context modeling and improving recommendation accuracy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. This approach avoids information leakage by using the Cloze objective to predict masked items based on both left and right contexts \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\].

Time-aware models such as TiSASRec have also been developed to explicitly model time intervals between interactions, improving the accuracy of next-item predictions by considering both the order and timing of user actions \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. Feature-level models like FDSA further enhance sequential recommendation by considering both item-level and feature-level transitions, capturing more comprehensive sequential patterns \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/63555b4f363ee72dd8d49d8deca68760542c0cd2)\]. These methods demonstrate the importance of modeling both item and feature-level interactions for better recommendation performance.

Session-based recommendation methods have also evolved, with models such as NARM incorporating attention mechanisms to capture user intent and session context \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\]. These models use hybrid encoders to represent sessions and compute item scores based on a bi-linear matching scheme \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04725)\]. Additionally, graph-based models such as SR-GNN have been introduced to capture complex item transitions by modeling sessions as graph-structured data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00855)\].

To address the limitations of traditional models, recent works have focused on parameter-efficient transfer learning, such as PeterRec, which allows for efficient adaptation of pre-trained models to new tasks without retraining the entire network \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2ca1abd015c72e7c04d63fa653b0a27d9592fc02)\]. Recent advances in sequential recommendation models have focused on capturing both short-term sequential patterns and long-term user preferences, with various approaches leveraging recurrent neural networks (RNNs), self-attention mechanisms, and convolutional architectures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Markov Chains (MCs) and RNNs have been widely used to model sequential dynamics, where MCs rely on recent actions to predict the next item, while RNNs capture longer-term dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, RNNs often struggle with computational efficiency and training complexity, especially in dense datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, attention-based models such as SASRec and BERT4Rec have been proposed, combining the efficiency of MCs with the flexibility of RNNs by using self-attention to selectively focus on relevant items in the user’s history \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. Additionally, models like Caser and TiSASRec have introduced convolutional and time-aware mechanisms to better capture sequential patterns and temporal dynamics \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]\[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. ### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-methods'></a>Sequential Recommendation Methods: 5 papers found.
Recent advancements in sequential recommendation have focused on improving the modeling of user behavior through deep learning techniques, with various approaches addressing challenges such as data sparsity, noise in logged data, and the need for transferability across domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. For instance, S3-Rec introduces self-supervised learning to enhance data representations by capturing correlations among item attributes, subsequences, and sequences through mutual information maximization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. Similarly, Feature-level Deeper Self-Attention Network (FDSA) emphasizes the importance of modeling feature-level transitions in addition to item-level sequences, leading to more comprehensive sequential pattern extraction \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/63555b4f363ee72dd8d49d8deca68760542c0cd2)\]. Meanwhile, UniSRec proposes a universal sequence representation learning approach that leverages item descriptions to enable transferability across recommendation scenarios, demonstrating effectiveness in cross-platform settings \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]. Additionally, FMLP-Rec integrates filtering algorithms inspired by signal processing to mitigate noise in logged data, achieving superior performance over Transformer-based models through an all-MLP architecture \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13556)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to improve sequential recommendation by addressing key limitations such as sparsity, noise, and domain adaptability.

[Snippet]: «Inductive transfer learning has had a big impact on computer vision and NLP domains but has not been used in the area of recommender systems. Even though there has been a large body of research on generating recommendations based on modeling user-item interaction sequences, few of them attempt to represent and transfer these models for serving downstream tasks where only limited data exists. In this paper, we delve on the task of effectively learning a single user representation that can be applied to a diversity of tasks, from cross-domain recommendations to user profile predictions. Fine-tuning a large pre-trained network and adapting it to downstream tasks is an effective way to solve such tasks. However, fine-t tuning is parameter inefficient considering that an entire model needs to be re-trained for every new task. To overcome this issue, we develop a parameter-efficient transfer learning architecture, termed as PeterRec, which can be configured on-the-fly to various downstream tasks. Specifically, PeterRec allows the pre-trained parameters to remain unaltered during fine-tuning by injecting a series of re-learned neural networks, which are small but as expressive as learning the entire network. We perform extensive experimental ablation to show the effectiveness of the learned user representation in five downstream tasks. Moreover, we show that PeterRec performs efficient transfer learning in multiple domains, where it achieves comparable or sometimes better performance relative to fine-tuning the entire model parameters. Codes and datasets are available at https://github.com/fajieyuan/sigir2020_peterrec.»
