## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the domain of Web search. These models often rely on large-scale labeled query-document pairs for training, which are typically expensive to obtain \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09268)\]. However, the availability of Web document collections like ClueWeb22 has enabled the use of synthetic queries generated by large language models as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, synthetic training queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal downstream retrieval performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Existing methods typically address this issue by filtering out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach, while effective, introduces additional complexity and computational overhead. In contrast, our framework leverages Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to integrate ranking signals directly into the query generation process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. By doing so, we aim to optimize the model towards generating high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

DPO has been shown to be a stable, performant, and computationally lightweight method for aligning large language models with human preferences \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. It eliminates the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning or significant hyperparameter tuning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. This is particularly advantageous in the context of query generation, where the goal is to produce high-quality synthetic queries that can be used to train retrieval models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align models with human preferences \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. However, RLHF is a complex and often unstable procedure, requiring the fitting of a reward model and subsequent fine-tuning using reinforcement learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, DPO simplifies this process by introducing a new parameterization of the reward model that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. This results in a simpler implementation and training process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\].

Additionally, the use of pairwise preferences in training has been explored in the context of learning from human preferences \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. This approach bypasses the need for pointwise rewards and allows for direct optimization of the policy based on pairwise comparisons \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. This is particularly relevant in the context of query generation, where the goal is to produce queries that are preferred over others \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

In summary, the use of DPO in the query generation process offers a promising approach to improving the quality of synthetic queries used in training retrieval models. This approach not only simplifies the training process but also leads to better downstream performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

### <a id='category-reinforcement-learning-for-nlp'></a>Reinforcement Learning for NLP: 2 papers found.
The category "Reinforcement Learning for NLP" encompasses methods that leverage reinforcement learning to improve model alignment with human preferences and enhance retrieval performance. Existing approaches, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), involve training a reward model to reflect human preferences and then fine-tuning the language model to maximize this reward, often with complex and unstable procedures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, recent work introduces Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which simplifies this process by parameterizing the reward model in a way that allows for closed-form policy extraction, eliminating the need for sampling during fine-tuning and reducing computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach has shown promise in aligning large language models with human preferences more effectively than traditional RLHF methods, particularly in controlling sentiment and improving response quality in tasks like summarization and dialogue \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Additionally, in the realm of information retrieval, models that combine local and distributed representations have been proposed to improve document ranking by leveraging both exact term matches and semantic similarity \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08136)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in using reinforcement learning and hybrid representation strategies to enhance the performance and controllability of NLP models.

### <a id='category-reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback'></a>Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback: 7 papers found.
The category "Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at aligning machine learning models with human preferences through direct optimization of reward functions derived from human feedback. This includes methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)\], which simplifies the implementation of trust region policy optimization while improving sample efficiency. Additionally, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\] has emerged as a method that bypasses the need for explicit reward modeling by directly learning from pairwise preferences, leading to significant improvements in tasks such as summarization and reasoning. However, DPO still relies on the assumption that pairwise preferences can be substituted with pointwise rewards \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. To address this, $\Psi$PO \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\] was introduced as a general objective that operates directly on pairwise preferences, thus avoiding both approximations. Furthermore, DPO-Positive (DPOP) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\] was developed to mitigate issues with the standard DPO loss, which can reduce the likelihood of preferred examples, thereby improving performance across various datasets. These methods are often applied in the context of fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) to align with human expectations, using techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\], which has been shown to enhance performance on a wide range of NLP tasks. The integration of human feedback into the training process, either through explicit reward modeling or direct preference optimization, has become a critical area of research, especially as models grow in complexity and scale \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741)\].

### <a id='category-dense-text-retrieval-techniques'></a>Dense Text Retrieval Techniques: 6 papers found.
Document 1 discusses the importance of ranking in information retrieval and highlights the limitations of lexical matching signals, which can lead to vocabulary mismatch issues. It introduces Dense Retrieval (DR) models as a more effective approach, emphasizing the role of representation learning in improving ranking performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. The document also points out that while DR models have shown promise, their performance is heavily dependent on training example sampling strategies, which are often inefficient and lack theoretical justification. To address these challenges, the authors propose two training strategies: STAR, which enhances training stability by introducing random negatives, and ADORE, which replaces static hard negative sampling with a dynamic method to directly optimize ranking performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\].

Document 2 provides an overview of text ranking using transformer-based models like BERT, highlighting their effectiveness across various domains and tasks. It categorizes existing techniques into two groups: transformer models that perform reranking in multi-stage architectures and learned dense representations that perform ranking directly. This distinction is crucial for understanding the evolution of dense retrieval methods and their practical deployment in real-world applications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06467)\].

Document 3 introduces the MS MARCO dataset, which is designed to overcome the limitations of previous datasets by using real user queries and web documents. This dataset is essential for evaluating retrieval models, as it provides a realistic and comprehensive benchmark for assessing performance in reading comprehension and question-answering tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09268)\].

Document 4 presents a re-implementation of BERT for query-based passage re-ranking, achieving state-of-the-art results on the TREC-CAR dataset and outperforming previous methods on the MS MARCO passage retrieval task. This work underscores the potential of BERT in dense retrieval settings and highlights the importance of high-quality training data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04085)\].

Document 5 introduces ANCE, a training mechanism that addresses the mismatch between training and testing data distributions by constructing negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index. This approach improves the effectiveness of dense retrieval models by ensuring that negative instances are more representative of irrelevant documents during testing \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

Document 6 explores the use of contextualized language models like ELMo and BERT in ad-hoc document ranking, demonstrating that these models can enhance the performance of neural ranking architectures. The paper also addresses practical challenges, such as input length constraints and runtime performance, which are important considerations when deploying these models in real-world applications \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07094)\].

### <a id='category-neural-methods-for-document-retrieval'></a>Neural Methods for Document Retrieval: 3 papers found.
The category "Neural Methods for Document Retrieval" encompasses several advanced approaches aimed at improving the efficiency and effectiveness of document retrieval systems. These methods often leverage deep learning architectures to unify the indexing and retrieval processes, addressing traditional limitations such as the difficulty of optimizing indexes for retrieval targets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. For instance, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) introduces a sequence-to-sequence network that generates relevant document identifiers directly for a query, supported by a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder and tailored techniques like semantic document identifiers and consistency-based regularization \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Similarly, K-NRM employs a kernel-based neural model that incorporates word embeddings and a kernel-pooling technique to extract multi-level soft match features, enhancing ranking performance through an end-to-end training approach \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06613)\]. The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) represents another paradigm shift, using a single transformer model for both indexing and retrieval, but faces challenges due to data distribution mismatches between indexing and retrieval phases \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address this, DSI-QG introduces a framework that generates and re-ranks potential queries during indexing, aligning the indexed documents with shorter queries and improving cross-lingual retrieval performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. These methods collectively highlight the trend toward end-to-end learning and the integration of ranking signals into the query generation process, which is also a key aspect of the paper's proposed framework that leverages Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to enhance the quality of synthetic training queries \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].
