## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, particularly through the development of end-to-end models that generate high-quality speech directly from text \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\]. These models, such as Tacotron \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\], FastSpeech \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\], and Fastpitch \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\], have enabled parallel processing and faster inference compared to autoregressive models. However, these systems often lack fine-grained control over prosody, which is essential for generating expressive and contextually appropriate speech \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\]. 

Prosody control in TTS has been a major research focus, with several approaches focusing on explicit modeling of prosodic features such as pitch, energy, and duration \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. For example, the work by \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\] proposes a model that generates speech conditioned on these acoustic correlates, allowing for flexible and interpretable control over prosody. This approach enables the generation of multiple distinct renditions of a text by modifying the acoustic features during inference. However, these methods often require additional training or specialized modules, limiting their applicability in post-hoc scenarios \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\].

Traditional approaches to mispronunciation correction rely on grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) dictionaries, which are not practical in low-resource settings due to the lack of annotated data \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\]. Recent work has explored alternative methods, such as using neural prosody generators to model prosody from text \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. These models, however, often fail to capture the full variability of natural speech, resulting in flat and less expressive output \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04233)\].

To address these limitations, several studies have proposed controllable TTS systems that allow for post-hoc adjustments of prosody and pronunciation. For example, the work by \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\] introduces "global style tokens" (GSTs), which enable the modeling of acoustic expressiveness without explicit labels. These embeddings can be used to control synthesis in novel ways, such as varying speed and speaking style independently of the text content \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\]. Similarly, \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03388)\] proposes a user-controllable, context-aware neural prosody generator that allows for the modification of prosody constraints during inference, enhancing the flexibility of TTS systems.

Another line of research focuses on the use of variational autoencoders (VAEs) to model the distribution of prosodic features, enabling the generation of varied intonation while maintaining naturalness \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04233)\]. This approach allows for the exploration of the tails of the prior distribution, leading to more idiosyncratic and varied renditions of speech

### <a id='category-end-to-end-speech-synthesis-models'></a>End-to-End Speech Synthesis Models: 9 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) have significantly improved speech naturalness, increasing the demand for precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Existing approaches for prosody manipulation often depend on specialized modules or additional training, limiting their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Similarly, traditional mispronunciation correction relies on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making it less practical in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed various end-to-end speech synthesis models that aim to simplify the training pipeline and improve the quality of synthesized speech. For instance, FastSpeech introduces a non-autoregressive model that uses a teacher-student distillation pipeline to improve the one-to-many mapping problem in TTS, although it suffers from issues related to duration prediction and information loss \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. FastSpeech 2 further improves upon this by directly training the model with ground-truth targets and incorporating more variation information such as pitch and energy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. FastPitch, another model, is fully-parallel and conditions on fundamental frequency contours, allowing for more expressive and engaging speech generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06873)\]. Additionally, models like Tacotron and Tacotron 2 have been developed to synthesize speech directly from text using a sequence-to-sequence framework, with Tacotron 2 achieving a mean opinion score comparable to professionally recorded speech \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05884)\]. These models often incorporate hierarchical latent variables or variational autoencoder frameworks to enable fine-grained control over prosody and other attributes \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\]. Moreover, recent work has introduced global style tokens (GSTs) to enable style transfer and control over speech synthesis without requiring additional training \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the flexibility and quality of TTS systems while reducing the complexity of their training and inference processes.

### <a id='category-text-to-speech-synthesis-models'></a>Text to Speech Synthesis Models: 5 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have focused on improving speech naturalness and enabling more flexible prosody control \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Modern sequence-to-sequence neural TTS systems generate high-quality speech but often struggle with explicit prosody control, as the prosody is implicitly encoded within the acoustic features and difficult to modify \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. This challenge is compounded when no prosodic labeling is available in the training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. To address this, researchers have explored methods for unsupervised speaking style learning and generation, though human inspection remains necessary for interpretation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Parallel end-to-end TTS models have also been developed to generate more natural-sounding audio than two-stage models, leveraging variational inference, normalizing flows, and adversarial training to improve expressive power and enable diverse speech synthesis \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\]. Additionally, flow-based generative models like Glow-TTS have been introduced to enable fast, controllable speech synthesis without requiring external aligners, achieving speed improvements over autoregressive models like Tacotron 2 \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance TTS systems with better prosody control and flexibility, particularly in post-hoc adjustments \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06103)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\].

These methods highlight the growing interest in making TTS systems more adaptable and controllable, aligning with the broader goal of editable speech synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\].

[Snippet]: «Unlike human speakers, typical text-to-speech (TTS) systems are unable to produce multiple distinct renditions of a given sentence. This has previously been addressed by adding explicit external control. In contrast, generative models are able to capture a distribution over multiple renditions and thus produce varied renditions using sampling. Typical neural TTS models learn the average of the data because they minimise mean squared error. In the context of prosody, taking the average produces flatter, more boring speech: an "average prosody". A generative model that can synthesise multiple prosodies will, by design, not model average prosody. We use variational autoencoders (VAEs) which explicitly place the most "average" data close to the mean of the Gaussian prior. We propose that by moving towards the tails of the prior distribution, the model will transition towards generating more idiosyncratic, varied renditions. Focusing here on intonation, we investigate the trade-off between naturalness and intonation variation and find that typical acoustic models can either be natural, or varied, but not both. However, sampling from the tails of the VAE prior produces much more varied intonation than the traditional approaches, whilst maintaining the same level of naturalness.»
