## Related Works

Protein dynamics and conformational sampling are essential for understanding biological function and have traditionally relied on molecular dynamics (MD) simulations \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. However, these simulations are computationally expensive and often intractable for large systems \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling, particularly denoising diffusion models, have enabled efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These models have shown promise in generating diverse and high-quality protein conformations, but integrating physical supervision remains a challenge \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to improve the physical plausibility of generated protein structures. For instance, idpGAN uses a generative adversarial network (GAN) trained on coarse-grained simulations of intrinsically disordered peptides to predict sequence-dependent conformational ensembles \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Similarly, EigenFold introduces a diffusion-based generative model that samples a distribution of structures from a given protein sequence, achieving a median TMScore of 0.84 \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of diffusion-based approaches in capturing conformational ensembles and flexibility \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\].

AlphaFold and its variants, such as AlphaFlow and ESMFlow, have also contributed significantly to the field. AlphaFold has achieved atomic accuracy in predicting protein structures, and AlphaFlow extends this capability to generate conformational ensembles by fine-tuning single-state predictors under a custom flow matching framework \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. These models provide a superior combination of precision and diversity compared to traditional methods \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\].

The development of generative models that incorporate physical constraints has also been explored. Boltzmann generators use deep learning and statistical mechanics to generate unbiased equilibrium samples of many-body systems, including proteins \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. These models learn a coordinate transformation from a system’s configurations to a latent space representation, enabling efficient sampling of equilibrium states \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. Similarly, ConfDiff proposes a force-guided SE(3) diffusion model for protein conformation generation, which incorporates physical prior knowledge to guide the generation process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\].

Recent work on denoising diffusion probabilistic models has also focused on improving the physical plausibility of generated structures. For example, the SE(3) diffusion model and FrameDiff have been developed to generate protein backbones while preserving group invariance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02277)\]. These models leverage the inherent symmetries of protein structures to improve the quality of generated conformations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02277)\].

### <a id='category-protein-structure-generation-models'></a>Protein Structure Generation Models: 7 papers found.
Protein structure generation models have seen significant advancements through the integration of machine learning with traditional computational methods. These models aim to overcome the limitations of molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which are computationally expensive and often struggle with rare event sampling \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Recent approaches, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) and flow-based models, have been developed to generate physically realistic conformational ensembles of proteins with reduced computational cost \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. For instance, idpGAN, a GAN-based model, has demonstrated the ability to predict sequence-dependent ensembles for peptides not present in the training set, showcasing transferability beyond limited data \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Similarly, AlphaFlow and ESMFlow utilize fine-tuned single-state predictors like AlphaFold and ESMFold within a flow matching framework to enhance precision and diversity in conformational sampling \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Additionally, Boltzmann generators leverage invertible neural networks to learn coordinate transformations, enabling one-shot sampling of equilibrium states by transforming simple distributions into complex ones \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. These models are trained on energy functions of many-body systems, allowing them to generate unbiased samples from equilibrium states \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. Furthermore, diffusion models such as ConfDiff incorporate physical guidance through force-guided networks, improving the fidelity and diversity of generated protein conformations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. These developments highlight the growing role of deep learning in efficiently capturing protein dynamics and conformational landscapes, offering promising avenues for structural biology and drug discovery \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]\[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\].

### <a id='category-protein-structure-prediction-models'></a>Protein Structure Prediction Models: 9 papers found.
Protein structure prediction has seen significant advancements with the development of deep learning models that leverage evolutionary information and physical principles to predict high-resolution structures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Models like AlphaFold \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\] and OmegaFold \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/867d80c8779e1d301a5fc6e267e263f7e4c4c5c7)\] have demonstrated the ability to achieve atomic accuracy, even in the absence of homologous structures, by incorporating multi-sequence alignments and geometric insights. These models have been validated on benchmark datasets such as CASP14, where they outperformed previous methods \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\]. Additionally, diffusion-based frameworks such as EigenFold \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\] and Genie \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12485)\] have been introduced to model conformational ensembles and generate diverse protein structures by learning from large-scale crystallographic data. These models address the limitations of traditional molecular dynamics simulations, which are computationally expensive and often intractable \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. Recent approaches also focus on integrating physical supervision into generative models, as seen in the Energy-based Alignment (EBA) method, which aligns generative models with feedback from physical models to improve the physical plausibility of generated structures \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Furthermore, methods like Str2Str \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\] and Distributional Graphormer (DiG) \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\] have been proposed to enable efficient conformation sampling and distributional modeling of molecular systems, offering faster and more accurate predictions compared to conventional simulation techniques. These developments highlight the growing trend of combining deep learning with physical principles to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of protein structure prediction.

### <a id='category-generative-models-for-protein-structures'></a>Generative Models for Protein Structures: 5 papers found.
Recent advances in generative modeling have significantly impacted the field of protein structure design, with several studies focusing on developing models that can generate novel, functional protein structures. These models often leverage diffusion processes and neural architectures tailored for molecular systems, enabling the synthesis of three-dimensional protein structures from predicted inter-residue geometries \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. One such model, Chroma, employs a diffusion process that respects the conformational statistics of polymer ensembles, allowing for the direct sampling of novel protein structures and sequences while conditioning on desired properties and functions \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. Similarly, a diffusion-based generative model has been introduced to generate protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state towards a stable folded structure, mirroring the natural folding process \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. This approach not only captures the complexity of naturally occurring proteins but also reduces the need for complex equivariant networks due to the inherent shift and rotational invariance of the backbone representation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. Another study addresses the challenge of diffusion on SE(3), the space of orientation-preserving rigid motions in 3D, by developing a framework called FrameDiff that enables the generation of designable monomers up to 500 amino acids without relying on pretrained protein structure prediction networks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02277)\]. Additionally, a generative model that operates at significantly larger scales than previous approaches has been introduced, capable of producing full-atom backbone configurations along with sequence and side-chain predictions based on a compact specification of protein topology \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\]. These models collectively highlight the growing trend of integrating physical supervision into data-driven approaches for protein structure generation, aiming to enhance the physical plausibility of generated structures and improve their utility in applications such as structural biology and drug discovery.

### <a id='category-molecular-dynamics-and-deep-learning'></a>Molecular Dynamics and Deep Learning: 3 papers found.
Recent advances in deep learning have significantly enhanced the prediction of molecular structures and their dynamic behavior, addressing limitations in traditional molecular dynamics (MD) simulations that are computationally expensive and intractable for large systems \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1446b534858fc3fc6d9406920450b5c3ca87ee13)\]. Researchers have developed deep learning frameworks, such as Distributional Graphormer (DiG), which leverage neural networks to predict equilibrium distributions of molecular systems by transforming initial distributions toward equilibrium, conditioned on molecular descriptors like chemical graphs or protein sequences \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1446b534858fc3fc6d9406920450b5c3ca87ee13)\]. This approach enables efficient generation of diverse conformations and estimation of state densities, offering substantial improvements over conventional methods \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/1446b534858fc3fc6d9406920450b5c3ca87ee13)\]. Similarly, score-based generative models have been used to learn coarse-grained (CG) force fields without requiring force inputs during training, allowing for the simulation of CG molecular dynamics with performance comparable to all-atom simulations \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00600)\]. Additionally, generative models based on the flow-matching paradigm over $3\mathrm(D)$ rigid motions (i.e., the group $\text(SE)(3)$) have been introduced to accurately model protein backbones, offering stability and efficiency over diffusion-based approaches \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02391)\]. These developments highlight the growing integration of deep learning with physical principles to improve the accuracy and efficiency of molecular simulations, supporting applications in protein design, drug discovery, and structural biology.
