## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a prominent approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These methods aim to mitigate the degradation of performance on previously learned tasks when new tasks are introduced. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is widely used, its optimality remains an open question \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. 

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of rehearsal strategies in CL. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\] examined the use of experience replay buffers for all past events, combining on- and off-policy learning, and found that this approach could reduce catastrophic forgetting in both Atari and DMLab domains. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\] proposed Deep Generative Replay, a framework inspired by the hippocampus's role in short-term memory, which enables the sampling and interleaving of past and new task data. This method has shown promising results in sequential learning settings involving image classification tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\].

The concept of sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data sequentially, has also been explored. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\] introduced a task-based hard attention mechanism that preserves previous tasks' information without affecting the current task's learning. This approach effectively reduces catastrophic forgetting by up to 80% \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. Additionally, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\] investigated the use of tiny episodic memories for continual learning and found that a simple baseline, which jointly trains on both current and memory examples, outperformed state-of-the-art CL approaches \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\].

In the context of online continual learning, \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\] proposed a method that retrieves the most interfered samples for replay, leading to improved performance and reduced forgetting. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where data is streamed continuously and cannot be revisited \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] introduced ExStream, a memory-efficient rehearsal algorithm that performs well with far less memory and computation compared to full rehearsal \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\].

### <a id='category-continual-learning-techniques'></a>Continual Learning Techniques: 14 papers found.
Continual learning remains a critical challenge in machine learning, as neural networks often suffer from catastrophic forgetting when learning sequentially from multiple tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. This phenomenon occurs when training on new tasks causes the model to lose previously acquired knowledge \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. To address this, various methods have been proposed, such as experience replay, where past data is stored and reused during training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\], and generative replay, which uses a generative model to sample previous data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. These approaches aim to mitigate forgetting by allowing the model to revisit past experiences, either through explicit memory storage or by generating synthetic data \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. However, storing all past data is often infeasible due to memory constraints, prompting research into more efficient strategies \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. For example, methods like Learning without Forgetting enable models to adapt to new tasks without requiring access to old training data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Additionally, task-based attention mechanisms have been introduced to selectively protect important weights for previous tasks while learning new ones \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. Recent work has also explored hybrid approaches, such as sequential rehearsal, where models revisit past tasks after learning new ones, which can be more effective when tasks are dissimilar \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These strategies highlight the ongoing efforts to develop scalable and efficient continual learning frameworks that balance memory usage, computational cost, and performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\].

### <a id='category-continual-learning-methods'></a>Continual Learning Methods: 7 papers found.
Continual learning (CL) aims to enable models to learn sequentially from a stream of tasks without catastrophic forgetting, a phenomenon where learning new tasks erases knowledge of previous ones \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10368)\]. Various approaches have been proposed to address this challenge, including methods that leverage episodic memory, such as reservoir sampling, which has shown effectiveness in maintaining performance across tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\]. Additionally, theoretical analyses under overparameterized linear models have provided insights into the factors influencing forgetting and generalization, such as task similarity, overparameterization, and task ordering \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\]. These findings have been validated in practical settings using deep neural networks, demonstrating that insights from theoretical models can inform better algorithm designs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\]. Furthermore, methods like Meta-Experience Replay (MER) have been introduced to optimize the trade-off between transfer and interference by enforcing gradient alignment across examples, leading to improved performance in non-stationary environments \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11910)\]. Inspired by human memory, architectures with dual memory systems have also been developed to emulate the hippocampus and neocortex, enabling generative replay of past experiences to maintain long-term memory \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10368)\]. Theoretical and empirical studies have further explored the interplay between task similarity at different levels (e.g., features and readouts) and how this affects forgetting and transfer \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04384)\]. Recent work has also introduced objectives like OML, which directly minimizes catastrophic interference by learning representations that support future learning and robustness to forgetting \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12588)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve continual learning by addressing both theoretical and practical challenges.
