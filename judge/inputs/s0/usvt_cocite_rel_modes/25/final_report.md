## Related Works

The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) into large language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning capabilities has gained significant attention in recent years. While KGs provide structured, semantically rich representations that are ideal for reasoning tasks, most existing claim verification methods rely on unstructured text corpora, limiting their ability to effectively leverage KGs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\]. This limitation is particularly evident in tasks requiring multi-step reasoning and modular pipelines, where LLMs often struggle without adaptation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of KGs in conjunction with LLMs. For instance, KEPLER \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13168)\] proposes a unified model that integrates factual knowledge from KGs into pre-trained language models, enhancing both knowledge embedding and language representation. Similarly, K-BERT \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.07606)\] incorporates knowledge graphs into BERT-based models to improve domain-specific tasks, demonstrating that domain knowledge can significantly enhance model performance. However, these approaches often require extensive pre-training or domain-specific adaptation, which limits their generalizability \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\].

In the realm of knowledge-intensive NLP tasks, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising approach. RAG combines pre-trained language models with external knowledge sources, such as Wikipedia, to improve the accuracy and credibility of generated responses \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\]. This paradigm has been extended to incorporate KGs, as seen in the work of MindMap \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\], which leverages KGs to enhance LLMs' inference and transparency. The method enables LLMs to comprehend KG inputs and infer using both implicit and external knowledge, leading to significant improvements in medical question-answering tasks.

Another notable approach is the use of graph neural networks (GNNs) to enhance LLMs' ability to reason over structured data. For example, StructGPT \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09645)\] introduces an iterative reading-then-reasoning approach to solve question-answering tasks based on structured data. This method constructs specialized functions to collect relevant evidence from structured data and allows LLMs to concentrate on reasoning tasks, significantly improving performance on various datasets.

Moreover, the integration of KGs into LLMs has also been explored in the context of fact-checking and reasoning. Program-Guided Fact-Checking (ProgramFC) \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12744)\] decomposes complex claims into sub-tasks that can be solved using specialized functions, leveraging the in-context learning ability of LLMs. This approach not only improves accuracy but also provides clear explanations of the reasoning process, enhancing transparency.

### <a id='category-knowledge-graph-and-language-models'></a>Knowledge Graph and Language Models: 8 papers found.
The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) with large language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning and factual verification has gained significant attention, with several approaches aiming to bridge the gap between structured knowledge and unstructured text \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\]. Early works, such as ERNIE \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07129)\], proposed combining pre-trained language models with KGs to improve knowledge-driven tasks by leveraging both lexical and structured information. Similarly, K-BERT \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.07606)\] introduced a method to inject domain-specific knowledge from KGs into LLMs, enabling better performance in specialized domains like finance and medicine. More recently, KEPLER \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\] proposed a unified model that jointly optimizes knowledge embedding and language representation, enhancing both text understanding and KG link prediction. The challenge of incorporating KGs into LLMs has also been addressed through methods like QA-GNN \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06378)\], which uses relevance scoring and joint reasoning over QA contexts and KGs to improve question-answering tasks. Additionally, RoG \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01061)\] introduced a planning-retrieval-reasoning framework that enables LLMs to perform faithful and interpretable reasoning by leveraging KG structure. These approaches highlight the growing interest in combining the strengths of LLMs and KGs to improve reasoning, accuracy, and generalizability in NLP tasks.

### <a id='category-knowledge-graphs-and-language-models'></a>Knowledge Graphs and Language Models: 4 papers found.
The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) with large language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning and claim verification has gained significant attention, with several studies exploring complementary approaches. Researchers have highlighted that while LLMs excel in reasoning tasks, they often struggle with structured data and multi-step reasoning pipelines without adaptation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\]. KGs, on the other hand, provide structured, semantically rich representations that are ideal for reasoning, yet most existing verification methods rely on unstructured text, limiting their effectiveness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09729)\]. To address these limitations, recent work has focused on developing frameworks that synergize LLMs and KGs, such as AutoKG, which employs multi-agent systems with LLMs and external sources for KG construction and reasoning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06136)\]. Additionally, the StructGPT approach introduces an Iterative Reading-then-Reasoning (IRR) method to improve zero-shot reasoning over structured data by leveraging external interfaces for evidence collection and reasoning \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09645)\]. Furthermore, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising solution, integrating external knowledge bases to enhance LLMs' accuracy and credibility, particularly for knowledge-intensive tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10997)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the potential of combining LLMs and KGs to achieve more robust and interpretable reasoning systems, paving the way for future research in this domain.

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 7 papers found.
The integration of knowledge graphs (KGs) with large language models (LLMs) to enhance reasoning and claim verification has been a growing area of research. Several studies have explored the use of KGs to improve the accuracy and explainability of LLMs in tasks such as fact-checking and question answering. For instance, the Knowledge Graph Prompting (KGP) method \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11730)\] introduces a graph construction and traversal module to help LLMs better understand the logical relationships between documents, which is crucial for multi-document question answering. Similarly, the Knowledge Solver (KSL) \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03118)\] leverages the generalizability of LLMs to perform zero-shot knowledge retrieval from external KGs, enabling them to handle tasks without prior adaptation. These approaches highlight the potential of KGs in providing structured, semantically rich information that can guide LLMs in more accurate and interpretable reasoning. Additionally, the use of external knowledge sources, such as ConceptNet \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.03975)\], has been shown to enhance the understanding of language by providing richer semantic context beyond what is captured by distributional semantics alone. These works collectively emphasize the importance of integrating structured knowledge with LLMs to overcome limitations in reasoning over complex, unstructured data.

[Snippet]: «Pretrained large language models (LLMs) are widely used in many sub-fields of natural language processing (NLP) and generally known as excellent few-shot learners with task-specific exemplars. Notably, chain of thought (CoT) prompting, a recent technique for eliciting complex multi-step reasoning through step-by-step answer examples, achieved the state-of-the-art performances in arithmetics and symbolic reasoning, difficult system-2 tasks that do not follow the standard scaling laws for LLMs. While these successes are often attributed to LLMs' ability for few-shot learning, we show that LLMs are decent zero-shot reasoners by simply adding"Let's think step by step"before each answer. Experimental results demonstrate that our Zero-shot-CoT, using the same single prompt template, significantly outperforms zero-shot LLM performances on diverse benchmark reasoning tasks including arithmetics (MultiArith, GSM8K, AQUA-RAT, SVAMP), symbolic reasoning (Last Letter, Coin Flip), and other logical reasoning tasks (Date Understanding, Tracking Shuffled Objects), without any hand-crafted few-shot examples, e.g. increasing the accuracy on MultiArith from 17.7% to 78.7% and GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7% with large InstructGPT model (text-davinci-002), as well as similar magnitudes of improvements with another off-the-shelf large model, 540B parameter PaLM. The versatility of this single prompt across very diverse reasoning tasks hints at untapped and understudied fundamental zero-shot capabilities of LLMs, suggesting high-level, multi-task broad cognitive capabilities may be extracted by simple prompting. We hope our work not only serves as the minimal strongest zero-shot baseline for the challenging reasoning benchmarks, but also highlights the importance of carefully exploring and analyzing the enormous zero-shot knowledge hidden inside LLMs before crafting finetuning datasets or few-shot exemplars.»
