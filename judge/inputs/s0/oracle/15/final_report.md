## Related Works

Brain tumor segmentation in medical imaging has been a focal point of research, with several approaches proposed to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of segmentation tasks. Early methods relied on traditional image processing techniques, but the advent of deep learning has significantly advanced the field. One of the most influential frameworks for biomedical image segmentation is the U-Net architecture, which has been widely adopted for its encoder-decoder structure and ability to capture contextual information \[[11]()\]. However, U-Net and its variants often require extensive training data and computational resources, which can be a limiting factor in clinical settings \[[13]()\].

Recent work has focused on improving the efficiency and performance of segmentation models by leveraging pre-trained models and advanced architectures. The nnU-Net framework, which automatically configures model parameters based on the input data, has demonstrated promising results in brain tumor segmentation tasks \[[14]()\]. This approach reduces the need for manual hyperparameter tuning and has been shown to achieve competitive performance on benchmark datasets such as BraTS \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\]. However, nnU-Net still requires substantial training time and may not fully utilize pre-trained weights from other domains \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\].

To address these limitations, researchers have explored the integration of pre-trained models from natural image datasets, such as ImageNet, into medical image segmentation frameworks. For instance, the Med3D model leverages transfer learning from large-scale natural image datasets to improve the performance of 3D medical image analysis tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00625)\]. Similarly, the Models Genesis framework demonstrates that self-supervised learning can yield superior results compared to traditional transfer learning methods, particularly in 3D medical imaging \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07882)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of transfer learning in reducing training time and improving segmentation accuracy.

Another notable advancement is the use of attention mechanisms and transformer-based architectures for 3D medical image segmentation. The Swin UNETR model, which combines the strengths of vision transformers with the U-Net architecture, has shown promising results in capturing long-range dependencies in 3D MRI scans \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\]. Similarly, the TransBTS model introduces a novel architecture that integrates 3D CNNs with transformers to enhance the modeling of both local and global features in brain tumor segmentation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\]. These models demonstrate the effectiveness of hybrid architectures in handling the complexity of 3D medical images.

In addition to architectural improvements, researchers have also focused on optimizing training strategies and data augmentation techniques. The category "3D Medical Image Segmentation Frameworks" encompasses various approaches aimed at improving the accuracy and efficiency of brain tumor segmentation in 3D MRI data. Early methods relied on manual delineation, which was time-consuming and prone to human error \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02629)\]. To address these limitations, semantic segmentation networks based on encoder-decoder architectures were introduced, with some models incorporating variational auto-encoders to regularize the decoding process and improve segmentation quality \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11654)\]. The nnU-Net framework emerged as a significant advancement, achieving top performance in the BraTS 2020 challenge through region-based training, aggressive data augmentation, and postprocessing refinements \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\]. Subsequent improvements built upon nnU-Net, such as the integration of axial attention mechanisms and group normalization, leading to further performance gains \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04653)\]. More recently, ensemble methods combining multiple frameworks like DeepSeg, nnU-Net, and DeepSCAN have demonstrated superior results, achieving high Dice scores and low Hausdorff distances across different datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09310)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of 3D medical image segmentation techniques, driven by the need for more efficient and accurate tumor detection in clinical settings.

### <a id='category-attention-and-transformer-based-models'></a>Attention and Transformer-Based Models: 2 papers found.
Recent advancements in 3D medical image segmentation have focused on integrating transformer-based models to improve the modeling of both local and global features, particularly for tasks like brain tumor segmentation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\]. Traditional approaches, such as Fully Convolutional Neural Networks (FCNNs), have been widely used due to their "U-shaped" architecture, which enables effective feature propagation and segmentation accuracy across various imaging modalities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\]. However, FCNNs are limited in capturing long-range dependencies due to the fixed kernel sizes in convolutional layers, which can hinder performance on tumors with complex or variable shapes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\]. In contrast, transformer models, originally developed for natural language processing, have demonstrated superior capabilities in capturing long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\]. This has led to the development of novel architectures like TransBTS, which combines 3D CNNs with transformers to capture both local 3D context and global features for improved segmentation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\]. Similarly, the Swin UNETR model leverages hierarchical transformers with shifted windows to extract features at multiple resolutions, while maintaining skip connections to the FCNN-based decoder for refined output \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\]. These transformer-based approaches have shown promising results in the BraTS challenges, achieving competitive or superior performance compared to traditional methods \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\]. The integration of pre-trained weights and efficient training strategies further enhances the performance of these models, as seen in recent works that adapt 2D pre-trained models to the 3D domain \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\].

### <a id='category-pre-trained-models-in-medical-imaging'></a>Pre-trained Models in Medical Imaging: 2 papers found.
The development of pre-trained models in medical imaging has gained significant attention due to the challenges associated with data scarcity and the complexity of 3D medical data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00625)\]. Researchers have explored the use of large-scale datasets such as ImageNet to pre-train models, which can then be fine-tuned for specific medical tasks, leading to improved performance and faster convergence \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00625)\]. However, transferring 2D pre-trained models to 3D medical imaging often results in loss of anatomical information, thereby compromising performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07882)\]. To address this, novel approaches such as the Med3D network have been introduced, which co-trains on multi-domain datasets to extract general 3D medical features \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00625)\]. Additionally, self-supervised learning frameworks like Models Genesis have been proposed, which leverage the inherent anatomical structures in medical images to generate strong supervision signals without manual labeling \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07882)\]. These models have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional methods, including those trained from scratch or fine-tuned from 2D pre-trained models \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07882)\]. The integration of such advanced pre-training strategies into medical imaging tasks, such as brain tumor segmentation, has shown promising results in terms of efficiency and accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00625)\].
