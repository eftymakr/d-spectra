## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images, leveraging the ability to model complex geometry and appearance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\]. The core idea involves optimizing a 3D scene representation by minimizing the photometric discrepancy between input views and differentiable renderings \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12231)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often limited by the number of input views, especially in dynamic or complex scenes where certain angles are never captured \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\]. To address this limitation, recent works have explored data augmentation techniques, including video frame interpolation (VFI), to generate additional views and improve the robustness of neural rendering models \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\].

Video frame interpolation has evolved from classical motion compensation-based methods to deep learning-based approaches, including kernel-, flow-, hybrid-, phase-, GAN-, Transformer-, Mamba-, and diffusion model-based approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\]. These methods aim to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones while maintaining spatial and temporal coherence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\]. Several approaches have focused on improving the quality of interpolation in the presence of large object motion, occlusion, and lighting variation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. For instance, the Depth-Aware Video Frame Interpolation method explicitly detects occlusion by leveraging depth information to synthesize intermediate flows that preferentially sample closer objects \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. Similarly, the Many-to-Many (M2M) splatting framework directly forward warps pixels to the desired time step and fuses overlapping pixels, achieving fast multi-frame interpolation with robustness to artifacts \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03513)\].

Real-time video frame interpolation has also seen significant progress, with methods like RIFE achieving state-of-the-art performance while maintaining high efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\]. RIFE uses a neural network named IFNet to estimate intermediate flows end-to-end, and it supports arbitrary-timestep frame interpolation with temporal encoding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\]. This enables efficient and high-quality video frame interpolation for real-time applications \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\].

Recent advances in neural rendering have also explored the integration of video frame interpolation as a data augmentation strategy. For example, the paper proposes using video frame interpolation to generate additional views for training neural rendering models, thereby improving the reconstruction performance on both static and dynamic scenes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\]. This approach is inspired by the fact that video frame interpolation can implicitly model camera movement and real-world 3D geometry \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\].

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-methods'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Methods: 4 papers found.
Video frame interpolation (VFI) has evolved from classical motion compensation-based approaches to modern deep learning-based methods, including kernel-, flow-, hybrid-, phase-, GAN-, Transformer-, Mamba-, and diffusion model-based approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\]. These techniques aim to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones while maintaining spatial and temporal coherence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\]. Recent works such as RIFE propose real-time intermediate flow estimation using a neural network (IFNet) that estimates intermediate flows end-to-end with faster speed, without relying on pre-trained optical flow models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\]. Similarly, the M2M splatting framework directly warps pixels to the desired time step using multiple bidirectional flows and fuses overlapping pixels, achieving efficient multi-frame interpolation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03513)\]. Another approach, EMA-VFI, introduces a module that explicitly extracts motion and appearance information through a unifying operation, enabling efficient VFI by integrating into hybrid CNN and Transformer architectures \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00440)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to improve efficiency, quality, and applicability of VFI across various domains such as event-based, cartoon, and medical image processing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\].

While significant advances have been made from the recent deep convolutional neural networks, the quality of interpolation is often reduced due to large object motion or occlusion. In this work, we propose a video frame interpolation method which explicitly detects the occlusion by exploring the depth information. Specifically, we develop a depth-aware flow projection layer to synthesize intermediate flows that preferably sample closer objects than farther ones. In addition, we learn hierarchical features to gather contextual information from neighboring pixels. The proposed model then warps the input frames, depth maps, and contextual features based on the optical flow and local interpolation kernels for synthesizing the output frame. Our model is compact, efficient, and fully differentiable. Quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate that the proposed model performs favorably against state-of-the-art frame interpolation methods on a wide variety of datasets.»

### <a id='category-neural-rendering-techniques'></a>Neural Rendering Techniques: 3 papers found.
Neural rendering techniques have seen significant advancements, particularly in the context of autonomous driving (AD), where the ability to synthesize novel views from sparse or limited input data is crucial. Recent methods such as NeuRAD \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15260)\] and Lightning NeRF \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05907)\] have addressed key challenges in this domain, including long training times, dense semantic supervision, and limited generalizability. NeuRAD introduces a robust view synthesis method tailored for dynamic AD data, incorporating extensive sensor modeling for both camera and lidar, including rolling shutter and beam divergence, and demonstrating state-of-the-art performance across multiple AD datasets \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15260)\]. Similarly, Lightning NeRF leverages LiDAR geometry priors to enhance novel view synthesis, achieving a five-fold increase in training speed and a ten-fold improvement in rendering speed on real-world datasets like KITTI-360 and Argoverse2 \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05907)\]. These approaches highlight the growing focus on efficient and scalable neural rendering methods that can handle the complexities of real-world driving environments. Additionally, methods like ViewCrafter \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\] further push the boundaries by synthesizing high-fidelity novel views from single or sparse images, utilizing video diffusion models and iterative view synthesis strategies to improve the quality and range of generated scenes. These developments underscore the importance of data augmentation and efficient scene representation in advancing neural rendering for dynamic and complex environments.

### <a id='category-neural-radiance-field-regularization'></a>Neural Radiance Field Regularization: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Under good conditions, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) have shown impressive results on novel view synthesis tasks. NeRFs learn a scene's color and density fields by minimizing the photometric discrepancy between training views and differentiable renderings of the scene. Once trained from a sufficient set of views, NeRFs can generate novel views from arbitrary camera positions. However, the scene geometry and color fields are severely under-constrained, which can lead to artifacts, especially when trained with few input views. To alleviate this problem we learn a prior over scene geometry and color, using a denoising diffusion model (DDM). Our DDM is trained on RGBD patches of the synthetic Hypersim dataset and can be used to predict the gradient of the logarithm of a joint probability distribution of color and depth patches. We show that, these gradients of logarithms of RGBD patch priors serve to regularize geometry and color of a scene. During NeRF training, random RGBD patches are rendered and the estimated gradient of the log-likelihood is backpropagated to the color and density fields. Evaluations on LLFF, the most relevant dataset, show that our learned prior achieves improved quality in the reconstructed geometry and improved generalization to novel views. Evaluations on DTU show improved reconstruction quality among NeRF methods.»
