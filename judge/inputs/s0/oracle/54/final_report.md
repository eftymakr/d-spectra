## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems due to their ability to model complex user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. However, existing GCN-based methods face challenges in capturing rich preference patterns and leveraging diverse neighborhood information effectively \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. For instance, traditional GCNs often suffer from the over-smoothing problem, where stacking more layers leads to indistinguishable node embeddings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\]. This issue is exacerbated by the rigid notion of neighborhood aggregation, which limits the ability to capture fine-grained interactions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)\]. 

To address these limitations, several works have proposed modifications to GCN architectures. Simplifying GCNs by removing non-linearities and collapsing weight matrices has shown that linear models can achieve comparable performance while being more interpretable and scalable \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07988)\]. Similarly, the introduction of residual structures has helped alleviate the over-smoothing problem by allowing deeper network training without losing discriminative power \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of architectural design in improving the expressiveness and efficiency of GCN-based recommenders.

Another line of research focuses on the theoretical understanding of GNNs' expressive power. For example, it has been shown that many GNN variants, including GCNs and GraphSAGE, cannot distinguish certain simple graph structures, which limits their representational capacity \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. In response, a new architecture has been proposed that is provably more expressive than existing GNNs, matching the power of the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. This work underscores the need for more flexible and expressive models in graph-based recommendation tasks.

Additionally, the role of graph signal processing in recommendation has been explored. Spectral-based methods, such as SpectralCF, leverage the spectral domain to capture deeper connections between users and items, effectively addressing the cold-start problem \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10044)\]. Similarly, the use of graph filters has been shown to be crucial for denoising and capturing meaningful patterns in user-item interactions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07265)\]. These methods highlight the potential of combining spectral analysis with graph-based models to enhance recommendation performance.

Recent work has also emphasized the importance of adaptive and personalized approaches. For instance, the Adaptive Graph Contrastive Learning (AdaGCL) framework introduces two adaptive contrastive view generators to better empower the CF paradigm by addressing data sparsity and noise \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05552)\]. Similarly, the Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) model focuses on disentangling user intents to yield more interpretable and accurate recommendations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03624)\]. ### <a id='category-recent-advances-in-graph-based-collaborative-filtering'></a>Recent Advances in Graph-Based Collaborative Filtering: 14 papers found.
Recent advances in graph-based collaborative filtering have focused on improving the representation of users and items by leveraging graph structures and neural networks. Several studies have proposed methods to enhance the modeling of user-item interactions through graph neural networks (GNNs). For instance, node2vec \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\] introduced a flexible framework for learning continuous node representations by using biased random walks to capture diverse neighborhood patterns. Similarly, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) adapted for graph-structured data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\] have been used to learn hidden layer representations that encode both local graph structure and node features. The NGCF framework \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\] further integrated user-item interaction graphs into the embedding process, allowing for the propagation of embeddings to model high-order connectivity. This approach was later refined by DGCF \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00826)\], which aimed to disentangle user intents by modeling distribution over intents for each interaction. Additionally, HMLET \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.07988)\] proposed a hybrid method combining linear and non-linear embedding propagation, showing that the linearity of propagation could be influenced by user centrality. GTN \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\] addressed the issue of unreliable interactions by introducing a principled graph trend collaborative filtering method. CAGCN \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] focused on analyzing message-passing mechanisms and proposed a recommendation-oriented topological metric, Common Interacted Ratio (CIR), to enhance collaborative effect modeling. HCCF \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\] tackled over-smoothing and sparse data issues by introducing a hypergraph contrastive learning framework. NCL \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.10044)\] enhanced contrastive learning by incorporating potential neighbors from both graph structure and semantic space. Finally, AdaGCL \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.07265)\] proposed an adaptive graph contrastive learning framework to improve user representations through two trainable view generators. These studies collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to refine graph-based collaborative filtering by addressing issues such as over-smoothing, data sparsity, and the modeling of diverse user intents.
