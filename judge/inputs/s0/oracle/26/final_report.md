## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as the de facto standard for privacy-preserving data analysis \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07756)\]. However, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behavior by servers, particularly in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. Servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. This issue has been explored in several prior works. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] highlights that local differential privacy (LDP) is vulnerable to malicious data providers who can manipulate their output before sending, thereby gaining plausible deniability. To address this, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] proposes a verifiable randomization mechanism that allows the data collector to verify the completeness of the randomization process, thereby preventing output-manipulation attacks.

In the context of verifiability, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\] introduces the concept of Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP), which requires the publishing entity to output a zero-knowledge proof that the output is both differentially private and reliable. This approach ensures that the randomness used in DP is faithfully generated without revealing it to the verifier. However, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\] also notes that computational assumptions are necessary, as a separation exists between information-theoretic DP and computational DP under verifiability. This distinction is crucial for understanding the trade-offs between security and efficiency in verifiable DP systems.

The local model of DP, while offering increased privacy, comes at the cost of reduced utility compared to the central model \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07756)\]. To bridge this gap, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07756)\] proposes Crypt$ε$, a system that achieves the accuracy and expressibility of the central model without requiring a trusted data collector. Instead, it uses two non-colluding untrusted servers to run DP programs on encrypted data, ensuring that the servers learn nothing more than the noisy outputs. This approach is particularly relevant for distributed settings where trust is limited.

In addition to these works, \[[3](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-540-24632-9\_31)\] develops cryptographically secure techniques for randomized response, ensuring that respondents cannot affect the tally beyond their own vote. These techniques are foundational for ensuring the integrity of DP mechanisms in distributed environments. Similarly, \[[9](https://doi.org/10.1145/3319535.3354256)\] presents a method for securely sampling biased coins, which is essential for generating noise in DP protocols. \[[10](https://doi.org/10.1145/3576915.3616641)\] further extends this by proposing a method for securely sampling discrete Gaussian noise, which is critical for multi-party differential privacy applications.

The challenge of efficiently sampling noise in distributed settings has also been addressed in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\], where a taxonomy of secure sampling protocols is presented, and existing protocols are extended to be resilient against Byzantine attackers. ### <a id='category-verifiable-differential-privacy-(vdp)-frameworks'></a>Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) Frameworks: 2 papers found.
Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) frameworks aim to address the vulnerabilities in traditional differential privacy (DP) by introducing mechanisms that ensure the correctness and reliability of DP implementations. These frameworks seek to prevent malicious entities from manipulating DP outputs, which can occur through incorrect noise sampling or correlated noise generation, as noted in the analysis of adversarial behaviors in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. To mitigate these risks, VDP requires the publishing entity to provide a zero-knowledge proof that confirms the output adheres to DP principles while maintaining privacy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. This approach ensures that the randomness introduced by DP cannot be exploited as a smokescreen for malicious activities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. Similarly, in local differential privacy (LDP) scenarios, malicious data providers can manipulate their outputs before transmission, undermining the integrity of the protocol \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. To counteract this, verifiable randomization mechanisms are proposed, allowing data collectors to verify that the randomization process was executed faithfully, thus preventing output-manipulation attacks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These frameworks demonstrate that computational assumptions are necessary for verifiability, as they distinguish between information-theoretic and computational DP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. Additionally, they show that zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) are sufficient but not necessary for achieving verifiable DP, offering flexibility in design \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. The development of efficient verifiable mechanisms, such as the Verifiable Distributed Discrete Laplacian Mechanism (VDDLM) and improved Verifiable Randomized Response (VRR), highlights the practicality of VDP in real-world applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. These advancements underscore the importance of integrating verification into DP to enhance security and trust in privacy-preserving data analysis.

### <a id='category-local-vs.-central-differential-privacy-models'></a>Local vs. Central Differential Privacy Models: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Differential privacy (DP) has steadily become the de-facto standard for achieving privacy in data analysis, which is typically implemented either in the "central" or "local" model. The local model has been more popular for commercial deployments as it does not require a trusted data collector. This increased privacy, however, comes at a cost of utility and algorithmic expressibility as compared to the central model. In this work, we propose, Crypt$\epsilon$, a system and programming framework that (1) achieves the accuracy guarantees and algorithmic expressibility of the central model (2) without any trusted data collector like in the local model. Crypt$\epsilon$ achieves the "best of both worlds" by employing two non-colluding untrusted servers that run DP programs on encrypted data from the data owners. Although straightforward implementations of DP programs using secure computation tools can achieve the above goal theoretically, in practice they are beset with many challenges such as poor performance and tricky security proofs. To this end, Crypt$\epsilon$ allows data analysts to author logical DP programs that are automatically translated to secure protocols that work on encrypted data. These protocols ensure that the untrusted servers learn nothing more than the noisy outputs, thereby guaranteeing DP (for computationally bounded adversaries) for all Crypt$\epsilon$ programs. Crypt$\epsilon$ supports a rich class of DP programs that can be expressed via a small set of transformation and measurement operators followed by arbitrary post-processing. Further, we propose performance optimizations leveraging the fact that the output is noisy. We demonstrate Crypt$\epsilon$'s feasibility for practical DP analysis with extensive empirical evaluations on real datasets.»
