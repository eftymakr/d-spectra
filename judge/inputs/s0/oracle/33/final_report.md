## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a promising approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04671)\]. This strategy has been widely adopted due to its effectiveness in mitigating forgetting, although its optimality remains an open question \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Prior work has explored various forms of rehearsal, including replaying past data during training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.09762)\], and has demonstrated that such methods can significantly improve performance on sequential tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02931)\]. However, most studies have focused on concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, without thoroughly analyzing the potential benefits of sequential rehearsal \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\].

Sequential rehearsal, inspired by human learning strategies where tasks are revisited in sequence, has been less explored in CL. Recent theoretical analyses have begun to address this gap by examining the performance of different rehearsal strategies in overparameterized linear models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]. These studies have shown that sequential rehearsal can offer advantages, particularly when tasks are less similar \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. This aligns with findings from other works that highlight the importance of task similarity in determining the effectiveness of continual learning strategies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11942)\]. For instance, the NTK overlap matrix has been introduced to quantify task similarity and its impact on catastrophic forgetting \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04003)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting by leveraging prior knowledge. Progressive Neural Networks (PNNs) use lateral connections to previously learned features to avoid forgetting \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. Similarly, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) employs orthogonal gradient descent to minimize interference with past tasks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03534)\]. Trust Region Gradient Projection (TRGP) further refines this idea by selecting relevant old tasks based on gradient projections and reusing their frozen weights \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09588)\]. These methods demonstrate that careful control of the optimization process can significantly reduce forgetting while maintaining performance on new tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\].

The Deep Generative Replay framework introduces a dual model architecture to enable efficient replay of past data without requiring large memory \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17583)\]. This approach is particularly useful in real-world applications where access to past data is limited \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06475)\]. Similarly, iCaRL introduces an incremental learning strategy that allows for class-incremental learning without requiring access to all previous data \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16939)\]. These methods highlight the importance of memory and replay in continual learning, although they often rely on either explicit memory storage or careful regularization to prevent forgetting \[[17]()\].

### <a id='category-regularization-techniques-for-continual-learning'></a>Regularization Techniques for Continual Learning: 3 papers found.
The category "Regularization Techniques for Continual Learning" encompasses several approaches aimed at mitigating catastrophic forgetting, a critical challenge in continual learning where neural networks struggle to retain knowledge from previous tasks when learning new ones \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. One notable method is iCaRL, which enables class-incremental learning by maintaining a small set of training data for previously seen classes while progressively adding new ones, allowing the model to learn strong classifiers and data representations simultaneously \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]. This contrasts with earlier methods that were limited to fixed data representations, making them incompatible with deep learning architectures \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.07725)\]. Another approach, regularization-based continual learning, has been re-examined through a novel perspective involving second-order Taylor approximations of the loss function, leading to a unified framework that encompasses existing algorithms like Elastic Weight Consolidation and Kronecker factored Laplace approximation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. Theoretical analyses further highlight the importance of accurately approximating the Hessian matrix for effective regularization \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]. Additionally, research has explored continual learning in linear classification tasks, showing that weak regularization can be viewed as solving a sequential max-margin problem, aligning with the Projection Onto Convex Sets (POCS) framework \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03534)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the role of regularization in maintaining performance across tasks while learning incrementally, offering both theoretical insights and practical implications for training strategies in continual learning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10974)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03534)\].

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Multi-task learning (MTL) is a machine learning paradigm that aims to improve the generalization performance of a model on multiple related tasks by training it simultaneously on those tasks. Unlike MTL, where the model has instant access to the training data of all tasks, continual learning (CL) involves adapting to new sequentially arriving tasks over time without forgetting the previously acquired knowledge. Despite the wide practical adoption of CL and MTL and extensive literature on both areas, there remains a gap in the theoretical understanding of these methods when used with overparameterized models such as deep neural networks. This paper studies the overparameterized linear models as a proxy for more complex models. We develop theoretical results describing the effect of various system parameters on the model's performance in an MTL setup. Specifically, we study the impact of model size, dataset size, and task similarity on the generalization error and knowledge transfer. Additionally, we present theoretical results to characterize the performance of replay-based CL models. Our results reveal the impact of buffer size and model capacity on the forgetting rate in a CL setup and help shed light on some of the state-of-the-art CL methods. Finally, through extensive empirical evaluations, we demonstrate that our theoretical findings are also applicable to deep neural networks, offering valuable guidance for designing MTL and CL models in practice.»
