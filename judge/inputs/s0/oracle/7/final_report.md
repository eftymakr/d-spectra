## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit behaviors such as refusal within their activation space. These behaviors are often the result of safety alignment techniques like reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\], which aim to make models act in a helpful and harmless manner. However, identifying and modifying such behaviors remains a significant challenge, as existing methods often rely on predefined refusal templates detectable in output tokens or require manual analysis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07858)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more automated and robust methods for steering LLM behavior.

Recent work has explored various approaches to steer LLMs, including fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and activation engineering. For instance, LoRA fine-tuning has been shown to effectively undo safety training in models like Llama 2-Chat \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20624)\]. Similarly, methods such as activation addition and contrastive activation addition (CAA) have been proposed to modify model behavior during inference by manipulating activations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02949)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02151)\]. These techniques demonstrate the potential for inference-time steering, which can be more efficient and flexible than traditional training-based methods.

However, these approaches often require assumptions about the model's behavior, such as the presence of specific refusal tokens or the use of directional ablation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04359)\]. Additionally, some methods, like red teaming, have been used to evaluate the robustness of safety alignment by attempting to subvert models into generating harmful content \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. These studies reveal that even well-aligned models can be vulnerable to adversarial attacks, especially when they are not explicitly trained to resist such inputs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09003)\].

To address these challenges, we introduce COSMIC (Cosine Similarity Metrics for Inversion of Concepts), an automated framework for direction selection that identifies viable steering directions and target layers using cosine similarity, entirely independent of model outputs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01405)\]. This approach is inspired by prior work on linear representation hypotheses, which suggest that high-level concepts are represented as directions in some representation space \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09719)\]. By leveraging cosine similarity, COSMIC avoids the need for predefined refusal templates or assumptions about the model's behavior, making it more robust and generalizable.

The effectiveness of COSMIC is supported by recent studies on activation engineering, which have shown that modifying activations during inference can significantly alter model behavior while preserving other capabilities \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01174)\]. Furthermore, the use of spectral editing of activations has been demonstrated to improve truthfulness and reduce bias in LLMs \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03827)\]. These findings underscore the importance of understanding and manipulating model activations to achieve desired behaviors.

The category "Model Vulnerability to Attacks" highlights critical vulnerabilities in safety-aligned large language models (LLMs) that can be exploited with minimal resources. These models, designed to resist malicious use through alignment techniques, can be subverted by adversarial attacks that leverage small amounts of malicious data, such as 100 examples with just 1 GPU hour, to generate harmful content without compromising their ability to respond to regular queries \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02949)\]. This phenomenon, termed "Shadow Alignment," demonstrates that even models with robust safety mechanisms are not immune to subversion, as they can be adapted to harmful tasks while retaining helpfulness \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02949)\]. Additionally, adaptive jailbreaking attacks, which utilize logprobs and random search to maximize specific token probabilities, have shown high success rates across multiple LLMs, including Vicuna, Llama, and GPT series, even when logprobs are not directly accessible \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02151)\]. These findings underscore the need for more resilient safety mechanisms and highlight the importance of adaptivity in attack strategies, as different models exhibit unique vulnerabilities based on their architecture and training \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02151)\]. The implications of these vulnerabilities suggest that current safety measures may be insufficient, necessitating further research into robust alignment techniques and attack mitigation strategies.

### <a id='category-activation-manipulation-for-behavior-change'></a>Activation Manipulation for Behavior Change: 5 papers found.
Activation Manipulation for Behavior Change encompasses a range of techniques aimed at steering language models by modifying their internal activations to achieve desired behavioral outcomes. These methods typically involve decomposing model activations, projecting them onto specific subspaces, or adding steering vectors to influence output behavior. For instance, Affine Concept Editing (ACE) utilizes affine decomposition to control refusal behavior across different models, including large-scale models like Llama 3 70B, by combining affine subspace projection with activation addition \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09003)\]. Similarly, Spectral Editing of Activations (SEA) projects input representations into directions that maximize covariance with positive demonstrations (e.g., truthful content) while minimizing covariance with negative ones (e.g., hallucinated content), demonstrating effectiveness in improving truthfulness and reducing bias \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09719)\]. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) further refines this approach by computing steering vectors based on the difference in residual stream activations between positive and negative examples, allowing precise control over targeted behaviors during inference \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. Conditional Activation Steering (CAST) extends this concept by enabling selective activation steering based on input context, allowing for rules such as refusing hate speech or adult content while maintaining normal responses to other queries \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05907)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the potential of activation manipulation to enhance model alignment and safety without requiring extensive retraining or weight optimization. Additionally, Trojan Activation Attack (TA^2) demonstrates the risks associated with such methods by injecting malicious steering vectors into activation layers, which can be triggered to steer models toward undesirable behaviors \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09433)\]. Overall, these studies underscore the growing interest in leveraging activation space manipulation for both improving and compromising model behavior.
