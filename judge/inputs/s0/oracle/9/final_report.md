## Related Works

Transfer learning has been extensively studied, particularly in domains where data scarcity is a challenge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. In the context of medical imaging, transfer learning is used to improve generalization by reusing pre-trained models on related tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\]. This is especially relevant in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), where the variability in image acquisition and the limited availability of annotated data pose significant challenges \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\]. Transfer learning is also beneficial for reusing models trained on related tasks to improve performance on the target task \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\].

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have emerged as a powerful tool for data augmentation and synthetic data generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)\]. GANs consist of a generator and a discriminator that are trained in an adversarial manner, allowing the generator to produce realistic synthetic data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)\]. This framework has been applied in various domains, including medical imaging, to generate synthetic images that can be used for training machine learning models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)\]. However, GANs can suffer from issues such as mode collapse, where the generator fails to produce diverse outputs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02340)\].

Conditional GANs extend the GAN framework by incorporating additional information, such as labels, to guide the generation process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784)\]. This allows for the generation of images conditioned on specific attributes, making them useful for tasks such as image tagging and segmentation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784)\]. However, conditional GANs can be sensitive to noisy labels, which can lead to the generation of incorrect or low-quality images \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03205)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed robust conditional GAN architectures that can handle noisy labels by incorporating mechanisms such as label corruption during training \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03205)\].

In the context of medical imaging, synthetic data generation has been used to address class imbalance, a common issue in tasks such as lesion detection and segmentation \[[12](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0267976)\]. For example, the use of synthetic data generated by GANs has been shown to improve classifier performance by increasing the representation of underrepresented classes \[[12](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0267976)\]. This is particularly relevant in the case of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), which are rare and difficult to detect due to their low prevalence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. ### <a id='category-transfer-learning-in-mri'></a>Transfer Learning in MRI: 2 papers found.
Transfer learning, which involves applying knowledge from one problem domain to another, has been increasingly utilized in MRI to address challenges such as variation in MR images and class imbalance in lesion detection \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. In the context of multiple sclerosis, where quantitative susceptibility maps are critical for distinguishing between rim and non-rim lesions, transfer learning enables the reutilization of pre-trained models to improve classification and segmentation tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\]. This is particularly important given the rarity of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), which necessitates synthetic data generation to enhance minority class representation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. Transfer learning strategies, especially those involving convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have shown promise in improving classifier performance and generating probabilistic segmentation maps \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\]. Additionally, the use of generative networks to create synthetic susceptibility maps has been explored to address data scarcity and improve model generalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. While existing approaches have focused on improving detection accuracy, there remains a gap in thoroughly examining the interpretability of these strategies and comparing them against alternative methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\].

These methods highlight the growing interest in leveraging generative models to overcome data scarcity and improve diagnostic accuracy in neuroimaging tasks.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «We propose a new framework for estimating generative models via an adversarial process, in which we simultaneously train two models: a generative model G that captures the data distribution, and a discriminative model D that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than G. The training procedure for G is to maximize the probability of D making a mistake. This framework corresponds to a minimax two-player game. In the space of arbitrary functions G and D, a unique solution exists, with G recovering the training data distribution and D equal to 1/2 everywhere. In the case where G and D are defined by multilayer perceptrons, the entire system can be trained with backpropagation. There is no need for any Markov chains or unrolled approximate inference networks during either training or generation of samples. Experiments demonstrate the potential of the framework through qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the generated samples.»

	Document 2: [Snippet]: «We study the problem of learning conditional generators from noisy labeled samples, where the labels are corrupted by random noise. A standard training of conditional GANs will not only produce samples with wrong labels, but also generate poor quality samples. We consider two scenarios, depending on whether the noise model is known or not. When the distribution of the noise is known, we introduce a novel architecture which we call Robust Conditional GAN (RCGAN). The main idea is to corrupt the label of the generated sample before feeding to the adversarial discriminator, forcing the generator to produce samples with clean labels. This approach of passing through a matching noisy channel is justified by corresponding multiplicative approximation bounds between the loss of the RCGAN and the distance between the clean real distribution and the generator distribution. This shows that the proposed approach is robust, when used with a carefully chosen discriminator architecture, known as projection discriminator. When the distribution of the noise is not known, we provide an extension of our architecture, which we call RCGAN-U, that learns the noise model simultaneously while training the generator. We show experimentally on MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets that both the approaches consistently improve upon baseline approaches, and RCGAN-U closely matches the performance of RCGAN.»
