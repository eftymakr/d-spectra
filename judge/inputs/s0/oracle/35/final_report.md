## Related Works

Protein conformation sampling and structure prediction have long relied on physics-based simulations such as molecular dynamics (MD) and Monte Carlo (MC) methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. These approaches, while accurate, are computationally intensive and often struggle with rare event sampling and long equilibration times \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. To address these limitations, recent advances in deep learning have introduced generative modeling techniques, particularly denoising diffusion models, which enable efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven models remains a challenge, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\].

Several studies have explored the use of deep learning for protein structure prediction. For instance, EigenFold employs diffusion models to generate a distribution of structures from a given protein sequence, modeling the structure as a system of harmonic oscillators and inducing a cascading-resolution generative process along the eigenmodes of the system \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Similarly, Str2Str proposes a structure-to-structure translation framework capable of zero-shot conformation sampling with roto-translation equivariant property, leveraging an amortized denoising score matching objective trained on general crystal structures \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of deep learning in capturing conformational ensembles and flexibility, but they often rely on simulation data for training, which can be costly and limit generalizability \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\].

To improve the physical plausibility of generated structures, researchers have proposed methods that incorporate force fields and energy-based objectives. For example, ConfDiff introduces a force-guided SE(3) diffusion model that integrates physical prior knowledge to guide the generation process, ensuring that sampled conformations remain close to the equilibrium distribution \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. Similarly, the Distributional Graphormer (DiG) framework employs deep neural networks to transform a simple distribution towards the equilibrium distribution, conditioned on a descriptor of a molecular system such as a chemical graph or a protein sequence \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of incorporating physical guidance in generative models to enhance the accuracy and reliability of predicted structures.

### <a id='category-conformation-sampling-techniques'></a>Conformation Sampling Techniques: 2 papers found.
Conformation sampling techniques have seen significant advancements through the integration of deep learning and statistical mechanics, enabling more efficient and accurate generation of equilibrium states in complex molecular systems. Traditional methods, such as molecular dynamics simulations, are computationally expensive and often intractable for large systems, necessitating the development of novel approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. Recent work has introduced Boltzmann Generators, which utilize neural networks to learn coordinate transformations that allow for the direct sampling of equilibrium distributions, bypassing the need for extensive simulation steps \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. These methods are particularly effective for systems like solvated proteins, where the accurate computation of free energy differences and discovery of new configurations are critical \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01729)\]. Similarly, the Distributional Graphormer (DiG) framework leverages deep neural networks to transform simple distributions towards equilibrium distributions, conditioned on molecular descriptors such as chemical graphs or protein sequences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. This approach enables the efficient generation of diverse conformations and provides state density estimations, further enhancing the statistical understanding of molecular systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of combining physical principles with data-driven models to improve the accuracy and efficiency of conformation sampling in molecular science.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-protein-generation'></a>Diffusion Models in Protein Generation: 3 papers found.
Recent advances in diffusion models have significantly improved the generation of protein conformations, addressing limitations of traditional physics-based simulations such as slow sampling and computational expense \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. These models, including EigenFold \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\], ConfDiff \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\], and Structure Language Modeling (SLM) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403)\], leverage deep learning to efficiently explore the conformational landscape of proteins. EigenFold introduces a diffusion process based on harmonic oscillators, enabling the sampling of diverse structures from a given sequence and achieving high accuracy with a median TMScore of 0.84 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. ConfDiff, on the other hand, integrates force-guided networks with score models to enhance the fidelity of generated conformations while maintaining diversity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. SLM encodes protein structures into a latent space using variational autoencoders and applies language modeling to capture sequence-specific conformation distributions, offering a 20-100x speedup over existing methods \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the growing trend of using diffusion models to model protein dynamics and conformational changes, providing more efficient and accurate alternatives to molecular dynamics simulations. The integration of physical priors and the ability to generate ensembles of structures are key advancements that align with the goals of modern protein structure prediction and functional analysis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18403)\].
