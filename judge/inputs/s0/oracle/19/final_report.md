## Related Works

Speech-driven facial animation has been a focal point in recent research, with various approaches aiming to generate realistic 3D facial expressions that align with speech input. Early methods often relied on phoneme-level feature extraction and deterministic models, which limited the expressiveness and flexibility of generated animations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. For instance, FaceFormer introduced a Transformer-based autoregressive model to encode long-term audio context and predict 3D face meshes, improving lip synchronization but still facing challenges in emotional expressiveness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Similarly, CodeTalker addressed the regression-to-mean problem by formulating the task as a code query in a learned codebook space, promoting more vivid and realistic facial motions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

The importance of emotional expressiveness in facial animation has also been emphasized. Emotional Speech-Driven Animation with Content-Emotion Disentanglement proposed EMOTE, which decouples speech and emotion during training, allowing for explicit control over emotional expressions while maintaining lip synchronization \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. This approach was further refined in EmoTalk, which introduced an emotion disentangling encoder to separate content and emotion in speech signals, enabling more natural and controllable facial expressions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. These works highlight the growing recognition of the need for emotional disentanglement in generating expressive facial animations.

In addition to emotional expressiveness, the issue of lip synchronization and the averaging effect of phonetically similar syllables has been a significant challenge. FaceXHuBERT leveraged self-supervised speech representation learning to incorporate both lexical and non-lexical information, improving robustness to background noise and handling audio from varied recording conditions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05416)\]. This approach was further enhanced by the use of HuBERT, which masked speech inputs in the latent space and solved a contrastive task over quantized representations, leading to better speech recognition performance with limited labeled data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07447)\]. These models laid the groundwork for more robust and expressive audio encodings.

Recent work has also focused on non-deterministic models to capture the variability in facial expressions. FaceDiffuser introduced a diffusion-based approach to generate speech-driven facial animations, allowing for non-deterministic outputs that better reflect the natural variability in facial cues \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11306)\]. This was further extended by 3DiFACE, which proposed a lightweight audio-conditioned diffusion model for 3D facial motion, enabling stochasticity and motion editing \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00870)\]. These models address the inherent one-to-many relationship between speech and facial expressions, offering greater flexibility in animation generation.

### <a id='category-speech-driven-facial-animation-techniques'></a>Speech-Driven Facial Animation Techniques: 10 papers found.
Speech-driven facial animation techniques have seen significant advancements, with recent works focusing on improving realism, emotional expressiveness, and lip synchronization. Early approaches primarily focused on phoneme-level feature extraction and short audio windows, often leading to inaccurate lip movements due to limited context \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. More recent methods, such as FaceFormer, employ Transformer-based autoregressive models to encode long-term audio context and predict sequential 3D face meshes, while integrating self-supervised speech representations to address data scarcity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Similarly, the VOCA model introduces a 4D face dataset and a neural network that factors identity from facial motion, enabling realistic animation of unseen subjects without retargeting \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\]. Emotional expressiveness has also been a focal point, with methods like EMOTE introducing explicit emotion control by decoupling speech and emotion losses, ensuring lip-sync while allowing for natural emotional expression \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\]. Additionally, the Mimic framework disentangles speaking style and semantic content, enabling more realistic and diverse facial animations by learning separate latent spaces for style and content \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10877)\]. Despite these advances, challenges remain, such as the averaging effect caused by phonetically similar syllables in self-supervised audio feature spaces, which can reduce the precision of lip shape generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008)\]. To address this, the Wav2Sem module was proposed as a plug-and-play semantic decorrelation module, leveraging semantic features to decouple audio encodings and enhance facial animation naturalness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008)\]. These developments collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve both technical accuracy and expressive capabilities in speech-driven facial animation.

### <a id='category-audio-conditioned-diffusion-models'></a>Audio-Conditioned Diffusion Models: 3 papers found.
In the category of Audio-Conditioned Diffusion Models, recent research has focused on improving the realism and expressiveness of speech-driven 3D facial animations. Methods such as FaceDiffuser \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11306)\] and 3DiFACE \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00870)\] address limitations in deterministic approaches by introducing non-deterministic models that generate diverse facial expressions from speech inputs. FaceDiffuser leverages diffusion techniques and pre-trained speech models like HuBERT to encode audio, enabling the generation of 3D facial animations compatible with rigged characters \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11306)\]. Similarly, 3DiFACE proposes a lightweight audio-conditioned diffusion model that supports motion editing and stochasticity, allowing for personalized facial animations trained on small datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00870)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in diffusion-based models for generating more natural and expressive facial animations, addressing issues such as averaging effects in lip motion generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008)\]. The integration of semantic information, as seen in the Wav2Sem module, further enhances the precision of audio-visual alignment by decorrelating audio encodings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008)\]. These developments underscore the importance of combining semantic understanding with diffusion models to achieve more accurate and diverse facial animation outputs.
