## Related Works

In the field of in-context learning (ICL), the reliance on high-quality demonstrations from large annotated corpora has been well established \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. However, the presence of noisy annotations poses a significant challenge, as existing methods often assume that noisy samples yield higher perplexity than their clean counterparts \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07461)\]. This assumption, while useful in low-noise scenarios, fails when the noise ratio is high and many demonstrations are flawed \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.06974)\]. 

Several approaches have been proposed to address label noise in training deep neural networks. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00038)\] introduces a framework that models the relationship between noisy and clean labels using an undirected graphical model, enabling semi-supervised learning from noisy datasets. This method has shown effectiveness in reducing label noise in image labeling tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\] explores the use of instance-dependent label noise, where the noise rate is not uniform across instances, and proposes a second-order approach to estimate covariance terms between noise rates and Bayes optimal labels. This method allows for the transformation of instance-dependent noise into class-dependent noise, enabling the use of existing solutions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

Another line of work focuses on learning with noisy labels through semi-supervised techniques. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12275)\] introduces DivideMix, a framework that dynamically divides training data into labeled and unlabeled sets, and trains the model in a semi-supervised manner. This approach helps avoid confirmation bias by training two diverged networks simultaneously \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02391)\]. Additionally, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\] presents a unified distillation framework that leverages side information, such as a small clean dataset and label relations in a knowledge graph, to hedge the risk of learning from noisy labels \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04019)\].

In the context of scene graph generation, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06125)\] addresses the issue of noisy labels by proposing NICEST, a strategy that detects and corrects noisy samples through a series of steps, including negative and positive sample detection and correction \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03683)\]. This method improves the quality of generated scene graphs by generating high-quality samples and designing an effective training strategy \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07131)\].

Furthermore, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\] discusses the challenges of text classification with noisy, historical data, highlighting the discrepancy between training and prediction data \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01326)\]. This work emphasizes the importance of evaluating performance metrics on clean, future inputs rather than noisy historical data \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.10456)\].

In summary, the existing literature on noisy labels and in-context learning has made significant strides in addressing the challenges posed by label noise. These approaches range from statistical modeling of noise to semi-supervised learning and distillation techniques, each offering unique insights and solutions to the problem

### <a id='category-gender-bias-in-language-models'></a>Gender Bias in Language Models: 3 papers found.
The category "Gender Bias in Language Models" explores how biases, particularly gender-related, can be embedded in language models and the methods to mitigate them. Existing research highlights that gender bias in word embeddings, such as those trained on news articles, can manifest through associations like "receptionist" with female \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\]. These biases are often geometrically represented as directions in the embedding space, where gender-neutral words are linearly separable from gender-specific terms \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\]. To address this, methodologies have been proposed to modify embeddings to reduce such stereotypes while preserving useful properties like analogy resolution and concept clustering \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\]. Additionally, studies on natural language generation (NLG) have shown that language models may exhibit biased regard towards different demographic groups, using sentiment scores as a proxy for bias measurement \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01326)\]. These findings underscore the need for careful analysis and bias mitigation strategies in language models to ensure fairness and accuracy in their outputs. The challenge lies in developing robust metrics and techniques that can effectively detect and correct these biases without compromising the models' performance on standard tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06520)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01326)\].

[Snippet]: «The presence of label noise often misleads the training of deep neural networks. Departing from the recent literature which largely assumes the label noise rate is only determined by the true label class, the errors in human-annotated labels are more likely to be dependent on the difficulty levels of tasks, resulting in settings with instance-dependent label noise. We first provide evidences that the heterogeneous instance-dependent label noise is effectively down-weighting the examples with higher noise rates in a non-uniform way and thus causes imbalances, rendering the strategy of directly applying methods for class-dependent label noise questionable. Built on a recent work peer loss , we then propose and study the potentials of a second-order approach that leverages the estimation of several covariance terms defined between the instance-dependent noise rates and the Bayes optimal label. We show that this set of second-order statistics successfully captures the induced imbalances. We further proceed to show that with the help of the estimated second-order statistics, we identify a new loss function whose expected risk of a classifier under instance-dependent label noise is equivalent to a new problem with only class-dependent label noise. This fact allows us to apply existing solutions to handle this better-studied setting. We provide an efficient procedure to estimate these second-order statistics without accessing either ground truth labels or prior knowledge of the noise rates. Experiments on CIFAR10 and CIFAR100 with synthetic instance-dependent label noise and Clothing1M with real-world human label noise verify our approach. Our implementation is available at https://github.com/UCSC-REAL/CAL.»
