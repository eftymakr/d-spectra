## Related Works

Protein dynamics and conformational ensembles play a critical role in biological function, necessitating advanced computational methods to study and generate realistic protein structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Traditional approaches rely on molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, which are computationally expensive and limited in throughput \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling, particularly denoising diffusion models, have enabled efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven approaches remains challenging, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\].

Several studies have explored energy-based models (EBMs) for protein conformation generation. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\] proposes an EBM that operates at atomic scale and is trained solely on crystallized protein data, achieving performance comparable to the Rosetta energy function. This model captures physicochemical properties relevant to protein energy and is evaluated on the rotamer recovery task \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\] introduces techniques to improve contrastive divergence training of EBMs, highlighting the importance of a neglected gradient term and demonstrating improved performance on benchmarks such as image generation and OOD detection \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.01316)\]. These efforts underscore the potential of EBMs in capturing physical properties of proteins.

Diffusion models have also been extensively applied to protein structure prediction. \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\] presents a diffusion-based generative model that generates protein backbone structures by denoising from a random, unfolded state towards a stable folded structure. This approach mirrors the natural folding process and leverages the inherent shift and rotational invariance of the representation to avoid complex equivariant networks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\] proposes a latent diffusion model that reduces the complexity of protein modeling while flexibly capturing the distribution of natural protein structures in a condensed latent space. This model uses an equivariant protein autoencoder to embed proteins into a latent space and then applies an equivariant diffusion model to learn the distribution of the latent representations \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\].

Moreover, \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\] introduces EvoDiff, a diffusion framework that combines evolutionary-scale data with the conditioning capabilities of diffusion models for controllable protein generation in sequence space. EvoDiff generates high-fidelity, diverse, and structurally-plausible proteins that cover natural sequence and functional space, demonstrating the potential of sequence-based approaches in protein design \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\]. These models highlight the growing trend of using deep learning to generate protein structures with high accuracy and diversity.

### <a id='category-protein-generation-and-modeling'></a>Protein Generation And Modeling: 9 papers found.
The category "Protein Generation And Modeling" encompasses a range of methodologies aimed at improving the prediction and generation of protein structures and conformations. Recent advances have focused on leveraging generative models, such as flow-based and diffusion-based approaches, to capture the complex conformational landscapes of proteins \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These models often integrate physical priors to ensure the generated structures are thermodynamically plausible \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. For instance, AlphaFlow and ESMFlow utilize flow matching frameworks to fine-tune single-state predictors like AlphaFold, enabling the generation of diverse and accurate protein structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. Similarly, ConfDiff introduces a force-guided SE(3) diffusion model that incorporates physical knowledge to enhance the fidelity of generated conformations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\]. Additionally, models like BioEmu and idpGAN demonstrate the potential of machine learning to generate statistically independent samples of protein ensembles, either from coarse-grained or atomistic simulations, offering a computationally efficient alternative to traditional MD simulations \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8fa4f6b5232868c2e400670716540f6877068439)\]\[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. The development of large-scale protein language models, such as ProGen2 and ProGen, highlights the importance of extensive training data and parameter scaling to achieve state-of-the-art performance in sequence generation and structure prediction \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03497)\]. These models are trained on vast datasets, including genomic and metagenomic sequences, to capture evolutionary patterns and generate novel, viable protein sequences \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Furthermore, the integration of attention mechanisms across multiple sequence alignments has enabled more efficient and accurate learning of protein structure and function \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/deee48c5e0ac0407a1e002905caaf2b174bdb0e6)\]. Overall, these approaches collectively address the challenges of conformational sampling, structural prediction, and the integration of physical principles into data-driven models, advancing the field of protein modeling and its applications in structural biology and drug discovery.

### <a id='category-energy-based-models-for-molecular-systems'></a>Energy-Based Models for Molecular Systems: 3 papers found.
Energy-based models (EBMs) have gained significant attention in molecular systems for their ability to capture complex energy landscapes and generate realistic molecular structures. These models operate by defining energy functions that can be optimized to align with physical principles, enabling accurate conformation prediction and molecular graph generation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13167)\]. Unlike traditional methods that rely on pre-defined physical rules, EBMs learn from data, allowing for more flexible and expressive representations of molecular energy \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03288)\]. Recent work has focused on improving the training of EBMs, particularly through techniques like Score Matching and Noise Contrastive Estimation, which address the challenge of training models with unknown normalizing constants \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.03288)\]. Additionally, researchers have explored permutation-invariant energy functions to ensure robustness in molecular graph generation, as these models must account for the discrete and irregular nature of molecular structures \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.00546)\]. The integration of physical supervision into data-driven models remains a key challenge, with recent approaches like Energy-based Alignment (EBA) aiming to bridge this gap by aligning generative models with physical feedback \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of EBMs in molecular systems, offering promising avenues for protein design and structural biology applications.

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 5 papers found.
The category "Others" encompasses a range of topics related to molecular recognition, energy-based models, and protein structure prediction. Molecular recognition is a fundamental process in biology, with the traditional "induced fit" hypothesis being challenged by the "conformational selection" model, which suggests that pre-existing protein conformations are selected by ligands \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f5082a31aa5ccf39ab11a4515972d3493b757bbb)\]. This paradigm shift has implications for understanding processes like signaling and drug design. In parallel, advancements in generative modeling, such as denoising diffusion models, have enabled efficient protein structure prediction and conformation sampling \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these models remains challenging, as energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b08a8e202e71cb4a9c10332dbff55f7448ebff2)\]. To address this, Energy-based Alignment (EBA) has been introduced, aligning generative models with physical models to improve conformational state balance \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b08a8e202e71cb4a9c10332dbff55f7448ebff2)\]. Additionally, methods like FoldFlow and latent diffusion models have been developed to enhance protein structure prediction by leveraging advanced mathematical frameworks and efficient training techniques \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02391)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04120)\]. These approaches collectively contribute to the evolving landscape of computational biology and protein design.

### <a id='category-protein-structure-prediction-and-design'></a>Protein Structure Prediction and Design: 4 papers found.
Recent advances in protein structure prediction and design have focused on leveraging generative modeling frameworks to capture the conformational diversity and functional flexibility essential for biological processes. Methods such as EigenFold \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\] employ diffusion generative models to sample a distribution of structures from a given protein sequence, offering a more comprehensive understanding of model uncertainty compared to traditional approaches. Similarly, ProteinMPNN \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\] introduces a deep learning-based sequence design method that outperforms physically based approaches like Rosetta in sequence recovery and structural robustness. These models are further enhanced by denoising diffusion probabilistic models and equivariant neural networks, as seen in Genie \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12485)\], which generates protein backbones that are more designable, novel, and diverse. Additionally, algorithms like TM-align \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/08a05b9e8548354e24542c2b5f68b1432b50b27d)\] improve the accuracy and efficiency of structural alignment, facilitating better model selection in blind predictions. These developments highlight the growing integration of deep learning and physical principles to advance the field of protein design and structural biology.

### <a id='category-protein-structure-generation-models'></a>Protein Structure Generation Models: 4 papers found.
Recent advances in protein structure generation models have significantly enhanced the ability to design proteins with specific functions and structures. These models leverage generative approaches, such as diffusion processes and denoising algorithms, to simulate the complex folding behavior of proteins and generate structurally plausible designs \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. For instance, Chroma employs a diffusion process that respects polymer conformational statistics, enabling the direct sampling of novel protein structures and sequences while conditioning on desired properties \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. Similarly, the diffusion-based model described in \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\] generates protein backbone structures by denoising from an unfolded state to a stable folded conformation, mirroring the natural folding process and producing highly realistic structures \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]. Additionally, EvoDiff integrates evolutionary-scale data with conditioning capabilities to generate diverse, high-fidelity proteins that cover natural sequence and functional space, even including those with disordered regions \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\]. These models address the limitations of traditional methods by incorporating physical supervision and energy-based objectives, improving the physical plausibility of generated structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. The ability to condition generation on specific properties or functions, such as symmetry or secondary structure elements, further expands the utility of these models in applications like drug discovery and synthetic biology \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. Overall, these approaches represent a shift towards more controllable and biologically accurate protein design, leveraging both data-driven and physics-informed methodologies \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\]\[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f0f0b680ac74960922634e3da5cf1e6f6883c840)\].
