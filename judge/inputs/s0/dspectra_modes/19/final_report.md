## Related Works

Speech-driven facial animation has been a focal point of research in computer vision and audio processing, with numerous approaches aiming to synthesize realistic 3D facial animations from speech signals. Early methods often relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, which were limited in their ability to capture the complex dynamics of facial expressions and lip movements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\]. More recent approaches have leveraged deep learning to improve the accuracy and realism of generated animations. For instance, WaveNet \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03499)\] introduced a deep neural network for generating raw audio waveforms, which has since been adapted for speech synthesis tasks, offering high-quality, natural-sounding outputs. Similarly, the use of GANs has enabled the generation of realistic talking head videos with lip synchronization \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\].

Several works have focused on improving the synchronization between audio and visual features. For example, the Temporal GAN approach \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12992)\] was designed to generate videos of talking heads using a still image and an audio clip, without relying on handcrafted intermediate features. This method achieved significant improvements in lip synchronization and natural facial expressions. Similarly, the work by \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\] proposed a system that generates videos of talking heads using a single reference image and audio input, achieving high-quality results with realistic lip movements and facial expressions.

The challenge of disentangling audio-visual representations has also been a key area of research. The Talking Face Generation by Adversarially Disentangled Audio-Visual Representation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\] introduced a method to disentangle subject-related and speech-related information, enabling arbitrary-subject talking face generation. This approach has been further refined in subsequent works, such as Pose-Controllable Talking Face Generation by Implicitly Modularized Audio-Visual Representation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\], which focuses on generating pose-controllable talking faces with accurate lip synchronization.

Another important direction is the integration of 3D facial models with audio-driven animation. MeshTalk \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09293)\] proposed a method for generating full facial 3D animation from speech, addressing issues such as uncanny or static upper face animation. This work demonstrated that a categorical latent space could effectively disentangle audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information, leading to more realistic facial animations.

Recent advances have also focused on improving the realism and expressiveness of generated animations. For example, FaceFormer \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/435b15745fb279db4ff8daf9d5facb9b21d58f3b)\] introduced a Transformer-based autoregressive model for speech-driven 3D facial animation, which addresses the limitations of prior methods by encoding long-term audio context and autoregressively predicting 3D face meshes. ### <a id='category-speech-driven-facial-animation'></a>Speech Driven Facial Animation: 6 papers found.
Speech-driven facial animation has been extensively explored in recent research, focusing on synthesizing realistic talking faces from speech signals. Most existing approaches rely on mapping audio features to visual features, often requiring post-processing to achieve subject-dependent realism \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\]. However, these methods typically struggle with preserving fine-grained lip movements and natural facial expressions, especially when dealing with phonetically similar syllables that lead to averaging effects in lip shape generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\]. To address these limitations, recent works have introduced end-to-end systems that generate talking head videos using only a still image and an audio clip, without relying on handcrafted features \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09313)\]. These systems employ temporal GANs with discriminators focused on audio-visual synchronization and realistic expressions, leading to more natural results \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\]. Additionally, some studies have proposed frameworks that incorporate self-supervision and cross-modal learning to improve the accuracy of 3D facial animations, leveraging modules like the SelfTalk framework which integrates speech recognition and lip-reading interpreters to enhance visual fidelity \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. Another approach involves casting speech-driven animation as a code query task in a learned codebook, enabling more vivid and synchronized facial motions by reducing cross-modal mapping uncertainty \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\]. These advancements highlight the growing emphasis on disentangling audio-visual representations and improving the expressiveness of generated facial animations.

### <a id='category-audio-visual-talking-face-generation'></a>Audio Visual Talking Face Generation: 3 papers found.
In the field of audio visual talking face generation, recent research has focused on improving the realism and synchronization of generated talking head videos. Methods such as SadTalker \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12194)\] address challenges like unnatural head movement and distorted expressions by leveraging 3D motion coefficients and a 3D-aware face render, which allows for more natural and coherent video generation. Similarly, the Audio-Visual Correlation Transformer (AVCT) \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02749)\] aims to infer talking motions from audio by learning consistent correlations between audio and visual motions, particularly from a specific speaker, leading to more authentic mouth movements. These approaches highlight the importance of modeling both audio and visual components in a unified framework to achieve high-quality results. Additionally, WaveNet \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03499)\] has been instrumental in generating realistic audio waveforms and has shown promise in text-to-speech applications, demonstrating the potential of deep neural networks in audio synthesis. These works collectively emphasize the need for advanced modeling techniques that can accurately capture and generate both audio and visual elements in a synchronized manner.

### <a id='category-talking-face-generation-research'></a>Talking Face Generation Research: 14 papers found.
In the field of talking face generation, recent research has focused on improving the synchronization between audio and visual outputs, particularly in the context of 3D facial animation. Many existing methods rely on pre-trained self-supervised audio models as encoders, which can lead to issues such as the averaging effect when dealing with phonetically similar syllables that have distinct lip shapes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. This problem is exacerbated by the coupling of audio features in self-supervised spaces, resulting in less expressive and less accurate lip motion generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. To address these limitations, several approaches have been proposed. For instance, some studies have introduced frameworks that disentangle content and speaker information in audio signals, allowing for more controlled and realistic facial animations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12992)\]. Others have explored the use of 3D facial models and neural radiance fields to generate high-fidelity, personalized talking heads with accurate lip synchronization and natural head movements \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\]. Additionally, methods such as Face-Former have been developed to encode long-term audio context and autoregressively predict 3D face meshes, improving the quality of generated animations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. Another line of research has focused on incorporating emotional dynamics into the generation process, using techniques like emotion disentanglement to produce more expressive and realistic talking faces \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09313)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance the realism, controllability, and scalability of audio-driven talking face generation systems.

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 7 papers found.
In the realm of speech-driven 3D facial animation, several approaches have been proposed to enhance the realism and accuracy of generated facial movements. For instance, methods like the audio-driven talking-head technique \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.09293)\] focus on generating photo-realistic videos by aligning head motions with speech prosody and maintaining visual consistency across the entire image. Similarly, Voice2Face \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/435b15745fb279db4ff8daf9d5facb9b21d58f3b)\] introduces a model that generates face and tongue animations directly from speech, offering style control and handling multi-quality data. Another approach, the joint audio-text model \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02214)\], leverages contextual text embeddings to improve the synthesis of facial expressions by disambiguating upper face variations not strongly correlated with audio. Additionally, the parametric viseme fitting algorithm \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\] enables the generation of animator-friendly viseme curves, supporting multilingual inputs and improving lip synchronization. The Learn2Talk framework \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12888)\] integrates insights from 2D talking face research to enhance 3D facial animation by improving lip-sync and vertex accuracy. Furthermore, the Mimic framework \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10877)\] explores the coupling between speaking style and semantic content, introducing disentangled representations for more realistic facial animations. Lastly, a deep learning framework \[[28](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/263ad0264a19ac58905804715f14fb53fe2f181a)\] enables real-time 3D facial animation by mapping speech spectrograms to facial action unit intensities, capturing emotional states and enabling speaker-independent applications. These works collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the accuracy, expressiveness, and generalizability of speech-driven facial animation techniques.
