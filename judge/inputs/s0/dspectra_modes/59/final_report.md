## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has garnered significant attention as recommender systems increasingly influence critical domains such as employment and housing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. Prior work has identified a trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness, highlighting the need for a more nuanced understanding of these interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. This trade-off is often framed in terms of a cost passed on to users, similar to economic principles where commodity taxes on suppliers translate into user-side costs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. The concept of elasticity, which quantifies how changes in one variable affect another, has been proposed as a framework to analyze the fairness-accuracy trade-off in re-ranking \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1507.05259)\].

Several approaches have been proposed to address fairness in ranking. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05723)\] introduces a re-ranking method that learns individual preferences across multiple fairness dimensions, enhancing provider fairness while maintaining recommendation accuracy. This approach is particularly relevant as it considers fairness from both user and provider perspectives \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05723)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\] presents a taxation perspective for fair re-ranking, reformulating previous methods as item-level tax policies. However, these policies often lack continuity and controllability, which are critical for practical implementation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].

Another line of work focuses on fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms that balance accuracy and provider-side fairness. The Fairness-Aware Re-ranking (FAR) algorithm \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\] iteratively generates rankings by trading off between accuracy and provider coverage, while also incorporating personalized user tolerance for diversity. This approach demonstrates that fairness can be improved with minimal accuracy loss, and it accounts for individual user preferences \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Additionally, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\] explores the concept of fairness of exposure in rankings, proposing a framework that allows for fairness constraints based on exposure allocation. This work highlights the importance of fairness not only for users but also for the items being ranked \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\].

The trade-off between accuracy and fairness is also a central theme in fairness-aware classification. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\] introduces a confidence-based approach to balance fairness and accuracy, proposing a method to shift decision boundaries for protected groups while maintaining high accuracy. This method allows for a transparent quantification of the trade-off between bias and error \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01788)\]. This framework enables the computation of the efficient frontier of accuracy-fairness trade-offs \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03577)\].

### <a id='category-fairness-in-learning-to-rank'></a>Fairness in Learning to Rank: 7 papers found.
The category "Fairness in Learning to Rank" explores the challenges and approaches to ensuring fairness in recommendation and ranking systems, particularly in scenarios where fairness metrics must be balanced with recommendation accuracy. Prior research has highlighted that traditional methods often focus on a single fairness dimension, such as race or gender, and overlook the role of individual preferences in limiting the ability to achieve fair outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that fairness and accuracy are inherently conflicting objectives, with trade-offs that vary across different fairness dimensions and groups \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\]. For instance, in criminal justice risk assessments, at least six distinct types of fairness have been identified, many of which are mutually exclusive or incompatible with accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. This complexity is further compounded by differences in base rates across protected groups, making it difficult to satisfy all fairness criteria simultaneously \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. In the context of recommender systems, fairness concerns extend beyond user preferences to include item providers, as myopic optimization for user utility can lead to unfair treatment of providers \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05723)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms that balance accuracy with provider-side fairness, incorporating user-specific preferences to enhance diversity and fairness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\]. Moreover, recent work has introduced flexible fairness regularizers that allow for a range of fairness notions, from group-level fairness to individual fairness, enabling the exploration of the accuracy-fairness trade-off through metrics like the Price of Fairness (PoF) \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02409)\]. These approaches underscore the need for more nuanced and comprehensive evaluation frameworks, such as the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve), which enables comparative analysis across different elasticity levels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\].

[Snippet]: «Ranking algorithms are deployed widely to order a set of items in applications such as search engines, news feeds, and recommendation systems. Recent studies, however, have shown that, left unchecked, the output of ranking algorithms can result in decreased diversity in the type of content presented, promote stereotypes, and polarize opinions. In order to address such issues, we study the following variant of the traditional ranking problem when, in addition, there are fairness or diversity constraints. Given a collection of items along with 1) the value of placing an item in a particular position in the ranking, 2) the collection of sensitive attributes (such as gender, race, political opinion) of each item and 3) a collection of constraints that, for each k, bound the number of items with each attribute that are allowed to appear in the top k positions of the ranking, the goal is to output a ranking that maximizes the value with respect to the original rank quality metric while respecting the constraints. This problem encapsulates various well-studied problems related to bipartite and hypergraph matching as special cases and turns out to be hard to approximate even with simple constraints. Our main technical contributions are fast exact and approximation algorithms along with complementary hardness results that, together, come close to settling the approximability of this constrained ranking maximization problem. Unlike prior work on the constrained matching problems, our algorithm runs in linear time, even when the number of constraints is large, its approximation ratio does not depend on the number of constraints, and it produces solutions with small constraint violations. Our results rely on insights about the constrained matching problem when the objective satisfies properties that appear in common ranking metrics such as Discounted Cumulative Gain, Spearman's rho or Bradley-Terry.»

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 9 papers found.
The concept of fairness in re-ranking tasks has been extensively explored, with a focus on the trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness. Several studies have highlighted that fairness considerations in re-ranking can be likened to economic taxation processes, where fairness constraints on items lead to a reduction in user-side accuracy, similar to how commodity taxes on suppliers are passed on to consumers \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. This analogy is further supported by the notion of elasticity, which quantifies how changes in item-side fairness affect user-side accuracy, akin to how elasticity measures the responsiveness of demand to price changes in economics \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. Current evaluation methods for fair re-ranking often rely on a single fairness metric, which limits their ability to comprehensively assess algorithm performance across varying fairness levels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed frameworks such as the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve), which enables a comparative analysis of algorithm performance across different elasticity levels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]. Additionally, methods like Tax-rank have been introduced to improve fairness in re-ranking by leveraging taxation principles, offering a more controllable and continuous approach to adjusting ranking resources \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. These contributions highlight the growing interest in integrating economic principles into fair re-ranking, aiming to achieve a more nuanced understanding of the accuracy-fairness trade-off \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09207)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\].
