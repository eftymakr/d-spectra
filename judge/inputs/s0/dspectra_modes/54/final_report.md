## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early works such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] emphasized the importance of integrating user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure. This approach aimed to capture high-order connectivity and explicitly inject collaborative signals into the embedding process. However, subsequent research has highlighted limitations in these methods, particularly in terms of over-smoothing and the inability to model deeper layers due to the non-linear activation functions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\].

The use of graph convolutional networks in recommendation systems has been further refined by models such as LightGCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which simplifies the design of GCNs by removing non-linearities and focusing on linear neighborhood aggregation. This approach has shown substantial improvements over NGCF, demonstrating the effectiveness of a simpler model in capturing collaborative signals. Similarly, the Linear Residual Graph Convolutional Network (LR-GCCF) \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\] revisits GCN-based CF models by removing non-linearities and introducing a residual network structure to alleviate the over-smoothing problem, which is crucial for large-scale user-item graphs.

Another important development is the Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\], which explicitly incorporates multiple graphs in the embedding learning process. This model not only models high-order information via the user-item interaction graph but also integrates proximal information through user-user and item-item graphs. This approach enhances the ability to capture diverse user-item relationships and improve recommendation accuracy.

The field has also seen the introduction of more sophisticated models such as Refined Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (RGCF) \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\], which addresses the issue of information redundancy caused by non-linear graph convolution operations. RGCF introduces a refined graph convolution structure and a new normalization technique to better capture popularity features, especially in sparse recommendation scenarios.

Furthermore, the Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\] focuses on disentangling user intents by modeling a distribution over intents for each user-item interaction. This allows for more granular and interpretable representations, which is essential for capturing diverse user preferences.

In addition, the work on Deep Item-based Collaborative Filtering (DeepICF) \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04392)\] extends traditional item-based collaborative filtering by incorporating nonlinear neural networks to model higher-order item interactions. This approach enables the capture of more complex decision-making processes in users, leading to improved recommendation performance.

These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to refine graph-based recommendation systems by leveraging spectral graph theory and more sophisticated filtering mechanisms.

### <a id='category-graph-convolutional-networks-research'></a>Graph Convolutional Networks Research: 3 papers found.
Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for semi-supervised learning on graph-structured data, integrating local vertex features with graph topology in convolutional layers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\]. This approach is motivated by a localized first-order approximation of spectral graph convolutions, enabling efficient computation and learning of hidden layer representations that encode both local graph structure and node features \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. However, GCNs face challenges such as over-smoothing with many layers and the need for labeled data for validation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed co-training and self-training approaches to improve performance with few labels \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07606)\]. Additionally, the original GCN model was designed with both training and test data available, posing challenges for large graphs, leading to the development of FastGCN, which uses Monte Carlo methods for efficient batched training and inference \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10247)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance GCNs for scalability and effectiveness in real-world applications.
