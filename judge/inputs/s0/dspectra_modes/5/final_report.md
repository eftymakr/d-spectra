## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area in natural language processing (NLP), aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. This task is particularly relevant for applications requiring real-time adaptation, such as information retrieval, semantic similarity, and task-specific embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Existing approaches, however, often suffer from significant computational overhead due to the need to re-encode the entire corpus for each new instruction \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more efficient methods that can adapt pre-computed embeddings in real time \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)\].

Several works have explored the use of pre-trained models for instruction-following tasks. BERT, for instance, is a bidirectional transformer model that pre-trains on unlabeled text and can be fine-tuned for various tasks, including question answering and language inference \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08773)\]. Similarly, the Transformer architecture, introduced in \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\], has become a foundational model for sequence transduction tasks, offering superior performance and parallelizability compared to recurrent and convolutional models \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. These models, while powerful, typically require retraining or re-encoding for new tasks, which is computationally expensive \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\].

Recent efforts have focused on instruction tuning, where pre-trained models are fine-tuned using instruction-following data to improve their ability to generalize to new tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03277)\]. For example, Self-Instruct introduces a framework for improving instruction-following capabilities by bootstrapping off the model's own generations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06726)\]. Similarly, the Flan Collection provides a comprehensive set of instruction-tuning methods and datasets, demonstrating that task balancing and enrichment techniques are critical for effective instruction tuning \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09689)\]. These approaches rely on human-generated instruction data, which is often limited in quantity and diversity \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\].

To address these limitations, Unnatural Instructions was introduced, a large dataset of creative and diverse instructions generated with minimal human labor \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\]. This dataset has been used to train models that perform well on various benchmarks, surpassing the performance of models trained on manually-curated datasets \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13688)\]. Similarly, the INSTRUCTOR method introduces a single embedder that can generate text embeddings tailored to different downstream tasks and domains without additional training \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05250)\]. This approach is particularly promising for real-time applications where re-encoding the entire corpus is impractical \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07316)\].

In addition to instruction tuning, there has been growing interest in in-context learning, where models perform new tasks by conditioning on a few input-label pairs \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14327)\]. This approach has shown promise in reducing the need for extensive training data

### <a id='category-instruction-tuning-and-generalization'></a>Instruction Tuning and Generalization: 9 papers found.
Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area of research, aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions by highlighting specific attributes of text \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\]. Existing methods often suffer from significant computational overhead due to the need to re-encode the entire corpus for each new instruction \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\]. To address this, recent work has introduced frameworks like Self-Instruct, which leverages a model's own generations to create synthetic instruction data, thereby improving instruction-following capabilities without relying on human annotations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10560)\]. Similarly, Evol-Instruct proposes a method to generate complex instruction data by iteratively rewriting initial instructions, enabling the fine-tuning of models like LLaMA to produce high-quality instruction-following outputs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. The development of GPT-4 further demonstrates advancements in instruction-following, showcasing human-level performance on professional benchmarks and improved adherence to desired behaviors through post-training alignment \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\]. Additionally, the NATURAL INSTRUCTIONS dataset provides a comprehensive set of 61 distinct tasks and 193k task instances, enabling cross-task generalization studies and measuring the effectiveness of instruction-based learning \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08773)\]. Flan-T5, through careful design decisions such as task balancing and mixed prompt settings, has shown significant improvements over prior work, achieving performance gains of 3-17% across various evaluation settings \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13688)\]. Dynosaur introduces a dynamic growth paradigm for automatically curating instruction-tuning data by leveraging existing annotated datasets, reducing API costs and improving data quality \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14327)\]. Instruction backtranslation is another approach that automatically labels human-written text with corresponding instructions, demonstrating effective self-alignment through self-augmentation and self-curation \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.06259)\]. These methods collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance instruction-following capabilities through synthetic data generation, efficient training strategies, and robust evaluation frameworks.

	Document 2: [Category]: «Language Model Research»
[Snippet]: «Large language models (LMs) are able to in-context learn—perform a new task via inference alone by conditioning on a few input-label pairs (demonstrations) and making predictions for new inputs. However, there has been little understanding of how the model learns and which aspects of the demonstrations contribute to end task performance. In this paper, we show that ground truth demonstrations are in fact not required—randomly replacing labels in the demonstrations barely hurts performance on a range of classification and multi-chose tasks, consistently over 12 different models including GPT-3. Instead, we find that other aspects of the demonstrations are the key drivers of endtask performance, including the fact that they provide a few examples of (1) the label space, (2) the distribution of the input text, and (3) the overall format of the sequence. Together, our analysis provides a new way of understanding how and why in-context learning works, while opening up new questions about how much can be learned from large language models through inference alone.»
