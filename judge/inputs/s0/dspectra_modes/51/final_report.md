## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area to address the "right to be forgotten" in federated learning (FL) systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. This concept allows users to remove their data contributions from the global model while preserving the model's utility \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Early approaches to federated unlearning primarily relied on retraining the model from scratch, which is computationally expensive \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. To improve efficiency, several methods have been proposed that aim to remove a client's influence without full retraining \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\].

One notable approach is FedEraser, which leverages historical parameter updates to reconstruct the unlearned model \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. This method trades server storage for faster unlearning by calibrating retained updates \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. Similarly, the SISA training framework reduces the computational overhead associated with unlearning by strategically limiting the influence of a data point during training \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\]. These methods demonstrate that unlearning can be achieved more efficiently than full retraining \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f2f8f7a2ec1b2ede48cbcd189b376ab9fa0735ef)\].

The importance of verifiable unlearning has also been emphasized, particularly in ensuring that the unlearning effect can be independently verified \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\]. VeriFi is a framework that enables systematic analysis and quantification of unlearning effects through marking and checking mechanisms \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\]. This approach ensures that the unlearning process is transparent and verifiable \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]. Additionally, the concept of differential privacy has been integrated into unlearning algorithms to enhance privacy guarantees \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\].

In the context of federated online learning to rank (FOLTR), unlearning capabilities are essential to comply with privacy regulations such as the "right to be forgotten" \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. Existing unlearning methods often rely on single evaluation metrics, which may not provide a comprehensive assessment of unlearning effectiveness \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. To address this, recent studies have proposed multiple evaluation metrics to better capture the nuances of unlearning \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08096)\]. These metrics help in assessing both under-unlearning and over-unlearning scenarios, ensuring a balanced approach to data privacy and model performance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\].

Furthermore, the challenge of unlearning in federated settings has led to the development of novel techniques such as the use of influence functions and knowledge distillation \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/06edb1f6eadb6c141d720fcca4c854578dc5d670)\]. These methods aim to remove a client's contribution while maintaining the model's accuracy \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a08f1c5c9f0bdb24f0dc75c0d969eb353899156b)\]. Additionally, the use of backdoor attacks during training has been proposed as a way to evaluate the effectiveness of unlearning \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.03575)\].

### <a id='category-federated-unlearning-research'></a>Federated Unlearning Research: 18 papers found.
The category "Federated Unlearning Research" explores the challenges and solutions for enabling data erasure in federated learning (FL) systems, driven by the "right to be forgotten" (RTBF) and privacy regulations like GDPR. Federated unlearning (FU) aims to remove specific clients' or data samples' influence from the global model without full retraining, addressing computational inefficiencies and privacy concerns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Existing approaches often rely on retraining or approximate unlearning, but these methods face limitations in efficiency, scalability, and verification \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. To overcome these, researchers have proposed novel frameworks such as FedEraser, which leverages historical parameter updates to reconstruct unlearned models efficiently \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\], and VeriFi, which introduces verifiable unlearning to ensure the unlearning effect is independently confirmed \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. Additionally, methods like SISA training and zero retrain forgetting (ZRF) aim to improve unlearning speed and evaluate performance without retraining \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\]. Despite progress, challenges remain, including information leakage risks, verification difficulties, and the need for robust, non-invasive techniques \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. These studies collectively highlight the growing interest in developing efficient, secure, and verifiable federated unlearning strategies to align with legal and ethical standards in FL \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\].

### <a id='category-privacy-preserving-collaborative-learning'></a>Privacy Preserving Collaborative Learning: 3 papers found.
The category "Privacy Preserving Collaborative Learning" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at enabling collaborative machine learning while safeguarding user privacy. Federated learning (FL) is a prominent framework in this domain, allowing multiple clients to collaboratively train models without sharing raw data, thereby preserving privacy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. However, FL can suffer from suboptimal performance when client data distributions differ significantly, prompting the development of clustered FL (CFL), which groups clients based on the geometry of the loss surface to improve model performance without altering the FL communication protocol \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. Additionally, the increasing emphasis on data privacy, particularly with regulations like the "right to be forgotten," has led to the exploration of unlearning methods in federated systems, such as FRU, which enables the removal of a user's contribution from the training process while maintaining model accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10958)\]. This is crucial for maintaining compliance with privacy laws and enhancing system robustness against malicious clients. Furthermore, research has highlighted the risks of data leakage in collaborative learning, where model updates can inadvertently reveal sensitive information about participants' training data, leading to membership inference attacks \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04049)\]. These findings underscore the need for robust privacy-preserving mechanisms in collaborative learning frameworks, including secure aggregation, differential privacy, and advanced unlearning techniques. The integration of these methods is essential for achieving both privacy and performance in decentralized learning environments.

### <a id='category-federated-learning-privacy-unlearning'></a>Federated Learning Privacy Unlearning: 4 papers found.
The category "Federated Learning Privacy Unlearning" explores the challenges and solutions related to enabling users to remove their data influence from federated learning models, particularly in privacy-sensitive applications like Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR). Existing approaches, such as Federated Unlearning (FU), aim to address the "right to be forgotten" by allowing clients to request data deletion from the global model \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\]. However, these methods often rely on historical data, such as gradients or locally trained models, which can lead to information leakage, as user data may be reconstructed from uploaded information \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09724)\]. To mitigate this, privacy-preserving frameworks like Starfish have been proposed, utilizing Two-Party Computation (2PC) techniques to ensure secure unlearning while minimizing computational overhead \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09724)\]. Additionally, the use of influence functions for feature unlearning has been explored, though it faces practical limitations due to the need for widespread client participation \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\]. Recent work has also introduced metrics like feature sensitivity to evaluate unlearning effectiveness, leveraging Lipschitz continuity to quantify model sensitivity to input perturbations \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of balancing privacy, efficiency, and model accuracy in federated unlearning systems, especially as regulatory frameworks increasingly mandate data deletion capabilities \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09724)\].
