## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods relied on matrix factorization to learn low-dimensional embeddings, but recent advances have shifted toward deep learning-based approaches that better capture complex interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Among these, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling user-item interactions through graph structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, the inherent complexity of GNNs, particularly in terms of computational overhead and over-smoothing, has motivated the development of simplified variants such as LightGCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which focuses on linear propagation and avoids unnecessary nonlinear operations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This approach has shown substantial improvements over more complex models like NGCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

The use of binary representations for recommendation has also gained traction, as it enables efficient online inference by reducing memory usage and computational costs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Prior work on binarized collaborative filtering, such as Binarized Collaborative Filtering with Distilling Graph Convolutional Networks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\], has explored the use of binary codes to represent users and items, leveraging distillation techniques to preserve ranking information. However, these methods often suffer from information loss due to the quantization process, leading to performance degradation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. To mitigate this, recent studies have proposed self-supervised learning approaches that enhance the robustness of graph-based recommendation models by incorporating auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]. These methods aim to minimize information loss during the binarization process, improving both accuracy and robustness.

Furthermore, the integration of contrastive learning into graph collaborative filtering has been explored to address data sparsity issues, with methods such as Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\] explicitly incorporating structural and semantic neighbors into contrastive pairs. This approach enhances the model's ability to capture meaningful relationships by leveraging both local and global graph structures \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]. Similarly, the use of graph convolution networks (GCNs) for recommendation has been extensively studied, with models like DeepWalk \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\] and LightGCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] demonstrating the effectiveness of learning latent representations from graph structures. However, these models often struggle with scalability and over-smoothing, prompting further research into simplification and efficiency improvements \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\].

In summary, while significant progress has been made in learning vectorized embeddings for recommendation, challenges remain in balancing efficiency, accuracy, and robustness. ### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Based Collaborative Filtering: 7 papers found.
Graph-based collaborative filtering has gained significant attention in recommendation systems due to its ability to model user-item interactions through bipartite graphs, enabling the capture of high-order connectivity and collaborative signals \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods primarily relied on matrix factorization and deep learning approaches that encoded user and item embeddings based on pre-existing features, but these methods often failed to fully leverage the collaborative information embedded in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. To address this limitation, researchers proposed Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), which explicitly integrates the user-item graph structure into the embedding process by propagating embeddings across the graph \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, NGCF and similar models, such as LightGCN, were found to include unnecessary components like nonlinear activation and feature transformation, which could hinder training and degrade performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. LightGCN was introduced as a simplified version of GCN, focusing only on neighborhood aggregation for collaborative filtering, which improved performance by about 16% over NGCF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Additionally, Multi-GCCF extended this idea by incorporating multiple graphs, including user-user and item-item graphs, to better capture proximal information and high-order interactions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Furthermore, contrastive learning approaches, such as Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning (NCL), have been proposed to enhance performance by leveraging structural and semantic neighbors in the graph \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of graph-based collaborative filtering by addressing issues such as over-smoothing, data sparsity, and information loss during embedding processes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\].

[Snippet]: «Low-dimensional embeddings of nodes in large graphs have proved extremely useful in a variety of prediction tasks, from content recommendation to identifying protein functions. However, most existing approaches require that all nodes in the graph are present during training of the embeddings; these previous approaches are inherently transductive and do not naturally generalize to unseen nodes. Here we present GraphSAGE, a general, inductive framework that leverages node feature information (e.g., text attributes) to efficiently generate node embeddings for previously unseen data. Instead of training individual embeddings for each node, we learn a function that generates embeddings by sampling and aggregating features from a node's local neighborhood. Our algorithm outperforms strong baselines on three inductive node-classification benchmarks: we classify the category of unseen nodes in evolving information graphs based on citation and Reddit post data, and we show that our algorithm generalizes to completely unseen graphs using a multi-graph dataset of protein-protein interactions.»

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 5 papers found.
The category "Others" encompasses a variety of research topics that extend beyond traditional machine learning paradigms, focusing on advanced techniques for representation learning and optimization. One such area involves the development of efficient methods for learning high-quality distributed vector representations, such as the continuous Skip-gram model, which improves both the quality of word vectors and training speed through subsampling of frequent words and the use of negative sampling \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4546)\]. Additionally, the challenge of representing idiomatic phrases and word order is addressed by methods that identify and learn vector representations for phrases, enhancing semantic understanding \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4546)\]. In the context of recommender systems, collaborative filtering methods often rely on encoding users and items into a shared representation space, with objectives like the BPR loss being used to train these encoders \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.12811)\]. However, recent work has focused on improving the alignment and uniformity of representations on the hypersphere, leading to the development of learning objectives such as DirectAU that directly optimize these properties \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.12811)\]. Furthermore, the efficiency of recommendation systems is enhanced through techniques like graph convolutional networks and binarized collaborative filtering, which aim to reduce computational overhead while maintaining performance \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to improve representation learning and optimization in various domains, from natural language processing to recommendation systems.
