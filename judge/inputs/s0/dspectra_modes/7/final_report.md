## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit behaviors such as refusal within their activation space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. This behavior, crucial for safety and alignment, is often mediated by a single direction in the model's residual stream activations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Prior research has shown that this direction can be leveraged to control model behavior, either by inducing or suppressing refusal \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. However, existing methods for identifying and manipulating these directions often rely on predefined refusal templates detectable in output tokens or require manual analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. These approaches are limited in their generalizability and robustness, particularly in adversarial settings or with weakly aligned models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\].

Recent work has explored the use of adversarial suffixes to suppress the propagation of refusal-mediating directions, thereby influencing model behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Additionally, the use of refusal tokens has been proposed as a method to calibrate refusal behavior, where specific tokens are prepended to model responses during training to control refusal rates \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06748)\]. This approach allows for fine-grained control over refusal behavior without requiring additional fine-tuning, though it depends on the presence of specific tokens \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06748)\].

The concept of steering LLMs using directional manipulation has also been explored. For instance, the work on Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\] demonstrated that deeper networks can be trained more effectively by learning residual functions, which could be analogous to the mechanisms underlying refusal direction in LLMs. Similarly, the development of BERT \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] highlighted the importance of bidirectional training for capturing contextual information, which is essential for understanding and manipulating complex behaviors like refusal.

In the realm of reasoning, LLMs have been shown to be capable of zero-shot reasoning through the use of chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, where a simple prompt like "Let's think step by step" significantly improves performance on various reasoning tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. This suggests that LLMs possess inherent reasoning capabilities that can be harnessed without the need for task-specific examples, which is a promising direction for understanding and manipulating model behavior.

The work on Multimodal Chain-of-Thought Reasoning \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00923)\] further expands on this by incorporating vision and language modalities, demonstrating that multimodal information can enhance the quality and reliability of reasoning outputs. This has implications for the broader understanding of how LLMs process and manipulate information across different modalities, including those relevant to safety and alignment.

### <a id='category-language-model-reasoning-and-alignment'></a>Language Model Reasoning and Alignment: 5 papers found.
The category "Language Model Reasoning and Alignment" encompasses a range of studies focused on enhancing the reasoning capabilities and alignment of large language models (LLMs). Research has shown that LLMs can perform complex reasoning tasks through techniques such as chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, where step-by-step reasoning is explicitly encouraged \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. This approach has demonstrated significant improvements in tasks like arithmetic and symbolic reasoning, with models achieving high accuracy without the need for hand-crafted examples \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Furthermore, the versatility of CoT prompting across diverse reasoning tasks suggests that LLMs possess untapped zero-shot reasoning capabilities that can be harnessed through simple prompts \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. In addition to reasoning, alignment efforts aim to ensure that LLMs behave in a manner that is helpful, honest, and harmless, often leveraging prompting strategies and training objectives such as imitation learning and ranked preference modeling to improve alignment \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17306)\]. Multimodal extensions of CoT, such as Multimodal-CoT, have also been proposed to incorporate vision and text modalities, enhancing performance on tasks like ScienceQA by generating more accurate rationales \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing exploration of LLMs' inherent capabilities and the development of methods to better align them with human values \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17306)\].

### <a id='category-refusal-calibration-in-llms'></a>Refusal Calibration in LLMs: 2 papers found.
Refusal calibration in large language models (LLMs) has been a focal point in recent research, emphasizing the need for safe and reliable model behavior. Studies have shown that refusal mechanisms can be mediated by a single direction in the activation space, allowing for targeted interventions to bypass refusals \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17306)\]. This approach is not limited to English but exhibits cross-lingual universality, as refusal directions derived from one language can effectively bypass refusals in others without additional fine-tuning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17306)\]. Furthermore, the use of refusal tokens has been proposed as a method to control refusal rates by prepending specific tokens during training, enabling adjustments to refusal behavior during inference without requiring further fine-tuning \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.06748)\]. These findings highlight the importance of understanding how refusal mechanisms operate across languages and how they can be manipulated, providing insights for improving multilingual safety defenses and addressing cross-lingual vulnerabilities in LLMs.

### <a id='category-deep-learning-models'></a>Deep Learning Models: 2 papers found.
In the realm of deep learning models, significant advancements have been made in both computer vision and natural language processing. In computer vision, the Inception architecture, later known as GoogLeNet, introduced a deep convolutional neural network that achieved state-of-the-art results in the ImageNet Large-Scale Visual Recognition Challenge 2014 \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.4842)\]. This model optimized computational resources by increasing network depth and width while maintaining a fixed computational budget, leveraging multi-scale processing and the Hebbian principle \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.4842)\]. In natural language processing, the BERT model revolutionized pre-trained language representations by using a bidirectional encoder that jointly conditions on both left and right context in all layers, enabling it to be fine-tuned for various tasks with minimal modifications \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. BERT demonstrated superior performance on multiple NLP tasks, including question answering and language inference, achieving new benchmarks on tasks like GLUE, MultiNLI, and SQuAD \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. These models exemplify the shift toward more sophisticated and adaptable architectures that can be applied across diverse domains.
