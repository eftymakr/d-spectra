## Related Works

The growing deployment of large language models (LLMs) has sparked significant interest in evaluating their fairness, particularly in the context of biased or discriminatory outcomes \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. Traditional fairness metrics often rely on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, which fail to account for the implicit impact of model uncertainty, such as higher confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more nuanced fairness measures that incorporate uncertainty-aware evaluation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

Several studies have explored fairness in machine learning, particularly in areas such as hiring, lending, and predictive policing, where biased data can lead to discriminatory outcomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. For instance, the concept of counterfactual fairness has been introduced to ensure that decisions remain consistent across different demographic groups \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This approach is grounded in causal inference and aims to capture the intuition that a decision should be fair regardless of an individual's membership in a particular group \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. However, these methods often focus on binary classification tasks and may not generalize well to complex models like LLMs \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\].

The importance of fairness in algorithmic decision-making has also been highlighted in the context of recommendation systems, where biased data can lead to unfair predictions against minority groups \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\]. Researchers have proposed new fairness metrics tailored to collaborative filtering, which can better capture the nuances of fairness in recommendation scenarios \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\]. These metrics are often optimized by incorporating fairness terms into the learning objective, allowing for more equitable predictions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\].

In the medical domain, fairness in machine learning has been a critical concern, as biased models can lead to significant disparities in diagnosis and treatment \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725)\]. Efforts have been made to benchmark fairness in medical imaging, with frameworks like MEDFAIR introduced to evaluate and compare fairness across different algorithms and datasets \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725)\]. These studies emphasize the need for robust and reproducible fairness benchmarks that can be applied across various domains \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01725)\].

The challenge of defining and measuring fairness has also been a central topic in the broader field of algorithmic fairness. Researchers have debated the trade-offs between different fairness definitions, such as demographic parity and equalized odds, and have explored how these definitions align with societal expectations \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04783)\]. Some studies have found that simpler definitions, such as demographic parity, often align more closely with laypeople's perceptions of fairness \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04783)\]. This suggests that fairness metrics should be tailored to the specific application context and the societal values they aim to represent \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04783)\].

### <a id='category-fairness-bias-machine-learning'></a>Fairness Bias Machine Learning: 2 papers found.
The growing reliance on machine learning (ML) algorithms in critical domains such as healthcare, education, and finance has intensified the need for fairness in AI systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, which often focus on accuracy and discrete outcome evaluation, fail to account for the nuanced ways in which model uncertainty can influence decision-making and perpetuate bias \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Recent research has highlighted that algorithmic fairness must consider not only the correctness of predictions but also the confidence with which these predictions are made, as higher confidence in incorrect predictions can exacerbate unfair outcomes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. This has led to the development of fairness-enhancing mechanisms that address bias at different stages of the ML pipeline, including pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing techniques \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. These approaches aim to mitigate unfairness by adjusting data, modifying model training, or refining post-hoc evaluations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Additionally, the lack of diverse and representative datasets has been identified as a key source of bias in AI applications, prompting efforts to create more inclusive and comprehensive evaluation benchmarks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. The proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metric, UCerF, exemplifies this trend by incorporating model confidence into fairness assessments, thereby offering a more refined evaluation of fairness compared to conventional measures \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10104)\]. Such advancements underscore the importance of developing fairness-aware ML systems that align with ethical and societal values \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

This underscores the importance of developing robust fairness measures that account for both model behavior and data distribution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. Furthermore, the need for diverse and representative datasets to evaluate fairness has been emphasized, with efforts to create new benchmarks that better reflect real-world scenarios \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05511)\].
