## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. Traditional approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms, such as SASRec and BERT4Rec, to model sequential dependencies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, these models typically incorporate domain-specific modules to enhance cross-domain learning, which may not fully exploit the inherent capabilities of the self-attention mechanism \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of self-attention in sequential recommendation. For instance, LightSANs introduces a low-rank decomposed self-attention mechanism that reduces computational complexity while maintaining performance \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d3f51870f4da5dd9c2a08a55cfa8a380b8d49208)\]. Similarly, TiSASRec incorporates time interval awareness to better model the temporal dynamics of user interactions \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. These works highlight the importance of modeling both the order and timing of interactions in sequential recommendation. 

In the context of cross-domain learning, a number of frameworks have been proposed. For example, DDTCDR employs a dual learning mechanism to transfer knowledge between domains, improving recommendation accuracy by capturing bidirectional latent relations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Similarly, DREAM introduces a decoupled representation approach that enables simultaneous learning of single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/96bfe33a2503e960153a934e10be91da4d8b13a6)\]. These methods often rely on explicit domain adaptation strategies, which can be computationally intensive and may lead to negative transfer if not carefully managed \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the efficiency and effectiveness of cross-domain models. For instance, LSAN proposes a lightweight self-attentive network that reduces memory usage while maintaining high accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11333)\]. Additionally, the introduction of contrastive learning techniques, such as in CL4SRec, has shown promise in improving user representation learning by leveraging self-supervision signals from user behavior sequences \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. These approaches highlight the growing interest in combining self-attention with advanced learning strategies to enhance cross-domain performance.

Moreover, the problem of data sparsity and cold-starts has been addressed through various means. For example, ASReP augments short sequences by generating pseudo-prior items, which helps improve performance for sparse datasets \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.00522)\]. Similarly, the use of graph neural networks in C2DSR allows for the modeling of inter-sequence item relationships, further enhancing the accuracy of cross-domain recommendations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. These methods demonstrate the importance of incorporating diverse modeling strategies to address the challenges inherent in cross-domain sequential recommendation.

### <a id='category-sequential-recommendation-models'></a>Sequential Recommendation Models: 7 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models have evolved significantly, leveraging deep learning techniques to capture user dynamics from historical interactions. Traditional approaches such as Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been widely used, with MCs excelling in sparse datasets due to their simplicity, while RNNs offer better performance in denser environments \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. More recently, self-attention mechanisms, inspired by the Transformer architecture, have gained prominence for their ability to model long-term dependencies and capture global item-item transitions \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/63555b4f363ee72dd8d49d8deca68760542c0cd2)\]. However, these models often suffer from issues like high computational costs and limited personalization \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5aed438fecf702ead9c0cddfa9a80bd00cff0138)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have introduced variants such as SASRec, which combines the strengths of RNNs and attention mechanisms, achieving state-of-the-art results \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Further improvements include the Personalized Transformer (SSE-PT), which integrates user embeddings to enhance personalization, leading to significant performance gains \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5aed438fecf702ead9c0cddfa9a80bd00cff0138)\]. Additionally, methods like CL4SRec utilize contrastive learning to generate self-supervision signals from user behavior sequences, improving user representation quality \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. Meanwhile, lightweight architectures such as LSAN aim to reduce memory bottlenecks by employing compositional embeddings and temporal context-aware schemes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.11333)\]. The integration of side information through mechanisms like NOVA-BERT has also shown promise in enhancing recommendation accuracy by leveraging additional features without overwhelming the model \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03578)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to balance model efficiency, personalization, and performance in sequential recommendation systems.

### <a id='category-cross-domain-sequential-recommendation'></a>Cross Domain Sequential Recommendation: 13 papers found.
Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical area in recommendation systems, aiming to predict future user interactions by leveraging sequential behaviors across multiple domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing approaches typically focus on transferring knowledge from one domain to another, often relying on overlapping users or explicit domain-specific modules \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. However, these methods often overlook the potential of the self-attention mechanism inherent in transformer-based models, which can naturally capture complex correlations between sequential items \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Recent works have attempted to enhance CDSR by incorporating domain-aware components, yet they fail to fully exploit the self-attention module’s ability to learn inter-item relationships \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed innovative frameworks such as C2DSR, which jointly learns intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to capture both single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Similarly, DDGHM introduces dual dynamic graph modeling and hybrid metric training to improve the integration of cross-domain information while mitigating data sparsity \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]. Other studies, like DDTCDR, utilize dual learning to iteratively transfer information between domains, enhancing recommendation performance in both source and target domains \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Additionally, the AMID framework addresses open-world assumptions by introducing adaptive multi-interest debiasing, allowing models to handle non-overlapping users and shifting data distributions \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to improve cross-domain knowledge transfer, enhance model adaptability, and address challenges such as data sparsity and cold-start problems \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\].

### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 8 papers found.
The related works on cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) focus on leveraging information from multiple domains to improve recommendation performance, contrasting with single-domain approaches that rely solely on interactions within a specific domain \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11245)\]. Early methods primarily concentrated on developing advanced transfer modules and aligning user representations using self-supervised learning techniques, but they largely overlooked the importance of aligning item representations, which can lead to sub-optimal sequential modeling and user representation alignment \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12473)\]. Recent studies have introduced frameworks like CA-CDSR, which propose a model-agnostic approach to achieve sequence-aware generation and adaptively partial alignment for item representations, enhancing the effectiveness of cross-domain sequential recommendations \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12473)\]. Additionally, efforts have been made to address the issue of negative transfer, where the performance of CDSR models may underperform compared to SDSR models due to a lack of domain relations or varying data sparsity levels \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11245)\]. To mitigate this, some works estimate the degree of negative transfer and adaptively assign weights to the prediction loss, thereby controlling gradient flows through domains with significant negative transfer \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11245)\]. Furthermore, methods like LightSANs have been proposed to overcome the limitations of self-attention networks by introducing low-rank decomposed self-attention, which reduces computational complexity and improves the modeling of sequential relations between items \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d3f51870f4da5dd9c2a08a55cfa8a380b8d49208)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance the self-attention mechanism and improve the overall performance of CDSR models.

[Snippet]: «Sequential Recommendation characterizes the evolving patterns by modeling item sequences chronologically. The essential target of it is to capture the item transition correlations. The recent developments of transformer inspire the community to design effective sequence encoders,e.g., SASRec and BERT4Rec. However, we observe that these transformer-based models suffer from the cold-start issue,i.e., performing poorly for short sequences. Therefore, we propose to augment short sequences while still preserving original sequential correlations. We introduce a new framework for Augmenting Sequential Recommendation with Pseudo-prior items (ASReP). We firstly pre-train a transformer with sequences in a reverse direction to predict prior items. Then, we use this transformer to generate fabricated historical items at the beginning of short sequences. Finally, we fine-tune the transformer using these augmented sequences from the time order to predict the next item. Experiments on two real-world datasets verify the effectiveness of ASReP.
