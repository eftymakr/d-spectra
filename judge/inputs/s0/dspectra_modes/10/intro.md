## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike traditional next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has shown significant gains in generative tasks, such as coding, where models trained with MTP outperform comparable next-token models by several percentage points \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. For instance, 13B parameter models trained with MTP solve 12% more problems on HumanEval and 17% more on MBPP than next-token models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Additionally, MTP enables faster inference, with models trained with 4-token prediction being up to 3 times faster at inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) struggle with the MTP objective, often leading to suboptimal performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. To address this limitation, we propose a curriculum learning strategy for MTP training, exploring two variants: a forward curriculum and a reverse curriculum \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The forward curriculum gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, allowing SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach improves downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. In contrast, the reverse curriculum starts with MTP and gradually reduces complexity, leading to stronger NTP performance and output quality but failing to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Curriculum learning has been widely studied in the context of neural machine translation (NMT) and sequence prediction tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. For example, competence-based curriculum learning for NMT reduces training time and improves performance by filtering training samples based on difficulty and model competence \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\]. Similarly, scheduled sampling for sequence prediction with recurrent neural networks introduces a curriculum learning strategy that gradually shifts the training process from fully guided to less guided, improving sequence prediction accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. These approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of curriculum learning in improving model performance and training efficiency.

In addition to curriculum learning, process supervision has been shown to significantly outperform outcome supervision in training models to solve complex reasoning tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. For instance, process-supervised models solve 78% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set, whereas outcome-supervised models perform less effectively \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. This highlights the importance of providing feedback at intermediate steps during training
