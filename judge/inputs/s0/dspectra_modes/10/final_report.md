## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike traditional next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has shown significant gains in generative tasks, such as coding, where models trained with MTP outperform comparable next-token models by several percentage points \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. For instance, 13B parameter models trained with MTP solve 12% more problems on HumanEval and 17% more on MBPP than next-token models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Additionally, MTP enables faster inference, with models trained with 4-token prediction being up to 3 times faster at inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) struggle with the MTP objective, often leading to suboptimal performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. To address this limitation, we propose a curriculum learning strategy for MTP training, exploring two variants: a forward curriculum and a reverse curriculum \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The forward curriculum gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP, allowing SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach improves downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. In contrast, the reverse curriculum starts with MTP and gradually reduces complexity, leading to stronger NTP performance and output quality but failing to provide any self-speculative decoding benefits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Curriculum learning has been widely studied in the context of neural machine translation (NMT) and sequence prediction tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. For example, competence-based curriculum learning for NMT reduces training time and improves performance by filtering training samples based on difficulty and model competence \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.09848)\]. Similarly, scheduled sampling for sequence prediction with recurrent neural networks introduces a curriculum learning strategy that gradually shifts the training process from fully guided to less guided, improving sequence prediction accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. These approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of curriculum learning in improving model performance and training efficiency.

In addition to curriculum learning, process supervision has been shown to significantly outperform outcome supervision in training models to solve complex reasoning tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. For instance, process-supervised models solve 78% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set, whereas outcome-supervised models perform less effectively \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. ### <a id='category-others'></a>Others: 4 papers found.
The category "Others" encompasses a variety of research directions that complement or intersect with the main topic of curriculum learning for multi-token prediction. One such direction is the use of reinforcement learning (RL) with outcome supervision to train large language models for complex reasoning tasks, as demonstrated by R$^3$ \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05808)\]. This method addresses the limitations of both outcome and process supervision by leveraging correct demonstrations and progressively adjusting the start state of reasoning, enabling more effective model exploration. Another relevant area is the comparison between outcome and process supervision, where process supervision has been shown to significantly outperform outcome supervision in training models to solve challenging problems, such as those in the MATH dataset \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. Additionally, curriculum learning strategies have been applied to sequence prediction tasks, where they gradually shift the training process from fully guided to less guided schemes, improving performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. These approaches highlight the broader application of curriculum learning in enhancing model performance across different tasks and model sizes. Furthermore, the paradigm of prompt-based learning offers an alternative framework for leveraging pre-trained language models, allowing for few-shot or zero-shot learning through carefully designed prompts \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13586)\]. These diverse approaches collectively underscore the importance of adaptive training strategies in improving the efficiency and effectiveness of language model training.

[Snippet]: «Current state-of-the-art NMT systems use large neural networks that are not only slow to train, but also often require many heuristics and optimization tricks, such as specialized learning rate schedules and large batch sizes. This is undesirable as it requires extensive hyperparameter tuning. In this paper, we propose a curriculum learning framework for NMT that reduces training time, reduces the need for specialized heuristics or large batch sizes, and results in overall better performance. Our framework consists of a principled way of deciding which training samples are shown to the model at different times during training, based on the estimated difficulty of a sample and the current competence of the model. Filtering training samples in this manner prevents the model from getting stuck in bad local optima, making it converge faster and reach a better solution than the common approach of uniformly sampling training examples. Furthermore, the proposed method can be easily applied to existing NMT models by simply modifying their input data pipelines. We show that our framework can help improve the training time and the performance of both recurrent neural network models and Transformers, achieving up to a 70% decrease in training time, while at the same time obtaining accuracy improvements of up to 2.2 BLEU.»
