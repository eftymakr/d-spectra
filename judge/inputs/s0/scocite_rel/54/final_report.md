## Related Works

Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, owing to their ability to model complex interactions between users and items \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Early GCN implementations, such as those in \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\], focused on simplifying the model by removing nonlinearities and collapsing weight matrices, leading to linear models that are easier to train and interpret. However, these simplifications often come at the cost of reduced expressiveness, as demonstrated by the need for deeper layers to capture non-linear interactions in recommendation tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. 

The integration of user-item interactions into the embedding process has been a key advancement. Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] was one of the first to explicitly model the bipartite graph structure by propagating embeddings through the graph, thereby capturing high-order connectivity and collaborative signals. This approach was further refined in LightGCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\], which simplified the model by removing feature transformations and nonlinear activations, resulting in a more efficient and effective framework. However, LightGCN still faces challenges in handling large-scale graphs due to training difficulties and over-smoothing effects \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\]. 

To address these issues, UltraGCN \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\] was proposed, which further simplifies GCNs by skipping infinite layers of message passing and instead approximating the limit of infinite-layer graph convolutions via a constraint loss. This approach not only improves efficiency but also allows for more flexible edge weight assignments and relationship modeling. 

In addition to GCN-based methods, other approaches have explored the use of graph attention mechanisms to better capture fine-grained interactions. Graph Attention Networks (GATs) \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\] introduced masked self-attentional layers that allow nodes to weigh their neighbors differently without requiring costly matrix operations. This has been particularly useful in social recommendation, where heterogeneous relationships and user-item interactions need to be modeled effectively \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07243)\]. 

The use of knowledge graphs (KGs) has also gained traction in recommendation systems. Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\] explicitly models high-order relations in KGs, which are crucial for capturing complex user-item interactions. This approach has shown significant improvements over traditional methods like Neural FM and RippleNet \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. 

Furthermore, self-supervised learning has been explored to improve the robustness and accuracy of GCNs in recommendation tasks. Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL) \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\] introduces auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning by generating multiple views of a node and maximizing agreement between different views.

Despite these advances, challenges remain, including the need for efficient training on large-scale graphs and the ability to model diverse user intents \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. Recent works, such as Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF), aimed to disentangle user intents by modeling intent-aware interactions, improving the interpretability and effectiveness of recommendations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\].
