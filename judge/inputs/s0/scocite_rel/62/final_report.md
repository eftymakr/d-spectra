## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional search index-based approaches, aiming to replace static index structures with a single large-scale neural network that can directly generate document identifiers during retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This paradigm offers the potential for improved efficiency and seamless integration with generative large language models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Prior work has explored various methods to implement this idea, including the use of autoregressive models to generate document identifiers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\], and the development of differentiable search indexes that encode the entire corpus within a single Transformer \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. These models typically rely on constrained decoding strategies to generate document identifiers that are specific to the corpus \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\].

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of autoregressive models in document retrieval. For example, SEAL proposes an approach where all ngrams in a passage are used as potential identifiers, allowing for the use of autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams, which are then mapped to full passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This method outperforms prior autoregressive approaches and establishes new state-of-the-art performance on the KILT benchmark \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, CorpusBrain introduces a pre-trained generative retrieval model that replaces the traditional "index-retrieve-then-rank" pipeline with a single-step generative model, enabling end-to-end optimization and significantly improving retrieval performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\].

Another line of research focuses on the use of dense representations for retrieval, such as in the case of Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), which uses a dual-encoder framework to learn embeddings from a small number of questions and passages \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. This approach outperforms traditional sparse retrieval methods like BM25 and helps establish new state-of-the-art results on open-domain QA benchmarks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. Additionally, ANCE introduces a training mechanism that constructs negative instances from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, improving the alignment between training and testing data distributions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

The integration of retrieval and generation has also been explored in the form of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) models, which combine pre-trained parametric and non-parametric memory for language generation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. These models leverage a dense vector index of Wikipedia and a pre-trained neural retriever to enhance the performance of large pre-trained language models on knowledge-intensive tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Similarly, the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) demonstrates that a single Transformer can encode all information about the corpus, allowing for direct query-to-docid mapping without the need for external indexes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\].

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-frameworks'></a>Generative Retrieval Frameworks: 4 papers found.
The category "Generative Retrieval Frameworks" explores the shift from traditional index-based retrieval systems to end-to-end generative models that directly produce document identifiers from queries. These frameworks aim to simplify the retrieval pipeline by eliminating the need for separate indexing and ranking stages, instead leveraging large-scale pre-trained models to generate relevant document IDs in a single step \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. This approach allows for seamless integration with generative large language models and offers potential improvements in efficiency and adaptability \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. However, existing models often rely on fixed or rule-based document identifiers, such as document titles or clustered embeddings, which may not fully capture semantic information \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. To address this, methods like GenRet introduce a discrete auto-encoding approach to learn semantic document identifiers through tokenization and reconstruction \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. Similarly, CorpusBrain proposes a pre-trained generative retrieval model that encodes all corpus information into its parameters, enabling end-to-end optimization without additional indexing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. GENRE further advances this by generating entity names token-by-token in an autoregressive fashion, allowing for better context and entity affinity modeling \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00904)\]. These frameworks also highlight challenges such as memory footprint and training efficiency, with solutions like RIPOR introducing novel ranking optimization and relevance-based document ID construction \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. Overall, the category emphasizes the potential of generative retrieval to overcome limitations of traditional methods while addressing key technical challenges \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00904)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\].

### <a id='category-differentiable-search-indexes'></a>Differentiable Search Indexes: 4 papers found.
The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) represents a paradigm shift in information retrieval, replacing traditional index-retrieval architectures with a single Transformer model that encodes the entire document corpus within its parameters \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach enables direct query-to-docid generation, eliminating the need for separate indexing and retrieval stages \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00904)\]. However, current DSI models face challenges due to data distribution mismatches between indexing and retrieval, particularly when queries are shorter than indexed documents or when cross-lingual retrieval is required \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed enhancements such as the prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder in Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI), which improves recall performance by optimizing the decoding process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Additionally, studies have explored the scalability of generative retrieval techniques, revealing that synthetic queries during indexing and careful consideration of compute costs are critical for effective performance on large corpora \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. These findings highlight the ongoing efforts to refine DSI models, ensuring they can generalize well across diverse and out-of-distribution datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\].
