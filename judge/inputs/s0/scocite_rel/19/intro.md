## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has been a focal point in computer vision and machine learning research, aiming to generate realistic facial expressions that match speech content and emotional tone. Early approaches often relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, but recent advances have shifted toward data-driven deep learning models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. These models typically use neural networks to map audio signals to facial animations, often leveraging pre-trained self-supervised audio encoders to extract meaningful features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\]. However, a critical limitation of these methods is the inability to accurately distinguish between phonetically similar syllables that produce distinct lip shapes, leading to an averaging effect in lip motion generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\].

Several studies have explored the use of self-supervised speech representations to improve the quality of audio-driven facial animation. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07452)\] introduced a method that integrates self-supervised pre-trained speech representations to address data scarcity issues, while \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079)\] proposed a Transformer-based model that captures long-term audio context for more accurate lip synchronization. These works highlight the importance of capturing both phonetic and emotional nuances in speech to generate realistic facial animations. However, they often fail to explicitly decorrelate audio encodings to prevent the averaging effect caused by phonetically similar syllables \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

To address this limitation, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00023)\] proposed a method that disentangles audio representations into phonetic content, emotional tone, and background noise, improving robustness against variations in speech input. Similarly, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15278)\] introduced a framework that learns disentangled audio-visual representations to enable arbitrary-subject talking face generation, demonstrating the potential of disentanglement in enhancing realism and controllability. These approaches, however, do not explicitly target the coupling between phonetically similar syllables and their corresponding lip shapes, which remains a key challenge in achieving natural facial animations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09488)\].

Another line of research focuses on the integration of emotional and linguistic cues for more expressive facial animation. For example, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\] proposed a system that decomposes speech into duration-independent emotion and duration-dependent content spaces, enabling more natural and emotionally expressive facial movements. Additionally, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\] introduced a method that disentangles speech into emotional and content components, allowing for more accurate lip synchronization and expressive facial expressions. These works underscore the importance of incorporating both linguistic and emotional information for more realistic facial animations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\].
