## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning general-purpose representations from raw data. This approach has been widely applied in computer vision and natural language processing, with notable works such as Contrastive Predictive Coding \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\], which leverages autoregressive models to learn latent representations by predicting future samples \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. Similarly, SimCLR \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\] presents a simple framework for contrastive learning of visual representations, demonstrating that composition of data augmentations and learnable nonlinear transformations significantly improve representation quality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. These methods have shown strong performance across various domains, including speech, images, and text \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. However, these approaches often rely on large amounts of unlabeled data and may not capture the complex relationships between different genomic profiles \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\].

In the field of genomics, unsupervised learning has also been explored. For example, the work by \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\] proposes an unsupervised learning approach to build features suitable for object detection and classification by solving jigsaw puzzles of natural images. This method pre-trains a context-free network (CFN) on a large dataset and transfers the features to other tasks through fine-tuning. While this approach is effective for visual tasks, it does not directly address the challenges of learning representations from genomic data, which are regulated by complex genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\].

Recent advances in self-supervised learning have also shown promise for visual tasks. For instance, the paper by \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\] explores the properties of self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViT), finding that these models can learn semantic segmentation information and achieve strong performance in k-NN classification tasks. The paper also highlights the importance of momentum encoder and multi-crop training in improving the performance of self-supervised ViTs \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\]. These findings suggest that self-supervised learning can be effective for visual tasks, but they do not directly address the challenges of learning representations from genomic data, which require capturing the relationships between DNA sequences and genomic profiles \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\].

### <a id='category-self-supervised-learning-in-vision-tasks'></a>Self-Supervised Learning in Vision Tasks: 10 papers found.
In the realm of self-supervised learning for vision tasks, researchers have explored various strategies to learn rich visual representations without explicit supervision. One approach involves leveraging spatial context by training convolutional neural networks to predict the relative position of image patches, which encourages the model to recognize objects and their parts \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. Another method focuses on instance-level classification using noise-contrastive estimation, demonstrating superior performance on ImageNet classification and enabling improvements in semi-supervised learning and object detection \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\]. Contrastive Predictive Coding has also been revisited and improved, leading to state-of-the-art linear classification accuracy on ImageNet and enhanced transfer learning for object detection \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. The development of deeper residual networks has further enabled significant improvements in tasks such as object detection, with models achieving competitive performance compared to fully supervised pre-trained classifiers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. SimCLR, a simple framework for contrastive learning, has shown that effective predictive tasks, learnable nonlinear transformations, and larger batch sizes contribute to high-quality visual representations, achieving performance comparable to supervised models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. Context encoders, which generate image regions based on their surroundings, have also been shown to capture semantic information, improving performance in classification, detection, and segmentation tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07379)\]. The application of self-supervised learning to Vision Transformers (ViTs) has revealed that these models can capture semantic segmentation information and serve as effective k-NN classifiers, with methods like DINO achieving strong results through self-distillation \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.14294)\]. Additionally, dense contrastive learning (DenseCL) has been proposed to address dense prediction tasks by optimizing pairwise contrastive losses at the pixel level, leading to significant improvements in object detection, segmentation, and instance segmentation \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09157)\]. Finally, unsupervised feature learning through jigsaw puzzles and context-free networks has demonstrated competitive performance in object detection and classification, highlighting the potential of self-supervised approaches in vision tasks \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\].

### <a id='category-semi-supervised-learning-approaches'></a>Semi-Supervised Learning Approaches: 4 papers found.
In the realm of semi-supervised learning, recent approaches have focused on leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data to improve model performance, particularly in scenarios with limited labeled examples. One prominent paradigm involves unsupervised pretraining followed by supervised fine-tuning, which has shown significant effectiveness in tasks like ImageNet classification. This approach utilizes a large, deep network during pretraining, allowing it to capture richer representations from unlabeled data, and subsequently fine-tunes it on a small set of labeled examples \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10029)\]. The use of a big network during pretraining is especially beneficial when the number of labels is low, as it enables the model to better generalize from the limited labeled data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10029)\]. Furthermore, after fine-tuning, the model can be distilled into a smaller network using unlabeled data in a task-specific manner, which helps maintain high classification accuracy while reducing computational costs \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10029)\]. In addition to this, other methods have explored graph-structured data and convolutional neural networks tailored for such structures, which can effectively learn from both local graph features and global patterns \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. Techniques like randomized data augmentation, dropout, and random max-pooling have also been employed to improve generalization and stability in semi-supervised learning, with proposed loss functions designed to exploit the stochastic nature of these methods \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\]. These diverse strategies highlight the evolving landscape of semi-supervised learning, where the integration of unlabeled data is increasingly seen as a critical component for improving model efficiency and performance.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-methods'></a>Contrastive Learning Methods: 3 papers found.
Contrastive learning methods have gained significant traction in unsupervised representation learning, with approaches like Momentum Contrast (MoCo) and SimCLR demonstrating promising results \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. MoCo introduces a dynamic dictionary through a queue and a moving-averaged encoder, enabling the construction of a large and consistent dictionary for contrastive learning \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. SimCLR, on the other hand, incorporates design improvements such as an MLP projection head and enhanced data augmentation, which contribute to its effectiveness \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\]. Recent work, such as Prototypical Contrastive Learning (PCL), further advances the field by addressing limitations in instance-wise contrastive learning through the use of prototypes as latent variables within an Expectation-Maximization framework \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\]. PCL not only improves low-level feature learning but also implicitly encodes semantic structures into the embedding space, leading to state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of contrastive learning, emphasizing the importance of architectural innovations and data augmentation strategies in improving model performance. The integration of these techniques into frameworks like MoCo has enabled stronger baselines that outperform earlier models without requiring large training batches \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04297)\]. Overall, these methods underscore the potential of contrastive learning in achieving competitive results across various tasks, including visual and genomic representation learning.
