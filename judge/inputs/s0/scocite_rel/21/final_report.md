## Related Works

Video frame interpolation and neural rendering have both seen significant advancements in recent years, with a growing emphasis on leveraging deep learning techniques to improve performance and efficiency. Video frame interpolation, which involves generating intermediate frames between two consecutive video frames, has been extensively studied. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation followed by pixel synthesis, but these approaches often struggled with occlusion, blur, and large motion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. More recent methods, such as Super SloMo \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\], have introduced end-to-end convolutional neural networks that jointly model motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning, achieving better results by refining flow estimates and using visibility maps to avoid artifacts. Similarly, the work in \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\] proposes a method that combines motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a single convolution process, enabling high-quality interpolation while gracefully handling challenges like occlusion and blur.

Neural rendering, which aims to reconstruct 3D scenes from 2D images, has also benefited from deep learning techniques. Traditional methods often require explicit 3D supervision, which is difficult to obtain for real-world datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\]. However, recent approaches, such as Deep Stereo \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\], have demonstrated the potential of end-to-end learning from posed image sets, enabling the synthesis of new views directly from pixels. This aligns with the core idea of the proposed PS4PRO model, which uses video frame interpolation as a data augmentation method for neural rendering. The work in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\] introduces Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF), a new neural scene representation that enables fast and high-quality free-viewpoint rendering by learning sparse voxel structures from posed RGB images. This is similar to the approach taken in PS4PRO, where the model implicitly models camera movement and real-world 3D geometry.

In addition to these, several works have explored the use of adaptive convolutions for video frame interpolation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. These methods allow for the estimation of spatially adaptive kernels that capture both motion and pixel synthesis, leading to improved performance. Video frame interpolation techniques have evolved significantly, with recent approaches focusing on deep learning and end-to-end training to improve accuracy and efficiency. Traditional methods rely on optical flow estimation and motion compensation, which can struggle with complex scenarios like occlusion, blur, and large motion \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. Modern methods, such as those using convolutional neural networks, aim to jointly model motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning, often employing U-Net architectures for flow estimation and refinement \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. These networks can generate intermediate frames by warping and fusing input images, using visibility maps to exclude occluded pixels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Unsupervised learning techniques have also been introduced, leveraging cycle consistency to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring ground-truth data \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\]. Additionally, methods like IM-Net focus on high-resolution performance, using structured architectures and multi-scale losses to achieve real-time results \[[30](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\]. These advancements highlight the shift towards more robust and efficient video frame interpolation techniques that can handle a wide range of motion and scene complexities.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Photo-realistic free-viewpoint rendering of real-world scenes using classical computer graphics techniques is challenging, because it requires the difficult step of capturing detailed appearance and geometry models. Recent studies have demonstrated promising results by learning scene representations that implicitly encode both geometry and appearance without 3D supervision. Furthermore, by utilizing an explicit sparse voxel representation, our method can easily be applied to scene editing and scene composition. We also demonstrate several challenging tasks, including multi-scene learning, free-viewpoint rendering of a moving human, and large-scale scene rendering.»

### <a id='category-neural-rendering-methods'></a>Neural Rendering Methods: 7 papers found.
Neural rendering methods have seen significant advancements in reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images, with a focus on improving geometry and appearance modeling without explicit 3D supervision \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02190)\]. Techniques like Scene Representation Networks (SRNs) leverage differentiable ray-marching to learn continuous 3D-structure-aware representations from 2D images and camera poses, enabling novel view synthesis and few-shot reconstruction \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. Similarly, pixelNeRF introduces a fully convolutional architecture that conditions NeRF on input images, allowing for efficient training across multiple scenes and enabling novel view synthesis from sparse views \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02190)\]. Extensions to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), such as NeRF-W, address limitations in uncontrolled environments by incorporating temporal consistency and handling variable illumination \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02268)\]. Implicit representations have also gained traction, with methods like those proposed in \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\] unifying surface and volume rendering to enable accurate surface reconstruction without per-pixel masks. NeuS further improves surface reconstruction by modeling surfaces as zero-level sets of signed distance functions (SDFs) and developing a bias-free volume rendering method \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the trend toward implicit, continuous representations that enhance reconstruction quality and flexibility in neural rendering.

### <a id='category-adaptive-convolution-architectures'></a>Adaptive Convolution Architectures: 2 papers found.
Adaptive convolution architectures have been increasingly utilized in video frame interpolation to address the limitations of traditional methods. Standard approaches typically estimate optical flow and synthesize intermediate frames separately, but recent work has merged these steps into a single convolution process using spatially adaptive kernels \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. However, large kernels required for handling significant motion lead to high memory demands and limit the number of pixels that can be processed simultaneously \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed local separable convolutions, which use pairs of 1D kernels instead of 2D kernels, significantly reducing the number of parameters to be estimated \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. This approach enables the development of deep fully convolutional neural networks that can estimate kernels and synthesize entire video frames at once, allowing for the incorporation of perceptual loss to enhance visual quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Additionally, simpler adaptive separable convolutions have been refined with low-level improvements to achieve near state-of-the-art results, demonstrating their effectiveness in frame interpolation and potential applicability to related tasks such as burst image denoising and video prediction \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01280)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of adaptive convolution architectures in improving the efficiency and quality of video frame interpolation techniques.
