## Related Works

Video frame interpolation and neural rendering have both seen significant advancements in recent years, with a growing emphasis on leveraging deep learning techniques to improve performance and efficiency. Video frame interpolation, which involves generating intermediate frames between two consecutive video frames, has been extensively studied. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation followed by pixel synthesis, but these approaches often struggled with occlusion, blur, and large motion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. More recent methods, such as Super SloMo \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\], have introduced end-to-end convolutional neural networks that jointly model motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning, achieving better results by refining flow estimates and using visibility maps to avoid artifacts. Similarly, the work in \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\] proposes a method that combines motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a single convolution process, enabling high-quality interpolation while gracefully handling challenges like occlusion and blur.

Neural rendering, which aims to reconstruct 3D scenes from 2D images, has also benefited from deep learning techniques. Traditional methods often require explicit 3D supervision, which is difficult to obtain for real-world datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\]. However, recent approaches, such as Deep Stereo \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\], have demonstrated the potential of end-to-end learning from posed image sets, enabling the synthesis of new views directly from pixels. This aligns with the core idea of the proposed PS4PRO model, which uses video frame interpolation as a data augmentation method for neural rendering. The work in \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\] introduces Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF), a new neural scene representation that enables fast and high-quality free-viewpoint rendering by learning sparse voxel structures from posed RGB images. This is similar to the approach taken in PS4PRO, where the model implicitly models camera movement and real-world 3D geometry.

In addition to these, several works have explored the use of adaptive convolutions for video frame interpolation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. These methods allow for the estimation of spatially adaptive kernels that capture both motion and pixel synthesis, leading to improved performance. The work in \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a285a442317f24068f7cc262531976e82a5c41d6)\] further refines this by using separable convolutions, reducing the number of parameters and enabling efficient training. The idea of using depth information to improve interpolation quality has also been explored, as seen in \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\], where a depth-aware flow projection layer is introduced to synthesize intermediate flows that preferentially sample closer objects.
