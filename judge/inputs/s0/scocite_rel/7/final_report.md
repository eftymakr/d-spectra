## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit behaviors such as refusal, alignment, and steering, often through mechanisms embedded in their activation space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Traditional approaches to identifying refusal behavior rely on predefined templates detectable in output tokens or require manual analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. However, these methods are limited in their generalizability and often depend on assumptions about the presence of specific refusal tokens \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. Recent work has focused on understanding the internal mechanisms of LLMs, particularly the one-dimensional subspace that mediates refusal behavior across models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. This subspace is critical for understanding how models decide to refuse or comply with instructions, and it has been shown to be a key factor in the brittleness of current safety fine-tuning methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\].

In addition to refusal, researchers have explored methods to steer LLMs toward desired behaviors. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) is an innovative approach that modifies model activations during inference to steer behavior, such as increasing factual accuracy or reducing hallucination \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. CAA computes steering vectors by averaging the difference in residual stream activations between positive and negative examples of a behavior, and then adds these vectors during inference to alter model outputs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\]. This method has been shown to be effective across a range of tasks and is minimally invasive, with little impact on model capabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681)\].

Another approach to improving truthfulness in LLMs is Inference-Time Intervention (ITI), which shifts model activations during inference to enhance truthfulness. ITI has been shown to significantly improve the performance of LLaMA models on the TruthfulQA benchmark, increasing truthfulness from 32.5% to 65.1% \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\]. This technique operates by modifying a limited number of attention heads during inference, demonstrating that even small interventions can have a substantial impact on model behavior \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03341)\].

Adversarial attacks on aligned LLMs have also been a focus of recent research. The Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks paper introduces a method that automatically generates adversarial suffixes to induce objectionable content in aligned models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. These attacks are transferable across different models and even to black-box systems, highlighting the vulnerabilities in current alignment techniques \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\].

Furthermore, the development of tools like Toolformer has enabled LLMs to use external APIs to perform complex tasks, improving their zero-shot performance without sacrificing core language modeling abilities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. 	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Conversational large language models are fine-tuned for both instruction-following and safety, resulting in models that obey benign requests but refuse harmful ones. Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «We introduce Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA), an innovative method for steering language models by modifying their activations during forward passes. CAA computes "steering vectors" by averaging the difference in residual stream activations between pairs of positive and negative examples of a particular behavior, such as factual versus hallucinatory responses. During inference, these steering vectors are added at all token positions after the user's prompt with either a positive or negative coefficient, allowing precise control over the degree of the targeted behavior. We evaluate CAA's effectiveness on Llama 2 Chat using multiple-choice behavioral question datasets and open-ended generation tasks. We demonstrate that CAA significantly alters model behavior, is effective over and on top of traditional methods like finetuning and system prompt design, and minimally reduces capabilities. Moreover, we gain deeper insights into CAA's mechanisms by employing various activation space interpretation methods. CAA accurately steers model outputs and sheds light on how high-level concepts are represented in Large Language Models (LLMs).»

### <a id='category-adversarial-attacks-on-aligned-models'></a>Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Models: 3 papers found.
The category "Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Models" encompasses research focused on exploiting vulnerabilities in large language models (LLMs) that have been aligned to follow ethical guidelines and avoid generating harmful content. This includes methods such as adversarial suffixes that can be appended to queries to elicit objectionable responses from aligned models, often bypassing safety mechanisms \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. These attacks are typically automated, leveraging techniques like greedy and gradient-based search to generate effective prompts without requiring manual engineering \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. Additionally, genetic algorithms have been employed to create universal adversarial prompts that can manipulate LLMs even when their internal architecture and parameters are not accessible, leading to unintended and harmful outputs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.01446)\]. Such work highlights the challenges in ensuring robust alignment, as these attacks can transfer across different models and even to black-box systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15043)\]. Furthermore, broader risk analyses have identified various potential harms associated with LLMs, including discrimination, misinformation, and environmental impacts, underscoring the need for comprehensive mitigation strategies \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04359)\]. These findings collectively emphasize the ongoing arms race between alignment techniques and adversarial methods, with implications for the safety and ethical deployment of LLMs.

One hypothesised cause of polysemanticality is \textit(superposition), where neural networks represent more features than they have neurons by assigning features to an overcomplete set of directions in activation space, rather than to individual neurons.
