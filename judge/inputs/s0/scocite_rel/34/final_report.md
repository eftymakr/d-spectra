## Related Works

Time series forecasting has been a cornerstone in financial applications, with recent advancements focusing on improving accuracy and robustness in the presence of uncertainty. Traditional approaches such as autoregressive models and Gaussian processes have been widely used but often struggle with capturing complex temporal dependencies \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. The introduction of deep learning frameworks has significantly enhanced forecasting capabilities, particularly with the advent of Transformer-based models \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\]. These models leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies, making them suitable for both short- and long-term forecasting tasks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. However, the application of Transformers to time series forecasting is not without challenges, including computational inefficiency and the inability to effectively model non-stationary data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of Transformers for time series forecasting, with notable contributions such as PatchTST, which introduces a segmentation strategy to reduce computational complexity while retaining local semantic information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, iTransformer redefines the attention mechanism to better handle multivariate time series by inverting the dimensions of the input \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. These models have demonstrated improved performance on real-world datasets, particularly in scenarios requiring long-term forecasting \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. However, the effectiveness of Transformers in time series analysis is still debated, with some studies suggesting that simple linear models can outperform complex Transformer-based architectures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. This highlights the need for further investigation into the validity of Transformer-based approaches in time series forecasting.

### <a id='category-transformer-based-time-series-models'></a>Transformer-Based Time Series Models: 11 papers found.
The related works in the field of Transformer-based time series models focus on improving forecasting accuracy, handling long-term dependencies, and addressing computational inefficiencies. Several studies have proposed modifications to the Transformer architecture to better suit time series data, such as PatchTST, which introduces a channel-independent patching design to retain local semantic information and reduce computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)\]. Similarly, iTransformer restructures the attention mechanism by inverting dimensions, allowing for more effective modeling of multivariate correlations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06625)\]. However, recent work has questioned the validity of these complex models, with LTSF-Linear demonstrating superior performance over sophisticated Transformer-based models on real-world datasets \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)\]. Additionally, challenges such as the memory bottleneck and locality-agnostic issues in canonical Transformers have been addressed through innovations like convolutional self-attention and LogSparse Transformers, which improve efficiency and accuracy for long-term forecasting \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. Pre-trained models, such as Frozen Pretrained Transformers (FPT), have also been explored for time series analysis, leveraging large-scale pre-training from NLP or CV domains to achieve comparable or state-of-the-art results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14415)\]. Furthermore, frameworks like Chronos and Autoformer have been developed to enhance zero-shot performance and long-term forecasting accuracy, respectively, by introducing novel mechanisms such as Auto-Correlation and probabilistic modeling \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.07125)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13008)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to refine Transformer-based approaches for time series modeling, balancing performance, efficiency, and adaptability.

### <a id='category-time-series-forecasting-models'></a>Time Series Forecasting Models: 6 papers found.
Time series forecasting models have seen significant advancements with the integration of deep learning techniques, addressing limitations of traditional methods such as Autoregressive models and Gaussian Processes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Recent approaches like DeepAR leverage recurrent neural networks trained on large-scale time series data to improve forecast accuracy, achieving up to 15% improvements over state-of-the-art methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04110)\]. Similarly, LSTNet combines CNN and RNN components to capture both short-term local dependencies and long-term trends, while also incorporating autoregressive models to handle scale insensitivity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07015)\]. The TimesNet framework further enhances temporal variation modeling by transforming 1D time series into 2D tensors, enabling more effective modeling of intraperiod and interperiod variations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02186)\]. Additionally, N-BEATS demonstrates that deep learning primitives such as residual blocks can achieve state-of-the-art performance without domain-specific components, showing strong generalization across diverse datasets \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10437)\]. The TimeMixer model introduces a novel multiscale-mixing approach, decomposing time series into microscopic and macroscopic patterns to improve forecasting accuracy in both short- and long-term tasks \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14616)\]. These developments highlight the growing trend of using deep learning to overcome the complexities of real-world time series, offering more accurate and adaptable forecasting solutions.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Transformers have shown great power in time series forecasting due to their global-range modeling ability. However, their performance can degenerate terribly on non-stationary real-world data in which the joint distribution changes over time. Previous studies primarily adopt stationarization to attenuate the non-stationarity of original series for better predictability. But the stationarized series deprived of inherent non-stationarity can be less instructive for real-world bursty events forecasting. This problem, termed over-stationarization in this paper, leads Transformers to generate indistinguishable temporal attentions for different series and impedes the predictive capability of deep models. To tackle the dilemma between series predictability and model capability, we propose Non-stationary Transformers as a generic framework with two interdependent modules: Series Stationarization and De-stationary Attention. Concretly, Series Stationarization unifies the statistics of each input and converts the output with restored statistics for better predictability. To address the over-stationarization problem, De-stationary Attention is devised to recover the intrinsic non-stationary information into temporal dependencies by approximating distinguishable attentions learned from raw series. Our Non-stationary Transformers framework consistently boosts mainstream Transformers by a large margin, which reduces MSE by 49.43% on Transformer, 47.34% on Informer, and 46.89% on Reformer, making them the state-of-the-art in time series forecasting. ### <a id='category-large-language-models-in-forecasting'></a>Large Language Models in Forecasting: 3 papers found.
Recent research has explored the application of large language models (LLMs) in time series forecasting, particularly in financial contexts. Several studies have demonstrated the potential of LLMs to address challenges such as cross-sequence reasoning, multi-modal signal integration, and model interpretability \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. For instance, one study focused on NASDAQ-100 stocks, utilizing historical stock price data, company metadata, and financial news to evaluate LLMs like GPT-4 and Open LLaMA, showing their ability to reason over textual and price data and generate explainable forecasts \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11025)\]. Another work introduced Time-LLM, a framework that reprograms LLMs for time series forecasting by aligning modalities through text prototypes and prompt-as-prefix techniques, achieving superior performance over specialized models \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01728)\]. However, not all studies have found LLMs to be beneficial; some ablation studies suggest that removing the LLM component or replacing it with simpler attention layers can sometimes improve forecasting performance, indicating that LLMs may not always be essential for time series tasks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16964)\]. These findings highlight the ongoing debate and exploration into the effectiveness of LLMs in time series forecasting, with varying results depending on the specific application and methodology used.
