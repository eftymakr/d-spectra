## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have become a cornerstone of modern information retrieval systems, particularly in Web search applications \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.06902)\]. These models leverage deep learning to learn representations of queries and documents that enable effective ranking \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06467)\]. However, training such models typically requires substantial amounts of labeled query-document pairs, which are expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Recent advances have explored the use of synthetic queries generated by large language models as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. While this approach offers promise, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal downstream retrieval performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Existing methods typically address this issue by filtering out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This process, however, introduces additional complexity and may not fully capture the nuances of query-document relevance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, we propose a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO has been shown to improve the alignment of large language models with human preferences by directly optimizing the model to generate high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach eliminates the need for an external re-ranker and streamlines the training process \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

DPO builds on the broader field of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which has been widely used to align language models with human preferences \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. RLHF typically involves two stages: first, training a reward model to reflect human preferences, and second, fine-tuning the language model using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. While effective, RLHF is often complex and computationally intensive \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO simplifies this process by introducing a new parameterization of the reward model that enables the extraction of the corresponding optimal policy in closed form, allowing for a simple classification loss \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This makes DPO more computationally lightweight and easier to implement compared to traditional RLHF methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Additionally, DPO has been shown to outperform existing methods in controlling the sentiment of generated responses and improving response quality in summarization and single-turn dialogue \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This is particularly important in the context of retrieval tasks, where the quality of generated queries directly impacts the effectiveness of downstream models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. By integrating DPO into the query generation process, we aim to directly optimize the model towards generating high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 3 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) is a framework designed to improve the alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human preferences by simplifying the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) process. Unlike traditional RLHF, which requires complex reward modeling and reinforcement learning steps, DPO introduces a new parameterization of the reward model that allows for the extraction of an optimal policy in closed form, enabling the use of a simple classification loss \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach eliminates the need for sampling from the LM during fine-tuning and reduces the reliance on hyperparameter tuning, making the process more stable and computationally efficient \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO has been shown to perform as well as or better than existing methods in aligning LMs with human preferences, particularly in controlling sentiment and improving response quality in tasks like summarization and dialogue \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, recent studies have identified potential limitations of the standard DPO loss, such as the risk of reducing the likelihood of preferred examples if the relative probability between preferred and dispreferred responses is not carefully managed \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed DPO-Positive (DPOP), a modified loss function that avoids this failure mode and achieves better performance across various datasets and tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. Additionally, methods like Direct Large Model Alignment (DLMA) have been developed to align LLMs without human-annotated preference data by leveraging contrastive prompt pairs and self-rewarding scores, demonstrating that DPO-based approaches can be adapted to different alignment strategies \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11907)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of DPO as a promising technique for improving the alignment of LLMs with human preferences while maintaining efficiency and effectiveness.

### <a id='category-reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback-(rlhf)-methods'></a>Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) Methods: 5 papers found.
The Related Works section for the paper's category on Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) Methods highlights several key approaches and their limitations. RLHF typically relies on two approximations: substituting pairwise preferences with pointwise rewards and assuming reward models generalize to out-of-distribution data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) addresses the second approximation by eliminating the reward modeling stage but still depends on the first \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Recent work introduces $\Psi$PO, a new general objective that bypasses both approximations by directly using pairwise preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach enables a deeper analysis of RLHF and DPO, identifying potential pitfalls \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Additionally, PPO, a policy gradient method, alternates between data collection and optimization, offering better sample efficiency and simplicity compared to TRPO \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. In summarization tasks, training models to optimize human preferences using reward models derived from human comparisons leads to significant improvements over ROUGE-based metrics \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)\]. Furthermore, RLHF has been applied to fine-tune language models to act as helpful assistants, demonstrating compatibility with specialized skills and robustness through iterated online training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. The ability to train complex behaviors with minimal human oversight, such as in Atari games and robotic locomotion, showcases the flexibility of preference-based RL methods \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the importance of integrating human preferences directly into the learning process to enhance model performance and alignment with user goals.

### <a id='category-neural-retrieval-models-and-their-training-challenges'></a>Neural Retrieval Models and Their Training Challenges: 8 papers found.
The category "Neural Retrieval Models and Their Training Challenges" encompasses a range of research focused on improving the efficiency, effectiveness, and scalability of neural retrieval models. These models, such as BERT-based systems and dense retrieval (DR) models, have shown significant improvements in ranking performance by leveraging deep learning techniques \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04085)\]. However, training these models remains challenging due to the reliance on large amounts of labeled data and the computational cost associated with fine-tuning deep language models for document ranking \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various strategies, including the use of contextualized language models like ELMo and BERT to enhance ad-hoc document ranking \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07094)\], as well as the development of efficient retrieval frameworks such as ColBERT, which introduces a late interaction architecture to reduce computational overhead \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. Additionally, the problem of static hard negative sampling in DR models has been identified, leading to the proposal of dynamic training strategies like STAR and ADORE to improve stability and ranking performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. Furthermore, the integration of vector compression techniques, such as Product Quantization (PQ), has been explored to enhance the efficiency of retrieval systems without compromising performance \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00644)\]. These efforts highlight the ongoing challenges and innovations in training neural retrieval models, emphasizing the need for more efficient and effective training strategies \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.06902)\].
