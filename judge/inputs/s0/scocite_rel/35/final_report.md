## Related Works

Protein dynamics are essential for understanding biological functions and properties, yet traditional methods such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are computationally expensive and time-consuming \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Recent advances in generative modeling have enabled more efficient and accurate protein structure prediction and conformation sampling by learning distributions over crystallographic structures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. However, integrating physical supervision into these data-driven approaches remains a challenge, as standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges and contribute to the field of protein conformational sampling and structure prediction.

Several studies have explored the use of deep learning for protein structure prediction and conformational sampling. For instance, Boltzmann generators use neural networks to learn coordinate transformations that enable efficient sampling of equilibrium states of many-body systems \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. These models are trained to provide unbiased, one-shot samples from the equilibrium distribution, which is crucial for capturing the conformational diversity of proteins \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dc32a984b651256a8ec282be52310e6bd33d9815)\]. Similarly, EigenFold employs a diffusion process to model protein structures as a system of harmonic oscillators, enabling the generation of conformational ensembles \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\]. This approach has been shown to provide a more comprehensive picture of model uncertainty compared to existing methods \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\].

Another notable work is Chroma, a generative model that can directly sample novel protein structures and sequences while being conditioned to steer the generative process towards desired properties and functions \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c49a0912595a1cc70aab63524f64ed08c92194a8)\]. Chroma leverages a diffusion process that respects the conformational statistics of polymer ensembles and uses an efficient neural architecture for molecular systems \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c49a0912595a1cc70aab63524f64ed08c92194a8)\]. This allows for long-range reasoning with sub-quadratic scaling, making it suitable for generating complex protein structures \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c49a0912595a1cc70aab63524f64ed08c92194a8)\].

In addition, the development of AlphaFold has significantly advanced the field of protein structure prediction \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. AlphaFold2 has demonstrated the ability to predict protein structures with atomic accuracy even in cases where no similar structure is known \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. This has been achieved through a novel machine learning approach that incorporates physical and biological knowledge about protein structure \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03117)\]. Furthermore, AlphaFold3 has been extended to predict the joint structure of complexes including proteins, nucleic acids, small molecules, ions, and modified residues \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\].

### <a id='category-deep-learning-approaches-for-conformational-sampling'></a>Deep Learning Approaches for Conformational Sampling: 4 papers found.
Recent advances in deep learning have significantly impacted the field of conformational sampling for proteins, offering efficient and accurate methods to generate equilibrium distributions of molecular systems \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Traditional approaches such as molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are computationally expensive and often infeasible for complex systems, necessitating the development of alternative strategies \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. Machine learning models, particularly generative adversarial networks (GANs) and invertible neural networks, have been leveraged to transform simple distributions into complex ones that reflect the equilibrium states of proteins \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. These models learn coordinate transformations that enable the sampling of configurations in a simplified latent space, which can then be mapped back to the original system \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to both coarse-grained and atomistic simulations, demonstrating the potential for generating conformational ensembles with high physical plausibility \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Furthermore, frameworks like the Distributional Graphormer (DiG) have been introduced to predict equilibrium distributions by conditioning on molecular descriptors such as chemical graphs or protein sequences \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. These models not only generate diverse conformations but also provide estimations of state densities, enhancing the statistical understanding of molecular systems \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\]. The integration of physical supervision into these data-driven models, as exemplified by the Energy-based Alignment (EBA) method, has further improved the accuracy and reliability of generated structures by aligning generative models with feedback from physical models \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Overall, these developments highlight the growing role of deep learning in overcoming the limitations of traditional methods and advancing the study of protein dynamics and structural biology \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]\[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2eb1ed9d4baf548391d8220c0a8a3d0be79845ed)\]\[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05445)\].

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-for-protein-structure-generation'></a>Diffusion Models for Protein Structure Generation: 7 papers found.
Recent advances in diffusion models have significantly enhanced the ability to generate and sample protein structures, addressing the limitations of traditional molecular dynamics simulations \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c49a0912595a1cc70aab63524f64ed08c92194a8)\]. These models, such as EigenFold \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\], Chroma \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\], and others, leverage diffusion processes to model the conformational ensembles and flexibility essential for biological function. For instance, EigenFold introduces a diffusion process that models proteins as harmonic oscillators, enabling the generation of diverse structures while capturing model uncertainty \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02198)\]. Similarly, Chroma employs a diffusion process that respects conformational statistics of polymer ensembles, allowing for the design of proteins with desired properties and functions \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2cfc26b4af99f195b433fa7aa00f221b111c7cd4)\]. Other approaches, such as the diffusion-based model presented in \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15611)\], generate protein backbone structures by denoising from an unfolded state, mirroring the natural folding process and achieving high realism. Additionally, the work in \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15019)\] introduces a generative model capable of producing full-atom backbone configurations along with sequence and side-chain predictions, demonstrating the scalability of these methods. Furthermore, the integration of physical priors into diffusion models, as seen in ConfDiff \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14088)\], ensures that generated conformations remain physically plausible by incorporating force-guided networks. These developments highlight the growing trend of using diffusion models to not only predict but also generate novel protein structures with high accuracy and functional relevance.

### <a id='category-alphafold-and-its-extensions'></a>AlphaFold and Its Extensions: 8 papers found.
AlphaFold and its extensions have significantly advanced the field of protein structure prediction, addressing longstanding challenges in computational biology. These models leverage deep learning architectures, incorporating evolutionary information from multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) to predict high-resolution protein structures with atomic accuracy, even in the absence of homologous structures \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. The introduction of AlphaFold2 marked a major breakthrough, achieving results comparable to experimental methods in the CASP14 competition \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\]. Subsequent developments, such as AlphaFold3, further expanded the scope by enabling the prediction of complex biomolecular assemblies, including proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules, with improved accuracy over specialized tools \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7572ba7f604ef95d7acdd657ebac458106bd35df)\]. Additionally, models like OmegaFold have addressed the challenge of predicting structures from single sequences, bypassing the need for MSAs, which is particularly useful for orphan proteins and fast-evolving proteins like antibodies \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/867d80c8779e1d301a5fc6e267e263f7e4c4c5c7)\]. The ability to generate diverse conformations and structural ensembles has also been explored, with methods such as AlphaFlow and ESMFlow offering enhanced sampling of conformational landscapes, capturing flexibility and higher-order ensemble properties \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04845)\]. These advancements highlight the growing capacity of deep learning to model not only static structures but also dynamic aspects of proteins, paving the way for more accurate and comprehensive structural bioinformatics tools \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ff9b84297f16760472c791456befb31ff1d224ef)\].

ProteinMPNN, a deep learning-based method, demonstrates outstanding performance in both in silico and experimental tests, achieving a sequence recovery of 52.4% on native protein backbones, surpassing Rosetta's 32.9% \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/98926d43356e87c22c82efc132dcaaac1ff40ebe)\]. Similarly, unsupervised learning has been applied to protein sequence data to learn representations that capture fundamental biological properties such as secondary structure, contacts, and biological activity \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18a93dc1558bf9d7534d0b416633cebaf75c1145)\]. These representations, derived solely from sequence data, have shown utility in benchmarks for remote homology detection, secondary structure prediction, and long-range contact prediction \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18a93dc1558bf9d7534d0b416633cebaf75c1145)\]. Additionally, FoldFlow introduces a series of generative models based on the flow-matching paradigm over SE(3), enabling accurate modeling of protein backbones with improved stability and training efficiency compared to diffusion-based approaches \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02391)\]. These models generate high-quality, diverse, and novel protein structures, further advancing the field of generative modeling in protein structure prediction. The integration of physical supervision into data-driven approaches, as demonstrated by Energy-based Alignment (EBA), highlights ongoing efforts to enhance the physical plausibility of generated structures, aligning them with feedback from physical models \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/21a4afa74caf78e28880b7fe4c30a09a5f9c14e3)\].
