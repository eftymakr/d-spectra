## Related Works

Fairness in machine learning has been a growing concern, particularly as algorithms are increasingly used in high-stakes decision-making scenarios. Early work on fairness focused on statistical definitions, such as parity of false positive and false negative rates across different demographic groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. However, these definitions often fail to capture the nuanced ways in which bias can manifest, especially when considering model uncertainty \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. For instance, a model might have equal accuracy across groups but exhibit higher confidence in incorrect predictions for one group, leading to disparate treatment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. This issue is particularly relevant in the context of large language models (LLMs), where model confidence can significantly influence the perceived fairness of predictions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\].

Several studies have highlighted the limitations of conventional fairness metrics, which often rely on discrete accuracy-based evaluations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. These metrics fail to account for the implicit impact of model uncertainty, which can lead to biased outcomes even when accuracy is balanced \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed alternative fairness definitions that incorporate uncertainty, such as the concept of counterfactual fairness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. This framework suggests that a decision is fair if it remains consistent across different demographic groups \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. However, applying such definitions in practice remains challenging, particularly when dealing with complex models like LLMs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\].

The importance of diverse and representative datasets for evaluating fairness has also been emphasized. Many existing datasets suffer from issues such as limited diversity, lack of clarity, and insufficient size, which can hinder the development of robust fairness benchmarks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. In response, new datasets have been introduced to better reflect the complexities of real-world applications. For example, the UCI Adult dataset has been widely used to study fairness in predictive models, though it has limitations in terms of external validity \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\]. More recently, researchers have proposed new datasets that span multiple domains and include a broader range of demographic groups \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\].

To further improve fairness evaluation, some studies have explored adversarial learning techniques to mitigate unwanted biases in models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08843)\]. These methods aim to reduce the ability of an adversary to infer protected attributes from model predictions, thereby promoting fairness \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. However, achieving fairness often comes at the cost of reduced model performance, highlighting the inherent trade-offs between fairness and accuracy \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. This trade-off is particularly relevant in the context of LLMs, where maintaining high accuracy is crucial for practical applications \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1605.07139)\].

### <a id='category-fairness-metrics-and-definitions'></a>Fairness Metrics and Definitions: 10 papers found.
The concept of fairness in machine learning has been extensively explored, with researchers focusing on defining and measuring fairness to prevent discriminatory outcomes in automated decision-making systems. Various fairness criteria have been proposed, such as error rate balance, statistical parity, and counterfactual fairness, which aim to ensure that decisions are not biased against certain demographic groups \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\]. These definitions often rely on statistical measures derived from the joint distribution of features, outcomes, and protected attributes, but they have limitations in capturing the nuanced and causal relationships that underlie discrimination \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. Some studies have highlighted the challenges of achieving fairness in practice, particularly when recidivism prevalence differs across groups, making it impossible to satisfy all fairness criteria simultaneously \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)\]. Additionally, the use of historical data for training models can perpetuate existing biases, leading to disparate mistreatment based on misclassification rates across social groups \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08452)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed fairness-enhancing mechanisms, including pre-process, in-process, and post-process approaches, which aim to mitigate bias while maintaining model utility \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. The debate around fairness in classification has also raised questions about the compatibility of different fairness notions, suggesting that achieving perfect fairness may be inherently difficult or impossible without making strong assumptions about the data-generating process \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)\]. These discussions underscore the importance of developing more robust and context-aware fairness metrics that can better reflect the complexities of real-world decision-making \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\].

### <a id='category-challenges-in-implementing-fairness-metrics'></a>Challenges in Implementing Fairness Metrics: 2 papers found.
The challenges in implementing fairness metrics in machine learning are well-documented, with a particular focus on the limitations of statistical definitions of fairness. These definitions often rely on pre-defined groups and require parity of statistics across them, which can lead to "fairness gerrymandering" where classifiers appear fair on individual groups but fail on structured subgroups \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. This issue highlights the need for more robust fairness definitions that account for an exponential or infinite number of subgroups, as defined by structured functions over protected attributes \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. Such an approach interpolates between statistical and individual notions of fairness but introduces computational challenges, as auditing for subgroup fairness is equivalent to weak agnostic learning, which is computationally hard in the worst case \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. Additionally, the practical implementation of fairness metrics is further complicated by the lack of alignment between academic research and industry needs, as evidenced by studies showing that existing tools often fail to address real-world challenges faced by ML practitioners \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05239)\]. These findings underscore the importance of developing fairness metrics that are both theoretically sound and practically applicable, ensuring that they can effectively mitigate bias in real-world systems.

### <a id='category-ethical-and-philosophical-considerations'></a>Ethical and Philosophical Considerations: 2 papers found.
The concept of fairness in machine learning has been extensively explored, drawing on philosophical and ethical frameworks to operationalize the idea of fairness. Scholars have debated whether fairness should ensure equal opportunity for all or focus on minimizing harm to the least advantaged, with differing assumptions about discrimination and justice \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.03586)\]. This debate has influenced the development of fairness metrics, which can be categorized into group fairness and individual fairness, often perceived as conflicting \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06883)\]. However, recent research challenges this notion, arguing that these concepts are not inherently at odds but can be reconciled through a nuanced understanding of fairness rooted in egalitarian principles \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06883)\]. This aligns with broader discussions in political and legal philosophy, which emphasize the importance of context and the sources of unfairness in specific applications \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06883)\]. Such insights are crucial for developing fairness measures that are both mathematically rigorous and ethically grounded, particularly as machine learning systems become increasingly integrated into societal decision-making processes.

### <a id='category-adversarial-learning-for-fairness'></a>Adversarial Learning for Fairness: 2 papers found.
Adversarial learning for fairness has emerged as a promising approach to mitigate biases in machine learning models, particularly in high-stake applications where fairness is critical \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. This method involves training a predictor to learn a target variable while simultaneously training an adversary to infer a protected attribute, with the goal of minimizing the adversary's ability to predict the protected variable \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. By doing so, the model can produce more equitable predictions without sacrificing accuracy \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. This approach is flexible and can be adapted to various fairness definitions and learning models, including both regression and classification tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. Additionally, interpretability plays a key role in diagnosing the sources of algorithmic discrimination, as it allows for a deeper understanding of how models may encode biases \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08843)\]. These techniques align with broader efforts to improve fairness in deep learning by addressing biases at different stages of the model development lifecycle \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08843)\].
