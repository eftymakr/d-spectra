## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been a central topic in recent years, particularly due to the increasing reliance on neural networks in safety-critical applications. Early work on calibration focused on improving the reliability of confidence scores in classification tasks. For instance, it was observed that modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated, meaning that their predicted probabilities do not accurately reflect the true likelihood of correct predictions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue was attributed to factors such as network depth, width, weight decay, and the use of Batch Normalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. To address this, temperature scaling—a simple post-processing technique—was found to be surprisingly effective in calibrating predictions across various architectures and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\].

Further research has explored alternative calibration methods, such as focal loss, which not only improves calibration but also maintains high accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. Focal loss has been shown to be particularly effective when combined with temperature scaling, leading to state-of-the-art results in calibration without compromising accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\]. Additionally, more sophisticated calibration techniques have been proposed, such as Dirichlet calibration, which extends the beta calibration method to multiclass settings and provides a natively multiclass calibration approach \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\]. This method is particularly useful for models where accurate probability estimates are essential \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\].

The limitations of traditional calibration methods have also been highlighted. For example, histogram binning, while effective in some cases, is sample inefficient, requiring a large number of samples to estimate calibration error accurately \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. In contrast, scaling methods like temperature scaling are more efficient but may not always provide accurate calibration guarantees \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. To address these issues, the scaling-binning calibrator was introduced, which combines the benefits of both approaches by first applying a parametric function to reduce variance and then binning the results to ensure true calibration \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\].

Another line of work has focused on improving the expressiveness of calibration functions. For instance, the use of monotonic neural networks has been proposed to learn more flexible and accurate calibration functions while preserving the order of predictions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\]. This approach, known as the Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Network (UMNN), allows for arbitrary monotonic transformations, which can better capture the complex relationships between model outputs and true probabilities \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\]. This is particularly relevant to the current work, as it aligns with the goal of improving calibration through flexible and expressive calibrators \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\].
