## Related Works

Dense retrievers have emerged as a powerful alternative to traditional sparse retrieval methods in information retrieval tasks. These models leverage pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, LLaMA) and fine-tune them using contrastive learning to encode text into dense vector representations that can be compared via shallow similarity operations, such as inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This approach has led to significant improvements in retrieval performance, particularly in open-domain question answering and passage retrieval \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

Recent research has questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers, with some arguing that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning only adjusts neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This claim was initially tested using a BERT-based encoder with DPR as the representative dense retriever \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. However, this analysis was limited in scope, focusing only on a specific encoder architecture and dataset. To address this, we extend the previous study by evaluating different representation approaches, such as CLS token vs. mean pooling, and different backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. We also expand the dataset to include MSMARCO and Natural Questions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\].

Our findings confirm that pre-trained knowledge significantly underpins retrieval performance in DPR tuning, with fine-tuning primarily adjusting neuron activation rather than reorganizing knowledge. However, this pattern does not universally apply to all models. For instance, mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based models like LLaMA show different behavior, suggesting that the relationship between pre-training and fine-tuning may vary depending on the model architecture and representation method \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. These results highlight the importance of understanding how different pre-training strategies and model designs influence the effectiveness of fine-tuning in dense retrieval tasks.

Several prior works have explored the use of dense retrievers and their training methodologies. For example, Condenser is a pre-training architecture designed to improve dense retrieval by learning to condense information into dense vectors through LM pre-training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, coCondenser introduces an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. The ANCE model further improves dense retrieval by constructing negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index of the corpus, resolving the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of contrastive learning and efficient training strategies in enhancing dense retrievers.

### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-in-dense-retrieval'></a>Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval: 11 papers found.
The category "Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval" focuses on the development and optimization of pre-trained language models for dense retrieval tasks, where text is encoded into dense vectors for efficient comparison and retrieval. Early work introduced BERT, a bidirectional encoder that pre-trains on unlabeled text to capture bidirectional context, achieving state-of-the-art results on multiple NLP tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Subsequent research explored the limitations of standard language models for dense retrieval, highlighting issues such as the inability to effectively aggregate information into dense representations due to their internal attention structures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. To address this, novel architectures like Condenser were proposed, which pre-train models to condense information into dense vectors by conditioning LM predictions on dense representations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Further advancements introduced coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised contrastive loss to improve performance without requiring large batches or heavy data engineering \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Autoencoder-based models were also explored, where theoretical analyses showed that low reconstruction loss may not always yield good representations, prompting the use of weak decoders to improve encoder quality \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. Retrieval-Augmented Language Models (REALM) were introduced to enhance open-domain question answering by incorporating a latent knowledge retriever that allows models to access external documents during pre-training \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Additionally, the importance of pre-training tasks in dense retrieval was emphasized, with studies showing that paragraph-level tasks like Inverse Cloze Task (ICT) and Wiki Link Prediction (WLP) significantly improve performance over BM-25 and non-Transformer models \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. More recently, methods like RetroMAE and SimLM were developed, leveraging Masked Auto-Encoder (MAE) and similarity matching objectives to improve dense retrieval performance across benchmarks like BEIR and MS MARCO \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]\[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance dense retrieval through better pre-training strategies, model architectures, and contrastive learning techniques.

### <a id='category-efficient-training-strategies-for-retrieval'></a>Efficient Training Strategies for Retrieval: 6 papers found.
Efficient training strategies for dense retrieval models have been a focal point in recent research, addressing challenges such as the discrepancy between training and testing data distributions, the need for representative negative samples, and computational efficiency during training and inference. Several approaches have been proposed to improve the effectiveness and scalability of dense retrieval systems. For instance, ANCE introduces an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index to generate more realistic negative training instances, thereby reducing the gap between training and testing data distributions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Similarly, RocketQA enhances dense passage retrieval by employing cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives, and data augmentation, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets like MSMARCO and Natural Questions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. Additionally, TAS-Balanced introduces topic-aware query clustering and balanced margin sampling, enabling efficient training on a single GPU while achieving competitive performance in terms of NDCG@10 and low-latency retrieval \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\]. These methods collectively highlight the importance of carefully curated training strategies to improve model performance without excessive computational overhead. Furthermore, knowledge distillation techniques, such as DistilBERT, have been explored to create smaller, faster models that retain most of the performance of larger pre-trained models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01108)\]. Lastly, theoretical investigations into training strategies for dense retrievers have led to the development of dynamic sampling methods like STAR and ADORE, which improve training stability and ranking performance by addressing the limitations of static hard negative sampling \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. These advancements collectively contribute to the evolving landscape of efficient and effective dense retrieval systems.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-techniques'></a>Contrastive Learning Techniques: 4 papers found.
Contrastive learning techniques have emerged as a powerful approach for learning high-quality sentence embeddings without the need for labeled data, addressing a key limitation of traditional supervised methods \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. These techniques, such as those introduced in DeCLUTR, leverage self-supervised objectives to train models that can generalize across different domains and languages \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. SimCSE further advances this field by proposing a simple yet effective framework that achieves state-of-the-art results, with both unsupervised and supervised variants outperforming previous methods \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\]. The success of these approaches is attributed to their ability to regularize the embedding space, making it more uniform and better aligned with positive pairs, especially when supervised signals are available \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08821)\]. Additionally, Contrastive Predictive Coding offers a universal unsupervised learning approach that can extract useful representations from diverse data types, including text, images, and speech, demonstrating the versatility of contrastive learning \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Practical considerations, such as memory efficiency, have also been addressed through innovations like gradient caching, which allows for efficient training by decoupling backpropagation between the contrastive loss and the encoder \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.06983)\]. These developments collectively highlight the growing importance of contrastive learning in advancing the field of natural language processing and beyond.
