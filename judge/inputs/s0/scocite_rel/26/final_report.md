## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy-preserving data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for data privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to adversarial behaviors in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Specifically, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This issue has been explored in several works, including those that examine the limitations of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols. For instance, manipulation attacks have been shown to be a fundamental limitation of LDP, where a small fraction of users can obscure the distribution of honest users' input, especially when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Similarly, data poisoning attacks have been demonstrated to allow attackers to inject fake users and manipulate the output of LDP protocols to skew estimations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These findings highlight the need for robust verification mechanisms in DP systems.

Several works have addressed the problem of ensuring correctness in DP mechanisms. For example, the shuffled model of differential privacy has been proposed as a middle ground between the local and central models, offering improved accuracy while maintaining user privacy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. This model leverages an anonymous channel to shuffle user inputs, allowing for more efficient and accurate computation of statistics \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Additionally, verifiable randomization mechanisms have been introduced to prevent malicious data providers from manipulating their output before sending it to the data collector \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. These mechanisms enable the data collector to verify that the randomization process was executed correctly, thereby preventing output-manipulation attacks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\].

In the context of verifiable DP, zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) have been explored as a potential tool for ensuring the correctness of DP protocols. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as alternative approaches can achieve the same goals with lower computational overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This has led to the development of novel mechanisms such as the Verifiable Distributed Discrete Laplacian Mechanism (VDDLM), which offers significant improvements in proof generation efficiency compared to existing verifiable differentially private mechanisms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Similarly, an improved solution to Verifiable Randomized Response (VRR) under local DP has been proposed, achieving up to a 5000x reduction in communication costs and the verifier's overhead \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\].

### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-limitations-and-attacks'></a>Local Differential Privacy Limitations and Attacks: 6 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has been widely studied and deployed in various systems to protect user privacy during data collection \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. However, it is vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can obscure the true distribution of data, especially under high privacy levels or large domain sizes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This vulnerability is further exacerbated by data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users to manipulate frequency estimates or heavy hitter identifications \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. Additionally, existing LDP protocols for frequency estimation often lack optimal accuracy, despite post-processing steps that leverage non-negativity and normalization constraints \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. While some LDP mechanisms have been designed for repeated data collection, they often fail to maintain strong privacy guarantees over time, leading to degraded performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. Recent work has introduced new mechanisms, such as hashed K-ary Randomized Response (KRR), which improve utility across all privacy levels \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07387)\]. These findings highlight the ongoing challenges in ensuring robustness and accuracy in LDP protocols, especially in the face of adversarial behaviors \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07387)\].

### <a id='category-privacy-amplification-techniques'></a>Privacy Amplification Techniques: 6 papers found.
Privacy amplification techniques have been extensively studied to enhance the privacy guarantees of differentially private algorithms, particularly in the context of local differential privacy (LDP). These techniques aim to reduce the privacy cost of LDP protocols by leveraging the anonymity of user reports, allowing for a more favorable privacy analysis in the central model of differential privacy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. By demonstrating that permutation-invariant LDP algorithms can satisfy a stronger central differential privacy guarantee, these methods reveal that the high noise and overhead typically associated with LDP may be an artifact of its stricter privacy requirements in the local model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This insight has significant implications for practical deployments, suggesting that existing LDP-based systems might offer better privacy than previously estimated when reports are properly anonymized \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, privacy amplification has been explored in relation to private learning and statistical estimation, where it helps in reducing the amount of noise required to maintain privacy while performing complex computations on sensitive data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. The development of general privacy amplification techniques has also contributed to the broader understanding of how differential privacy can be achieved and maintained in various computational settings, including statistical databases and learning problems \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]\[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d762c3b48d3949dc2cd803d955c3c983e7aa16ba)\]. These advancements highlight the importance of privacy amplification in improving the efficiency and effectiveness of privacy-preserving mechanisms across a range of applications \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d762c3b48d3949dc2cd803d955c3c983e7aa16ba)\].

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-of-differential-privacy'></a>Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy: 2 papers found.
The shuffled model of differential privacy has emerged as a promising framework that strikes a balance between the local and central models, offering both improved accuracy over the local model and avoiding the need for a trusted server \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes user-supplied messages, enabling more efficient computation of aggregate statistics while preserving privacy \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Recent work has demonstrated that the shuffled model can achieve significantly better sample complexity than the local model for certain tasks, such as histogram estimation, where the error is independent of the domain size \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. However, the shuffled model is not without limitations; under certain constraints, such as pure differential privacy and single-message randomizers, it becomes equivalent to the local model \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. These findings highlight the nuanced trade-offs between privacy guarantees, computational efficiency, and accuracy in distributed settings, positioning the shuffled model as a critical area of ongoing research in privacy-preserving data analysis.
