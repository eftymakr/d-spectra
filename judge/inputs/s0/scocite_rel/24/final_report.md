## Related Works

Diffusion models have gained significant traction in image and video generation due to their ability to produce high-quality outputs \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00426)\]. However, their deployment on edge devices is hindered by high computational demands and memory usage \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00426)\]. To address these challenges, various quantization techniques have been proposed for diffusion models. Q-Diffusion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\] introduces a post-training quantization (PTQ) method tailored for diffusion models, targeting the multi-timestep pipeline and model architecture. It identifies key challenges such as changing output distributions over time steps and bimodal activation distributions, and proposes timestep-aware calibration and split shortcut quantization to mitigate these issues. This method successfully quantizes full-precision models to 4-bit while maintaining comparable performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\].

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), an alternative to traditional U-Net-based diffusion models, have shown superior scalability and performance in image and video generation \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. Despite their advantages, DiTs face challenges in deployment due to their high parameter count and computational complexity \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. PTQ4DiT \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\] is a PTQ method specifically designed for DiTs, addressing challenges such as salient channels with extreme magnitudes and temporal variability in activation distributions. It introduces Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) to improve quantization accuracy. PTQ4DiT successfully quantizes DiTs to 8-bit precision (W8A8) and further enables 4-bit weight precision (W4A8) for the first time \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\].

For video generation, diffusion models face additional challenges due to the need for temporal coherence and high-resolution outputs \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03458)\]. Latte \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03048)\] is a latent diffusion transformer designed for video generation, incorporating spatio-temporal tokens and efficient variants to model video distribution in the latent space. It achieves state-of-the-art performance on standard video generation datasets \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03048)\]. Similarly, Stable Video Diffusion \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\] extends latent diffusion models to video generation, emphasizing the importance of well-curated pretraining datasets for generating high-quality videos. It demonstrates the necessity of a strong base model for downstream tasks like image-to-video generation \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\].

Quantization methods for diffusion models often struggle with maintaining performance at low bit-widths due to the unique properties of diffusion models, such as temporal variation in activation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. PTQD \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\] introduces a unified formulation for quantization noise and diffusion perturbed noise, addressing issues like mean deviation and variance schedule

### <a id='category-quantization-techniques-for-diffusion-models'></a>Quantization Techniques for Diffusion Models: 9 papers found.
Diffusion models have gained significant attention for their ability to generate high-quality images, but their computational intensity and memory consumption hinder deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have explored post-training quantization (PTQ) as a method to reduce model size and accelerate inference without retraining \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. However, quantizing diffusion models presents unique difficulties, such as the temporal variation in activation distributions and the bimodal nature of shortcut layers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Several studies have proposed tailored PTQ approaches to overcome these issues, including timestep-aware calibration and split shortcut quantization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\], as well as dynamic quantization intervals based on time step information \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]. These methods aim to maintain generation quality while achieving significant compression, with some achieving 4-bit quantization with minimal performance degradation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. Additionally, efforts have been made to extend quantization techniques to diffusion transformers (DiT), which are used in video and text-to-image generation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. For instance, ViDiT-Q was introduced to address quantization challenges in DiT models, achieving W8A8 and W4A8 quantization with negligible quality loss \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. Similarly, HQ-DiT introduced 4-bit floating-point quantization for DiT, which aligns better with the data distribution and reduces quantization error \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to make diffusion models more efficient for real-world applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.19751)\].

### <a id='category-post-training-quantization-methods-for-dit'></a>Post-Training Quantization Methods for DiT: 5 papers found.
Recent advancements in diffusion models, particularly the shift from U-Net-based architectures to Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), have significantly improved the quality and scalability of image and video generation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. However, the computational demands of these large-scale models hinder their deployment on edge devices, prompting the need for efficient quantization techniques \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising solution, enabling model compression and accelerated inference without retraining \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Despite its success in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs), existing PTQ methods face challenges when applied to DiTs, such as biased quantization and performance degradation \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Researchers have identified key issues, including spatial and temporal variance in activations and weights, which complicate the quantization process \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. To address these challenges, novel approaches like PTQ4DiT and Q-DiT have been proposed, incorporating techniques such as channel-wise salience balancing and dynamic activation quantization to maintain generation quality at lower bit-widths \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Additionally, methods like ZeroQuant and PTQ4ViT have demonstrated the feasibility of efficient quantization for large models, achieving near-lossless performance with minimal accuracy drops \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01861)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12293)\]. These efforts highlight the growing interest in developing tailored quantization strategies for DiTs, balancing computational efficiency with the preservation of model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12293)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\].

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-image/video-generation'></a>Diffusion Models in Image/Video Generation: 10 papers found.
Diffusion models have shown significant advancements in image and video generation, with recent works focusing on improving efficiency and quality. Latent diffusion models (LDMs) leverage pretrained autoencoders to operate in the latent space, reducing computational demands while maintaining high visual fidelity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. This approach has enabled state-of-the-art results in image inpainting, text-to-image synthesis, and super-resolution \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Similarly, diffusion transformers (DiTs) have been introduced to enhance scalability and performance, with models like DiT-XL/2 achieving superior FID scores on ImageNet benchmarks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. For video generation, Latte and Make-A-Video extend diffusion models to handle temporal coherence, using Transformer-based architectures to model spatio-temporal patterns and generate high-resolution videos \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13824)\]. Additionally, classifier-free guided diffusion models have been optimized for high-resolution image generation, though they face computational challenges during inference \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12293)\]. To address these issues, methods like distillation and quantization have been proposed to reduce model size and inference time while preserving quality \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12293)\]. Furthermore, recent works such as PIXART-$\alpha$ emphasize efficient training strategies and transformer-based architectures to achieve competitive performance with lower computational costs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to balance quality, efficiency, and scalability in diffusion models across various modalities.

### <a id='category-quantization-noise-and-perturbation-handling'></a>Quantization Noise and Perturbation Handling: 5 papers found.
Network quantization has emerged as a critical technique for reducing computational costs and enabling efficient deployment of deep learning models on resource-constrained devices \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323)\]. Existing methods, such as Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) and Quantization-Aware-Training (QAT), aim to mitigate accuracy degradation caused by quantization noise while maintaining low-bit representations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08295)\]. PTQ is a lightweight approach that requires no retraining, making it suitable for 8-bit quantization with minimal accuracy loss \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08295)\], whereas QAT enables lower bit quantization by incorporating quantization effects during training \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08295)\]. Recent advances have focused on improving the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency, particularly for vision transformers and large language models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. For instance, methods like Power-of-Two Factor (PTF) address inter-channel variation in LayerNorm inputs, reducing performance degradation in fully quantized vision transformers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\], while Log-Int-Softmax (LIS) leverages 4-bit quantization and BitShift operators to simplify inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Similarly, SmoothQuant enables 8-bit weight and activation (W8A8) quantization for large language models without requiring retraining, by smoothing activation outliers and migrating quantization difficulty to weights \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10438)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to develop robust quantization strategies that preserve model performance while enabling efficient inference on edge devices \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323)\].
