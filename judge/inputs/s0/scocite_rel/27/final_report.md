## Related Works

Recent advancements in information retrieval have seen a shift from traditional index-retrieve pipelines to more unified, end-to-end models that integrate indexing and search within a single framework. This trend is driven by the limitations of conventional methods, which often suffer from error accumulation during representation and indexing phases \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Additionally, insufficient query-document interaction hinders semantic alignment, limiting accuracy improvements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Several works have explored differentiable search indexes (DSIs) as a solution, where a single transformer model encodes all corpus information and directly maps queries to document identifiers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. 

The DSI approach eliminates the need for separate indexing and retrieval components, enabling direct query-to-docid mapping \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This paradigm has been further refined with variations such as DSI-QG, which addresses data distribution mismatches by generating relevant queries during indexing \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. Similarly, the Semantic-Enhanced DSI (SE-DSI) model improves document memorization by incorporating semantic features and elaborative descriptions during indexing \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of aligning query and document representations during the indexing phase to improve retrieval performance.

Another line of research focuses on the continual learning of DSI models, as dynamic corpora require incremental updates without catastrophic forgetting \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. DSI++ introduces an index update strategy using parameter isolation to mitigate forgetting during continual indexing \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09744)\]. This work demonstrates that flatter minima in the loss landscape can stabilize learning and improve memory retention, offering a promising direction for maintaining performance over time.

Generative retrieval models, such as those proposed in the SEAL framework, use autoregressive models to generate and score ngrams as document identifiers, mapping them to full passages through efficient data structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This approach outperforms prior autoregressive methods and established retrieval solutions, achieving significant improvements on benchmark datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) introduces a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder and semantic document identifiers to enhance recall performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. These models demonstrate the potential of end-to-end neural architectures for document retrieval, reducing reliance on traditional indexing techniques.

The use of large language models (LLMs) for query generation and semantic alignment has also gained traction. For instance, the TOME framework employs tokenized URLs as identifiers and introduces a two-stage generation architecture to improve retrieval accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\]. This approach addresses the discrepancy between pre-training and fine-tuning, as well as training and inference, by leveraging tokenized identifiers and structured generation pipelines \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\]. ### <a id='category-differentiable-search-index-(dsi)-frameworks'></a>Differentiable Search Index (DSI) Frameworks: 4 papers found.
Differentiable Search Index (DSI) frameworks represent a novel paradigm in information retrieval, where a single transformer model is used to perform both indexing and retrieval, eliminating the need for separate index and retrieval components \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach encodes all information about the corpus into the model's parameters, enabling direct query-to-document identifier mapping \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. However, existing DSI models face challenges such as data distribution mismatches between indexing and retrieval phases, particularly when queries are shorter than indexed documents or when cross-lingual retrieval is required \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed enhancements like DSI-QG, which generates relevant queries during indexing to improve alignment between queries and documents \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\], and SE-DSI, which incorporates semantic features to enhance document memorization \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\]. Additionally, frameworks like RIPOR have been introduced to optimize generative retrieval models for large-scale benchmarks by improving relevance score estimation and document ID construction based on query-document relevance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. These advancements highlight ongoing efforts to refine DSI models for better performance and robustness in diverse retrieval scenarios.

### <a id='category-end-to-end-retrieval-models'></a>End-to-End Retrieval Models: 7 papers found.
Recent advancements in end-to-end retrieval models have aimed to overcome the limitations of traditional index-retrieve paradigms by integrating training and indexing stages into a unified framework \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. For instance, Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) introduces a sequence-to-sequence network that generates document identifiers directly for a query, improving recall performance through a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Similarly, ColBERT adapts deep language models like BERT for efficient retrieval by using a late interaction architecture that independently encodes queries and documents, then computes their fine-grained similarity through a lightweight interaction step \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. This approach enables pre-computation of document representations and significantly reduces computational costs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. Another model, TOME, eliminates the need for an explicit index by memorizing candidate corpora within model parameters, using a two-stage generation architecture and tokenized URLs as identifiers to capture query-document relevance more effectively \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\]. Meanwhile, dense retrievers have shown promise in open-domain question answering, outperforming traditional sparse models like BM25 by leveraging embeddings learned from a dual-encoder framework \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. Additionally, generative retrieval models such as GenRet address the challenge of defining document identifiers by learning semantic docids through a discrete auto-encoding approach, enabling fully end-to-end modeling \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These models collectively highlight a shift toward more efficient, accurate, and adaptable retrieval systems that can be optimized directly for the final retrieval task \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11161)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\].

### <a id='category-continual-learning-in-dsi-models'></a>Continual Learning in DSI Models: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Differentiable Search Indices (DSIs) encode a corpus of documents in model parameters and use the same model to answer user queries directly. Despite the strong performance of DSI models, deploying them in situations where the corpus changes over time is computationally expensive because reindexing the corpus requires re-training the model. In this work, we introduce DSI++, a continual learning challenge for DSI to incrementally index new documents while being able to answer queries related to both previously and newly indexed documents. Across different model scales and document identifier representations, we show that continual indexing of new documents leads to considerable forgetting of previously indexed documents. We also hypothesize and verify that the model experiences forgetting events during training, leading to unstable learning. To mitigate these issues, we investigate two approaches. The first focuses on modifying the training dynamics. Flatter minima implicitly alleviate forgetting, so we optimize for flatter loss basins and show that the model stably memorizes more documents ($+12\%$). Next, we introduce a generative memory to sample pseudo-queries for documents and supplement them during continual indexing to prevent forgetting for the retrieval task. Extensive experiments on novel continual indexing benchmarks based on Natural Questions (NQ) and MS MARCO demonstrate that our proposed solution mitigates forgetting significantly. Concretely, it improves the average Hits@10 by $+21.1\%$ over competitive baselines for NQ and requires $6$ times fewer model updates compared to re-training the DSI model for incrementally indexing five corpora in a sequence.»
