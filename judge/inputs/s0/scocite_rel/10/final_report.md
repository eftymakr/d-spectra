## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike traditional next-token prediction (NTP), which predicts one token at a time, MTP extends this by predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to enhance generative capabilities, particularly for large models, by enabling more efficient training and faster inference \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has highlighted that smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in effectively leveraging the MTP objective \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several studies have explored alternative training strategies to address these limitations. For instance, curriculum learning has been proposed as a method to gradually increase the complexity of the training objective, helping models adapt more effectively \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. A forward curriculum approach, which transitions from NTP to MTP, has been shown to improve the performance of SLMs on downstream NTP tasks while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. In contrast, a reverse curriculum, which starts with MTP and gradually reduces complexity, achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide the same speculative decoding advantages \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The concept of process supervision has also been explored as a way to improve reasoning capabilities in language models. Process supervision involves providing feedback at each intermediate reasoning step, which has been shown to outperform outcome supervision in tasks such as solving mathematical problems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. This approach aligns with the idea of using structured feedback to guide model learning, which is also relevant to curriculum learning strategies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Additionally, speculative decoding has been introduced as a method to accelerate inference in large language models by allowing the model to generate multiple tokens in parallel \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. This technique has been further refined with the development of Medusa, which uses multiple decoding heads to predict multiple tokens simultaneously, significantly reducing inference time without compromising generation quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. These methods highlight the importance of parallel processing and efficient decoding strategies in improving the practicality of large language models.

The effectiveness of training strategies is also influenced by the scale of the model and the amount of training data. Research has shown that compute-optimal training, where both model size and training data are scaled equally, leads to better performance across a range of downstream tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556)\]. This finding underscores the importance of balancing model complexity with training resources when designing pre-training objectives like MTP.

### <a id='category-pre-training-efficiency-and-inference-speed'></a>Pre-training Efficiency and Inference Speed: 2 papers found.
Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for improving language models' performance, efficiency, and inference speed, as opposed to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. By training models to predict multiple future tokens simultaneously using independent output heads, MTP enhances sample efficiency and downstream capabilities, particularly for large models \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has demonstrated significant gains in generative tasks, such as coding, where models trained with MTP outperform NTP baselines by several percentage points \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Additionally, MTP enables faster inference, with models trained using 4-token prediction achieving up to 3 times speed improvements even with large batch sizes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The benefits of MTP are further extended by methods like Medusa, which introduces parallel decoding heads to predict multiple tokens simultaneously, reducing decoding steps and improving inference speed without compromising generation quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. Medusa achieves speedups of over 2.2x with Medusa-1 and up to 3.6x with Medusa-2, while maintaining or improving prediction accuracy \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. These findings highlight the potential of MTP and related techniques to enhance both training efficiency and inference performance in large language models.

### <a id='category-speculative-decoding-methods'></a>Speculative Decoding Methods: 2 papers found.
Speculative decoding has emerged as a promising approach to accelerate inference in large language models (LLMs) by enabling parallel token generation during decoding. Unlike traditional autoregressive decoding, which sequentially generates one token at a time, speculative decoding leverages a combination of approximation models and verification steps to generate multiple tokens in parallel \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.07851)\]. This method involves drafting potential future tokens using a more efficient model and then verifying them against the main model, allowing for faster inference without compromising output quality \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. The core idea is that many language modeling tasks can be decomposed into subtasks that can be efficiently handled by simpler models, which can then be validated by the larger, more accurate model \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. This approach has been shown to significantly reduce inference latency, with some implementations achieving up to a 3X speedup compared to standard decoding methods \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\]. Additionally, speculative decoding can be applied to existing models without requiring retraining or architectural changes, making it a versatile technique for improving the efficiency of LLM inference \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192)\].
