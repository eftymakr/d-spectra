## Related Works

Brain tumor segmentation is a critical task in medical image analysis, with significant implications for diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis. Over the years, numerous approaches have been proposed to address this challenge, ranging from traditional image processing techniques to modern deep learning models. A key challenge in this domain is the limited availability of annotated data, as well as the high computational cost of training deep models from scratch \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.11654)\]. To mitigate these issues, several studies have explored the use of pre-trained models and data augmentation strategies to improve performance and reduce training time \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04597)\].

Early approaches to brain tumor segmentation relied on hand-crafted features and traditional image processing techniques, which were often limited in their ability to capture complex spatial relationships \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05959)\]. With the advent of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become the de facto standard for this task \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10508)\]. The U-Net architecture, introduced by Ronneberger et al., has been particularly influential due to its symmetric encoder-decoder structure, which enables precise localization of features while capturing contextual information \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04797)\]. This architecture has been widely adopted and adapted for 3D medical image segmentation, with modifications such as 3D convolutions and multi-scale processing \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\].

To address the issue of limited training data, several works have proposed methods that leverage transfer learning by using pre-trained models on other tasks, such as image classification, and fine-tuning them for segmentation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.00382)\]. For example, the V-Net architecture was designed specifically for 3D medical image segmentation and incorporates a fully convolutional structure to handle volumetric data \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/580062407427236ced45253a2ff7df2e147a81e2)\]. Similarly, the 3D U-Net, which extends the original U-Net to three dimensions, has been used for segmenting brain tumors from MRI scans \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.00848)\]. These models often incorporate techniques such as dice loss functions and data augmentation to improve performance and reduce overfitting \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02040)\].

Recent advances have also focused on improving the efficiency and accuracy of segmentation models by integrating attention mechanisms and transformer-based architectures \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02629)\]. For instance, the Swin UNETR model, which combines the strengths of vision transformers with the U-Net architecture, has shown promising results in capturing long-range dependencies and achieving state-of-the-art performance on brain tumor segmentation tasks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\]. Similarly, the TransUNet model, which integrates transformers with U-Net, has been used to enhance the localization capabilities of segmentation models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.02314)\].
