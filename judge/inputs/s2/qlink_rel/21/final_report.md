## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images, leveraging deep learning to model both geometry and appearance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. The core idea involves taking multiple views as input and optimizing the reconstructed scene by minimizing the uncertainty in geometry and appearance across these views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often limited by the number of input views, especially in complex and dynamic scenes where certain angles of objects are never captured \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. To address this limitation, researchers have explored various data augmentation techniques, including video frame interpolation, to generate additional views and improve the robustness of neural rendering models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\].

These methods demonstrate that integrating motion and synthesis into a single network can lead to more efficient and effective video interpolation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\].

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-methods'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Methods: 11 papers found.
Video frame interpolation methods have evolved significantly, moving from traditional optical flow-based approaches to more sophisticated deep learning techniques. Early methods typically involved estimating optical flow between input frames and then synthesizing intermediate frames guided by motion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. However, these methods often required large kernels to handle complex motion, leading to high memory demands and computational costs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Recent advancements have focused on integrating motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a single process, often using deep convolutional neural networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. For example, some approaches employ local separable convolutions with 1D kernels, which reduce parameter counts and improve efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Others propose end-to-end models that jointly model motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning, using architectures like U-Net to refine optical flow and predict visibility maps \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. These models often incorporate perceptual losses to enhance visual quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Additionally, methods such as deep voxel flow combine traditional optical flow with neural networks to synthesize frames by flowing pixel values from existing ones, achieving high-quality results without human supervision \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\]. Context-aware synthesis approaches further improve results by warping not only input frames but also their contextual information, leading to more accurate intermediate frames \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. The integration of motion estimation and compensation into a single differentiable framework has also shown promise, enabling efficient and high-quality frame interpolation while being adaptable to other video enhancement tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. Overall, these methods address challenges like occlusion, blur, and large motion, offering practical solutions for high-quality video frame interpolation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\].

### <a id='category-3d-geometry-modeling-in-neural-rendering'></a>3D Geometry Modeling in Neural Rendering: 8 papers found.
In the realm of 3D geometry modeling within neural rendering, recent advancements have focused on improving surface reconstruction from 2D inputs without requiring explicit foreground masks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Methods like NeuS introduce a neural SDF representation, leveraging a new volume rendering formulation to avoid geometric bias and achieve high-quality surface reconstructions, especially for complex structures and self-occluded objects \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]. Similarly, implicit shape and texture representations have been explored, where depth gradients are derived analytically for implicit differentiation, allowing direct learning from RGB images and producing watertight meshes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\]. These approaches address the limitations of voxel- and mesh-based representations, which suffer from discretization or low resolution \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Another key development involves unifying implicit surface models with radiance fields, enabling both surface and volume rendering with a single model and improving reconstruction accuracy without masks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. Additionally, modeling volume density as a function of the geometry, rather than the reverse, has led to more accurate sampling and disentanglement of shape and appearance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12052)\]. This has been further enhanced by representing surfaces using implicit functions like the truncated signed distance function, which avoids artifacts during surface extraction \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\]. Furthermore, methods such as Point-NeRF combine the strengths of volumetric rendering and multi-view stereo, using neural 3D point clouds for efficient and high-quality reconstructions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. The integration of monocular geometric priors, such as depth and normal cues, has also shown significant improvements in reconstruction quality and optimization efficiency, particularly for complex and large-scale scenes \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. These works collectively highlight the shift towards implicit representations and the integration of geometric priors to enhance the fidelity and robustness of 3D reconstruction in neural rendering.

### <a id='category-scene-representation-models-in-neural-rendering'></a>Scene Representation Models in Neural Rendering: 6 papers found.
Scene representation models in neural rendering have been extensively explored to enable high-quality free-viewpoint rendering from 2D images without explicit 3D supervision. Early approaches like Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07492)\] model scenes as continuous functions that map world coordinates to feature representations, enabling novel view synthesis through differentiable ray-marching. However, NeRF suffers from issues such as aliasing and slow rendering due to its reliance on ray sampling \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13415)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed extensions like mip-NeRF, which introduces a continuously-valued scale representation to improve anti-aliasing and rendering efficiency \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13415)\]. Additionally, methods such as NSVF \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\] and SRNs \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\] have been developed to enhance scene modeling by incorporating sparse voxel structures or 3D-structure-aware representations, allowing for faster and more accurate rendering. These models also support scene editing and composition, expanding their applicability to dynamic and complex environments. Furthermore, pixelNeRF \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02190)\] introduces a framework that conditions the neural scene representation on input images, enabling efficient training across multiple scenes and supporting novel view synthesis from sparse views. These advancements collectively contribute to the evolving landscape of neural rendering, emphasizing the importance of implicit scene modeling and efficient rendering techniques.
