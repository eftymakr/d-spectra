## Related Works

Recent advances in neural retrieval methods, particularly those leveraging transformer-based pre-trained language models, have significantly improved multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval capabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. However, the effectiveness of these models for low-resource, morphologically rich languages such as Amharic remains underexplored due to data scarcity and suboptimal tokenization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges and provide foundational insights for the proposed approach.

Multilingual BERT (mBERT) has demonstrated impressive cross-lingual performance on various NLP tasks, but it struggles with low-resource languages due to limited capacity and uneven pretraining data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. Similarly, the performance of mBERT on low-resource languages is often worse than monolingual models, highlighting the need for language-specific adaptation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. This issue is further exacerbated by the fact that many multilingual models are trained on a limited set of languages, typically the top 104 languages in Wikipedia \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\].

To address these limitations, several works have focused on improving the performance of multilingual models for under-resourced languages. For instance, the work by \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15562)\] proposes a method for adapting pretrained multilingual models to new scripts and low-resource languages by leveraging matrix factorization and lexically overlapping tokens. This approach has shown significant improvements for languages with unseen scripts and low-resource languages \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04672)\]. Similarly, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\] introduces a conditional compute model based on Sparsely Gated Mixture of Experts to improve the performance of low-resource language translation, achieving a 44% BLEU improvement over the previous state-of-the-art \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.01488)\].

In the context of information retrieval, ColBERT has emerged as a powerful model that enables efficient and effective passage retrieval by introducing a late interaction architecture that independently encodes queries and documents \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13897)\]. This model has been further extended to support cross-language information retrieval (CLIR) with the introduction of ColBERT-X, which uses the XLM-RoBERTa (XLM-R) encoder for cross-language mappings \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These models have demonstrated substantial improvements over traditional lexical CLIR baselines \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.11811)\].

Additionally, the work by \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10084)\] introduces M3-Embedding, a versatile embedding model that supports multilingual, multi-functional, and multi-granular text retrieval. This model is capable of handling tasks such as dense retrieval, multi-vector retrieval, and sparse retrieval, making it suitable for a wide range of applications \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\].

### <a id='category-dense-retrieval-models'></a>Dense Retrieval Models: 3 papers found.
Dense retrieval models have shown significant improvements in open-domain question answering by leveraging dense representations over traditional sparse methods like TF-IDF or BM25 \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. These models use dual-encoder frameworks to learn embeddings from questions and passages, enabling more accurate passage retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. However, the effectiveness of dense retrieval often requires integration with sparse retrieval techniques to address limitations in training mechanisms, where negative instances are not representative of irrelevant documents \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. To resolve this, Approximate Nearest Neighbor Negative Contrastive Estimation (ANCE) was introduced, which constructs negatives from an ANN index, improving the alignment between training and testing data distributions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Additionally, advancements in sentence embedding models, such as Sentence-BERT (SBERT), have enabled efficient semantic similarity search by using siamese and triplet network structures, reducing computational overhead while maintaining accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. These developments highlight the evolving landscape of dense retrieval, emphasizing the importance of both model architecture and training strategies in achieving state-of-the-art performance.

After we investigate the publicly available pre-trained semantic models, we fine-tune two pre-trained models and train seven new different models. The models include Word2Vec embeddings, distributional thesaurus (DT), BERT-like contextual embeddings, and DT embeddings obtained via network embedding algorithms. Moreover, we employ these models for different NLP tasks and study their impact. We find that newly-trained models perform better than pre-trained multilingual models. Furthermore, models based on contextual embeddings from FLAIR and RoBERTa perform better than word2Vec models for the NER and POS tagging tasks. DT-based network embeddings are suitable for the sentiment classification task. We publicly release all the semantic models, machine learning components, and several benchmark datasets such as NER, POS tagging, sentiment classification, as well as Amharic versions of WordSim353 and SimLex999.»
