## Related Works

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start problems in traditional sequential recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges, often leveraging domain-specific information or cross-domain knowledge transfer. For instance, C2DSR \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\] introduces a framework that simultaneously captures intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences. This approach uses graph neural networks to mine collaborative relationships and sequential attentive encoders to capture sequential patterns, followed by a contrastive cross-domain infomax objective to enhance the correlation between single- and cross-domain user representations.

Similarly, DA-GCN \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\] proposes a domain-aware graph convolution network to learn user-specific node representations by linking users and items in each domain as a graph. It introduces attention mechanisms to selectively guide message passing and improve the modeling of domain-specific preferences. Another work, DASL \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\], employs a dual learning mechanism to iteratively transfer information between two domains, enabling simultaneous improvement of cross-domain click-through rate predictions. This method constructs dual latent embeddings and uses a dual-attention learning mechanism to match these embeddings with candidate items.

In the realm of self-attention based models, SASRec \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\] was introduced to capture long-term semantics while using an attention mechanism to make predictions based on relatively few actions. This model outperforms various state-of-the-art sequential models on both sparse and dense datasets. BERT4Rec \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\] further improves upon this by employing a bidirectional self-attention mechanism to model user behavior sequences, allowing each item to fuse information from both left and right sides of the sequence.

Recent works such as π-Net \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] address the shared-account and cross-domain challenges by proposing a parallel information-sharing network that simultaneously generates recommendations for two domains. This model includes a shared account filter unit and a cross-domain transfer unit to handle the complexities of shared accounts and cross-domain interactions. Similarly, DDGHM \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] introduces a dual dynamic graph modeling approach combined with hybrid metric training to enhance user and item representations, addressing the data sparsity issue and improving prediction accuracy.

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical research direction in recommender systems, aiming to predict user behavior across multiple domains by leveraging sequential interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Existing approaches often rely on domain-specific modules or transfer learning strategies to bridge domain gaps, but they frequently overlook the potential of the self-attention mechanism inherent in transformer-based models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. For instance, some works focus on mining inter-sequence item relationships using graph neural networks and sequential attentive encoders to capture intra-sequence patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Others propose domain-aware graph convolution networks (DA-GCN) to learn user-specific representations by integrating cross-domain graph structures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. The challenge of information leakage in datasets has also been acknowledged, with efforts to correct and release improved versions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Furthermore, approaches such as Tri-CDR jointly model source, target, and mixed behavior sequences to highlight global and target preferences, using triple cross-domain attention and contrastive learning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. The importance of time intervals in sequential recommendations has also been highlighted, with methods like TiDA-GCN incorporating time-aware features to enhance representation learning \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. Overall, these works highlight the need for more effective integration of cross-domain information while maintaining domain-specific preferences, with a growing emphasis on model flexibility and computational efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\].

### <a id='category-dual-learning-and-knowledge-transfer-methods'></a>Dual Learning and Knowledge Transfer Methods: 4 papers found.
Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has emerged as a critical area in recommender systems, aiming to predict user behavior across multiple domains by leveraging shared knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Existing approaches often rely on self-attention mechanisms within transformer-based models, yet they typically fail to fully exploit the inherent power of self-attention for learning correlations among sequential behaviors \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. To address this, recent works have introduced dual learning and knowledge transfer methods that enable bidirectional information exchange between domains, improving both source and target domain performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. For instance, the Dual Attentive Sequential Learning (DASL) model employs dual embedding and dual attention components to simultaneously extract and match user preferences across domains \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\]. Similarly, the Deep Dual Transfer Cross Domain Recommendation (DDTCDR) model uses latent orthogonal mapping and autoencoder techniques to preserve user-item relations while extracting domain-specific features \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. The Collaborative Cross Networks (CoNet) approach further enhances knowledge transfer by introducing cross connections between base networks, enabling adaptive representation selection and reducing training data requirements \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Additionally, the BiTGCF model leverages graph collaborative filtering to enable two-way knowledge transfer through common users, effectively fusing domain-specific and shared features \[[29](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\]. These methods collectively highlight the growing emphasis on bidirectional learning and the integration of domain-aware components to enhance cross-domain recommendation performance.

### <a id='category-self-attention-based-sequential-recommendation-models'></a>Self-Attention Based Sequential Recommendation Models: 5 papers found.
Sequential recommendation models have evolved significantly, with self-attention based approaches gaining prominence for their ability to capture complex sequential patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. Traditional methods like Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been widely used, but they face limitations in modeling long-term dependencies and capturing bidirectional context \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Recent works such as SASRec \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02768)\] and BERT4Rec \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\] have introduced self-attention mechanisms to overcome these limitations, enabling models to dynamically weigh relevant items in a user's history while preserving the order of interactions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04590)\]. These models have shown superior performance across both sparse and dense datasets, offering efficiency and adaptability to varying data densities \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. However, most of these models assume that interaction sequences are purely ordered without considering the time intervals between actions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. To address this, TiSASRec \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10163)\] was proposed to incorporate time-aware positional encodings, enhancing the model's ability to capture temporal dynamics \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a8f2764a76772ab957b31f5d7bb88527d46fbdc8)\]. Additionally, session-based recommendation models like NARM \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] have been developed to better capture user intent within short sessions, using hybrid encoders and attention mechanisms to model both sequential behavior and session-specific goals \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of self-attention in sequential recommendation, offering flexible and effective solutions for capturing user behavior across diverse scenarios.
