## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural network models that directly generate document identifiers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This approach leverages large language models to encode entire document corpora, enabling seamless integration with generative models \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Unlike conventional retrieval systems that rely on separate indexing and retrieval stages, generative retrieval models operate as a single differentiable search index, generating identifiers through constrained decoding \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. This paradigm has been explored in various studies, each addressing specific challenges in scalability, effectiveness, and generalization.

Early work on generative retrieval focused on autoregressive models that generate document identifiers by leveraging n-grams or semantic representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. These models often use hierarchical structures to partition the search space, but recent approaches have moved toward using all n-grams in a passage as potential identifiers, allowing for more flexible and effective retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. This shift has led to significant improvements in performance, with some models achieving up to a 10-point improvement over traditional retrieval systems on benchmark datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\].

The integration of generative retrieval with large language models has also been explored in the context of knowledge-intensive tasks, where models must retrieve and generate answers based on supporting evidence \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) combines pre-trained models with dense vector indexes to enhance the ability of models to access and manipulate knowledge \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. This approach has been shown to outperform both parametric and task-specific architectures, particularly in open-domain question answering \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)\]. Additionally, the use of query generation models to improve the alignment between indexed documents and retrieval queries has been proposed to address data distribution mismatches \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

Another critical challenge in generative retrieval is the optimization of retrieval performance through beam search and constrained decoding. While beam search is commonly used to generate document identifiers, recent studies have shown that the use of marginal distributions during generation may not be optimal \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. This has led to the development of learning-to-rank frameworks that directly optimize passage ranking, bridging the gap between autoregressive generation and the final retrieval target \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\].

The scalability of generative retrieval to large corpora has also been a focus of recent research. Studies have explored how generative models can be trained to handle millions of passages, emphasizing the importance of synthetic queries and efficient indexing strategies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. These findings highlight the potential of generative retrieval but also underscore the challenges in scaling to real-world applications \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].
