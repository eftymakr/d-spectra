## Related Works

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have emerged as a promising direction in natural language processing, aiming to integrate linguistic and non-linguistic information for more comprehensive understanding and generation. Early efforts in vision-language pre-training, such as ViLBERT \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02265)\], extended BERT to a multi-modal two-stream model, enabling joint processing of visual and textual inputs through co-attentional transformer layers. This approach laid the foundation for later models that sought to better align visual and linguistic representations.

Recent advancements have focused on improving the ability of models to understand and generate nonverbal cues, such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language. Flamingo \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\] introduced a family of Visual Language Models (VLMs) capable of few-shot learning, bridging pretrained vision-only and language-only models. This work emphasized the importance of handling interleaved visual and textual data and demonstrated the effectiveness of in-context learning for vision-language tasks. Similarly, InstructBLIP \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06500)\] extended this idea by introducing vision-language instruction tuning, transforming datasets into instruction-tuning formats and achieving state-of-the-art zero-shot performance across multiple tasks.

The integration of nonverbal cues into language models has also been explored through datasets and training paradigms. Kosmos-1 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\] was trained on web-scale multimodal corpora, including arbitrarily interleaved text and images, and demonstrated impressive performance on perception-language tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering. This model highlighted the potential of MLLMs to understand and generate content across multiple modalities. Similarly, mPLUG-Owl \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.14178)\] introduced a modular training paradigm that enables LLMs to learn multimodal abilities through the collaboration of foundation LLMs, visual knowledge modules, and visual abstractor modules. This approach supports both unimodal and multimodal tasks and enables the model to handle complex interactions between different modalities.

The need for comprehensive evaluation benchmarks has also been addressed in recent work. MME \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\] introduced a comprehensive benchmark for MLLMs, measuring both perception and cognition abilities across 14 subtasks. This benchmark emphasized the importance of avoiding data leakage and ensuring fair comparisons between models. Similarly, MMBench \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06281)\] proposed a bilingual benchmark for assessing the multi-modal capabilities of VLMs, incorporating multiple-choice questions in both English and Chinese to enable apples-to-apples comparisons.

### <a id='category-vision-language-pre-training-models'></a>Vision-Language Pre-training Models: 4 papers found.
Vision-Language Pre-training Models have been pivotal in advancing multimodal understanding and generation, enabling models to process and generate content across text, images, and other modalities. These models, such as Kosmos-1 \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\], ViLBERT \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02265)\], and BLIP \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12086)\], demonstrate the ability to learn joint representations of vision and language, allowing for tasks like visual question answering, image captioning, and zero-shot transfer to new tasks. Kosmos-1, for instance, is trained on web-scale multimodal corpora, including interleaved text and images, and can perform OCR-free NLP directly on document images \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. Similarly, ViLBERT extends BERT to a two-stream model, incorporating co-attentional transformer layers to process visual and textual inputs separately while enabling interaction between modalities \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02265)\]. BLIP further improves upon these models by effectively utilizing noisy web data through caption bootstrapping, achieving state-of-the-art results in image-text retrieval, image captioning, and VQA \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.12086)\]. Additionally, models like CLIP \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\] demonstrate the power of learning from raw text about images, enabling zero-shot transfer to a wide range of downstream tasks without the need for task-specific training. These models highlight the importance of pre-training on large, diverse datasets and the potential for cross-modal transfer, which is essential for building more versatile and intelligent AI systems.

### <a id='category-evaluation-benchmarks-for-mllms'></a>Evaluation Benchmarks for MLLMs: 4 papers found.
Recent advancements in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have focused on developing comprehensive evaluation benchmarks to assess both perception and cognition abilities across a wide range of multimodal tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\]. These benchmarks aim to address the limitations of existing evaluation methods, such as data leakage and the lack of fine-grained ability assessment \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06281)\]. For instance, MME introduces a set of 14 subtasks with manually designed instruction-answer pairs to ensure fair comparisons between models without relying on prompt engineering \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\]. Similarly, MMBench provides a bilingual benchmark with a rigorous evaluation pipeline, including CircularEval strategies and multiple-choice questions in both English and Chinese, enabling scalable and unbiased assessments of vision-language models \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06281)\]. MM-Vet further expands on this by structuring complex multimodal tasks around six core vision-language capabilities, evaluating their integration through 16 combinations and using an LLM-based evaluator to unify scoring across different question types \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02490)\]. Additionally, SEED-Bench offers a large-scale benchmark with 19K multiple-choice questions, covering 12 evaluation dimensions, including image and video comprehension, and enables objective and efficient model assessment through human-annotated groundtruth options \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16125)\]. These benchmarks collectively highlight the growing need for systematic, scalable, and comprehensive evaluation frameworks to drive the development of more capable MLLMs.

### <a id='category-instruction-tuning-for-vision-language-tasks'></a>Instruction Tuning for Vision-Language Tasks: 3 papers found.
Instruction tuning for vision-language tasks has seen significant advancements, with researchers exploring methods to enhance the zero-shot performance of large language models (LLMs) on multimodal tasks. Early work focused on generating instruction-following data using language-only models like GPT-4 to train multimodal models such as LLaVA, which connects a vision encoder with an LLM for general-purpose visual and language understanding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. This approach demonstrated impressive results, achieving a 85.1% relative score compared to GPT-4 on synthetic multimodal instruction-following datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13549)\]. Subsequent studies expanded on this by systematically investigating vision-language instruction tuning, leveraging pretrained models like BLIP-2 and introducing instruction-aware mechanisms such as the Query Transformer to extract task-specific features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. These efforts led to state-of-the-art performance on held-out datasets, with models like InstructBLIP achieving 90.7% accuracy on ScienceQA with image contexts \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14045)\]. Similarly, Video-LLaMA extended instruction tuning to video understanding by addressing challenges such as temporal scene changes and audio-visual integration, using techniques like the Video Q-former and ImageBind for cross-modal alignment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13394)\]. These studies collectively highlight the growing importance of instruction tuning in vision-language models, emphasizing its role in improving both zero-shot and fine-tuned performance across diverse tasks.

Recent advancements in few-shot learning for multimodal tasks have focused on developing models that can rapidly adapt to new tasks with minimal annotated examples. Flamingo, a family of Visual Language Models (VLMs), introduces key architectural innovations to bridge pretrained vision-only and language-only models, enabling seamless handling of interleaved visual and textual data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. This capability allows Flamingo models to be trained on large-scale multimodal web corpora, which is crucial for endowing them with in-context few-shot learning abilities \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]. Similarly, another approach demonstrates that auto-regressive language models can be adapted to a multimodal setting by training a vision encoder to represent images as sequences of continuous embeddings, which are then used to prompt a pre-trained language model to generate appropriate captions \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13884)\]. These models show the ability to learn new tasks with just a few examples, including visual question-answering and understanding novel visual categories. Both approaches highlight the potential of few-shot learning in multimodal settings, where models can be prompted with task-specific examples to achieve state-of-the-art performance across a range of tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13884)\].
