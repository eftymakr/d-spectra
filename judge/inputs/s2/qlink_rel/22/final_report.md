## Related Works

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation has attracted significant attention due to the high cost of pixel-level annotations in real-world applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13589)\]. Traditional UDA methods assume that the category settings between source and target domains are known, which is often not the case in practical scenarios \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. This assumption leads to performance degradation when private classes (classes present in the target domain but not in the source) exist \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. To address this limitation, several approaches have been proposed to handle domain adaptation without prior knowledge of category settings.

One line of research focuses on adversarial learning to align the feature distributions between domains \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\]. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\] introduces an adversarial domain adaptation framework that enforces domain invariance while preserving semantic information. However, these methods often fail to account for category-level discrepancies, leading to misalignment of specific classes \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09478)\]. To address this, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01886)\] proposes a category-level adversarial network that aligns local semantic features, improving the alignment of classes that are originally well-matched between domains.

Another approach involves self-supervised learning and pseudo-labeling to adapt models to the target domain \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10349)\]. For example, \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f9f801aec1f87ae81524f1928407673805927cb)\] employs entropy minimization to generate pseudo-labels, but this can lead to bias towards easy-to-transfer samples \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.02649)\]. To mitigate this, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08040)\] introduces a maximum squares loss to balance the training process and prevent dominance by easy-to-transfer samples. Additionally, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16372)\] proposes an image-wise weighting ratio to alleviate class imbalance in the target domain.

Some works explore the use of Fourier transforms to reduce domain discrepancy \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.09465)\]. This method simplifies domain alignment by swapping the low-frequency spectrum between domains, achieving state-of-the-art performance without complex adversarial training \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12833)\]. Similarly, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05427)\] introduces a curriculum-style learning approach that starts with easy tasks, such as learning global label distributions, to gradually adapt to the target domain \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12197)\].

Moreover, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.03213)\] focuses on improving the quality of pseudo-labels by estimating prediction uncertainty during training, which helps rectify noisy pseudo-labels and improve adaptation performance \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.03773)\]. This approach dynamically sets confidence thresholds based on prediction variance, leading to more reliable pseudo-labels \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9b15362b9a025071aa170f7ed81a761bc057c859)\]. Additionally, \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.10979)\] proposes a method that uses prototype-based pseudo label denoising and target structure learning to enhance domain adaptation performance by aligning features with class centroids \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.7495)\].

To mitigate these gaps, researchers have introduced techniques such as two-step self-supervised domain adaptation, where models are first adapted from the source to an easy subset of the target domain, and then further adapted to the harder subset \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07703)\]. Additionally, bidirectional learning frameworks have been proposed, where image translation and segmentation adaptation models are learned alternately, promoting mutual improvement \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13049)\]. Another approach involves source-free domain adaptation, where the source data is not directly accessible, and instead, knowledge is transferred from a pre-trained source model to the target domain through self-supervised learning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.05498)\]. Furthermore, self-ensembling techniques have been adapted for semantic segmentation, combining data augmentation via GANs with self-ensembling to improve domain alignment and model performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10620)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance domain adaptation for semantic segmentation through self-supervised learning and pseudo-labeling strategies.
