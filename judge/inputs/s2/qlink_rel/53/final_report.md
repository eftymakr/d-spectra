## Related Works

Sequential recommendation systems aim to predict the next item a user will interact with, leveraging the sequential nature of user behavior \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04830)\]. Traditional approaches such as Markov Chains (MCs) assume that a user's next action depends only on their last few actions, while Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are capable of capturing longer-term dependencies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, RNNs often suffer from computational inefficiency and difficulty in modeling long-range dependencies \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00396)\]. To address these limitations, self-attention based models such as SASRec \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\] and BERT4Rec \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] have been proposed. These models utilize attention mechanisms to capture both similar and diverse item relationships, enabling more accurate recommendations. However, they often face challenges in handling long sequences due to quadratic computational complexity \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752)\].

To improve efficiency, models like LinRec \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752)\] and Mamba \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00396)\] have been introduced. LinRec employs a linear attention mechanism to reduce computational complexity while preserving the learning capabilities of traditional attention mechanisms. Mamba, on the other hand, is a state space model that allows for linear-time sequence modeling by leveraging a selective state space mechanism. This enables efficient processing of long sequences while maintaining model expressiveness \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00396)\]. These models have shown significant improvements in both efficiency and performance compared to traditional attention-based approaches \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752)\].

Another line of research focuses on incorporating temporal information into sequential recommendation. TiSASRec \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\] explicitly models the time intervals between interactions, allowing for more accurate predictions by considering the actual timestamps of user actions. Similarly, MEANTIME \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08273)\] introduces multi-temporal embeddings to capture diverse temporal patterns in user behavior. These models demonstrate the importance of temporal information in improving recommendation accuracy \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.08273)\].

Additionally, graph neural networks have been applied to sequential recommendation to better capture complex item transitions and user preferences \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00855)\]. SR-GNN \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00855)\] models sessions as graph-structured data, enabling the capture of intricate item transitions that are difficult to model with traditional sequential methods. ### <a id='category-temporal-modeling-in-sequential-recommendations'></a>Temporal Modeling in Sequential Recommendations: 5 papers found.
Temporal modeling in sequential recommendations has been a focal point in recent research, with a growing emphasis on capturing both the order and timing of user interactions. Traditional models, such as Markov Chains and RNNs, have been widely used to model sequential patterns, but they often neglect the temporal intervals between interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. More recent approaches, like SASRec, have leveraged self-attention mechanisms inspired by the Transformer model, enabling better modeling of sequential dependencies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. However, these models typically rely on simple positional embeddings and fail to incorporate timestamp information, which is crucial for understanding user behavior over time \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. To address this, TiSASRec was introduced, explicitly modeling both the absolute positions of items and the time intervals between them, leading to improved performance on various datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Similarly, the Caser model employs convolutional filters to capture sequential patterns in the time and latent spaces, offering a flexible framework for both general preferences and sequential modeling \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. Additionally, the Personalized Transformer (SSE-PT) model was proposed to overcome the limitations of SASRec by incorporating personalized user embeddings, achieving significant improvements in metrics like NDCG@10 \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. Furthermore, MEANTIME was developed to better utilize timestamp information by employing multiple types of temporal embeddings and an attention structure that leverages diverse positional information, resulting in superior performance on real-world data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. These advancements highlight the importance of temporal modeling in sequential recommendation systems and the ongoing efforts to enhance their ability to capture dynamic user behaviors.

### <a id='category-efficient-attention-mechanisms'></a>Efficient Attention Mechanisms: 1 papers found.
Efficient attention mechanisms have been a focal point in improving the scalability of Transformer-based models, particularly in sequential recommendation tasks where computational complexity poses significant challenges. Traditional dot-product attention mechanisms suffer from quadratic complexity due to the computation of the attention matrix, which becomes infeasible for long sequences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed various efficient attention mechanisms that maintain the expressive power of traditional attention while reducing computational overhead. One such approach is the L2-Normalized Linear Attention, which introduces a linear complexity mechanism that preserves the essential properties of attention \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. This method is theoretically grounded in the equivalence conditions of efficient attention mechanisms, ensuring that the model retains the ability to capture complex interactions while being more computationally efficient \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. Additionally, the linear complexity of this approach allows for better scalability, making it suitable for real-world applications with long user interaction sequences. The effectiveness of such mechanisms has been validated through extensive experiments on benchmark datasets, demonstrating that they can achieve comparable or superior performance to state-of-the-art models while significantly improving time and memory efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. These findings highlight the importance of developing efficient attention mechanisms to enhance the practical applicability of Transformer-based models in sequential recommendation systems.

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-for-sequential-recommendation'></a>Graph Neural Networks for Sequential Recommendation: 4 papers found.
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been increasingly applied to sequential recommendation to better model complex user-item interactions and sequential patterns. Existing methods often struggle with capturing third-order interactions between users, previously visited items, and the next item, which are critical for accurate next-item prediction \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. To address this, TransRec proposes a unified approach by embedding items into a 'transition space' where users are modeled as translation vectors, enabling more effective modeling of sequential behavior \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9c7a455b1e48d01a99e58e884a6c1acb75074ad0)\]. Similarly, the SURGE model tackles the challenges of noisy and dynamic user preferences by integrating long-term user behaviors into clusters through metric learning and constructing tight item-item interest graphs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.14226)\]. This allows for the dynamic extraction of core interests from noisy sequences. Additionally, session-based recommendation methods like SR-GNN model session sequences as graph-structured data, leveraging GNNs to capture complex item transitions that are difficult to reveal with conventional sequential methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. Furthermore, TGSRec introduces a framework that unifies sequential patterns with temporal collaborative signals by designing a Temporal Collaborative Transformer (TCT) layer, which captures both user and item collaborative signals while considering temporal dynamics \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01537)\]. These approaches highlight the growing trend of using GNNs to enhance sequential recommendation by modeling intricate interactions and temporal dynamics more effectively.
