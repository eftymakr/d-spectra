## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning general-purpose representations from raw data. This approach has been widely applied across various domains, including computer vision and natural language processing. However, its application to DNA pre-training has faced challenges due to the limited information content of pure DNA sequences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. In contrast, supervised training for genomic profile prediction has been shown to be more effective, as it leverages additional regulatory signals such as chromatin accessibility \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\]. This is supported by prior work that highlights the importance of incorporating domain-specific knowledge to improve representation learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\].

Several methods have been proposed for unsupervised visual representation learning, such as Contrastive Predictive Coding \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\], which uses a probabilistic contrastive loss to learn latent representations that are useful for predicting future samples. Similarly, the SimCLR framework \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\] simplifies contrastive learning by eliminating the need for specialized architectures or memory banks, and it demonstrates that data augmentation and larger batch sizes can significantly improve representation quality. These methods have been successful in learning robust representations from unlabeled data, but they often require careful design of the predictive tasks.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-methods-for-representation-learning'></a>Contrastive Learning Methods for Representation Learning: 5 papers found.
Contrastive learning methods have gained significant traction in representation learning, offering a powerful unsupervised or self-supervised approach to learning meaningful features from high-dimensional data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. These methods typically rely on contrastive losses to distinguish between similar and dissimilar data points, enabling models to capture rich, predictive representations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. For instance, Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) leverages autoregressive models to predict future samples in latent space, using a probabilistic contrastive loss to ensure the latent space encodes maximally useful information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. This approach has demonstrated strong performance across diverse domains, including speech, images, text, and reinforcement learning in 3D environments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Similarly, SimCLR simplifies contrastive learning by using data augmentations and a learnable nonlinear transformation, achieving state-of-the-art results on ImageNet with linear classifiers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) further advances the field by introducing a dynamic dictionary and moving-averaged encoder, enabling large-scale contrastive learning and effective transfer to downstream tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\]. These methods highlight the potential of contrastive learning to achieve competitive or even superior performance compared to supervised approaches, especially when labeled data is limited \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\]. Additionally, contrastive learning has been applied to Earth observation tasks, where it leverages the abundance of satellite imagery to improve land cover classification, even with sparse ground truth data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. Overall, contrastive learning continues to evolve, offering promising avenues for representation learning in both computer vision and other domains.
