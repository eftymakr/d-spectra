## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a powerful paradigm for information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to encode text into dense vector representations that can be compared using simple similarity operations like inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This approach has been widely studied, with several works exploring the effectiveness of different pre-training and fine-tuning strategies. 

The use of contrastive learning for dense retrieval has been extensively explored, with early work focusing on the effectiveness of fine-tuning in improving retrieval performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. However, recent studies have questioned the extent to which fine-tuning contributes to retrieval performance, suggesting that much of the knowledge required for effective retrieval is already captured during pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This idea was initially tested using BERT-based encoders with the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework as a representative example \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. 

Several works have since extended this analysis by examining different representation strategies, such as the use of CLS tokens versus mean pooling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. For instance, the work by \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\] compared the performance of CLS-based and mean-pooled representations, finding that the latter can lead to better retrieval performance in some cases. Additionally, the role of different backbone architectures, such as encoder-only models like BERT and decoder-only models like LLaMA, has been investigated \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. 

The importance of pre-training in dense retrieval has also been highlighted by works such as \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\], which introduced the Condenser architecture to pre-train models for dense retrieval. This approach aims to improve the quality of dense representations by conditioning the LM prediction on the dense representation during pre-training. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] proposed the coCondenser model, which adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training. 

The effectiveness of fine-tuning in dense retrieval has also been explored in the context of different datasets. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\] evaluated the performance of dense retrievers on the MS-MARCO, Natural Questions, and Trivia QA datasets, showing that simple small batch fine-tuning can achieve comparable performance to heavily engineered systems like RocketQA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\].

### <a id='category-pre-training's-role-in-retrieval-performance'></a>Pre-training's Role in Retrieval Performance: 11 papers found.
The role of pre-training in retrieval performance has been extensively studied, highlighting its critical importance in enabling models to capture and utilize world knowledge effectively. Pre-training allows language models to implicitly store a vast amount of knowledge, which is essential for tasks like open-domain question answering (Open-QA) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This foundational knowledge is often leveraged through retrieval mechanisms, such as dense retrievers, which encode text into representations that can be compared via similarity operations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. Recent work has questioned whether fine-tuning or pre-training is more crucial for retrieval performance, with some arguing that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. This claim was initially tested using BERT-based encoders, but subsequent studies have extended this analysis to other architectures, including encoder-only models like BERT and decoder-only models like LLaMA, as well as different pooling strategies such as CLS token versus mean pooling \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. These studies confirm that pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance in models like DPR, with fine-tuning mainly adjusting neuron activations rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. However, this pattern does not universally apply, as seen in models like Contriever (mean-pooled) and LLaMA-based models, where fine-tuning plays a more significant role in knowledge acquisition \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. Overall, these findings underscore the nuanced interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning in dense retrievers, emphasizing the need for further research into how these components contribute to retrieval effectiveness \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-and-fine-tuning-impact'></a>Contrastive Learning and Fine-tuning Impact: 4 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful approach for training dense retrievers and sentence embeddings without the need for labeled data, offering strong performance across various retrieval settings \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. This method leverages self-supervised objectives to learn universal representations, which can be fine-tuned for specific downstream tasks \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. Research has shown that contrastive learning can significantly improve retrieval performance, particularly when combined with large-scale pre-training, as demonstrated by models like SimCLR that achieve competitive results with minimal labeled data \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15466)\]. Furthermore, contrastive learning has been applied to multi-lingual and cross-lingual retrieval, enabling effective retrieval between different scripts and languages \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. These findings highlight the potential of contrastive learning to bridge the gap between unsupervised and supervised pre-training, making it a valuable technique for dense retrievers and other NLP tasks.

### <a id='category-negative-sampling-techniques-in-training'></a>Negative Sampling Techniques in Training: 6 papers found.
Negative sampling techniques in training dense retrieval models have been extensively explored to improve retrieval performance and address the limitations of traditional methods. Several approaches have been proposed to enhance the quality of negative samples, which are critical for contrastive learning. For instance, ANCE introduces an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index to construct more realistic negative instances during training, aligning the data distribution with testing conditions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. Similarly, TAS-Balanced employs topic-aware query clustering and balanced margin sampling to improve resource efficiency and achieve state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\]. RocketQA further advances the field by introducing cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives, and data augmentation strategies, significantly outperforming previous models on tasks like MSMARCO and Natural Questions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. Theoretical analyses have also been conducted to explain the effectiveness of hard negative sampling, leading to the development of dynamic training strategies such as STAR and ADORE, which enhance stability and ranking performance \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07577)\]. Additionally, knowledge distillation techniques, such as those used in ColBERT-based models, enable efficient training by transferring knowledge from a bi-encoder teacher to a student model, allowing for in-batch negative sampling during distillation \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.03611)\]. These methods collectively highlight the importance of carefully designed negative sampling strategies in achieving robust and efficient dense retrieval systems.
