## Related Works

The segmentation of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been a focal point of research due to its clinical significance in disease monitoring and treatment evaluation. Early methods relied on manual segmentation, which is time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-observer variability \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. To address these limitations, automated segmentation techniques have been extensively explored. These methods often leverage advanced image processing algorithms and machine learning models to improve accuracy and consistency \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. 

Several studies have focused on improving the detection of specific lesion types, such as chronic active lesions, which are characterized by a paramagnetic rim and are associated with increased disability \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. The identification of these lesions is crucial for understanding disease progression and response to treatment. However, manual detection remains challenging due to the variability in lesion appearance and the need for expert interpretation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Automated methods, such as those based on deep learning, have shown promise in improving both the speed and accuracy of lesion detection \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5d7b728c6713930325216755d6d0c331b4dc915d)\]. 

A significant challenge in MS lesion segmentation is the class imbalance between rim and non-rim lesions, as rim lesions are relatively rare \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. This imbalance can negatively impact the performance of classifiers, particularly in deep learning models that require balanced data for optimal training \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13755)\]. To address this issue, researchers have proposed synthetic data generation techniques to augment the minority class. For instance, some studies have used generative adversarial networks (GANs) to create synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps (QSMs) of paramagnetic rim lesions, thereby improving classifier performance \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ebff27a8db308b40ebb43fe974403369d56685ef)\]. These synthetic data can be used to train models on ambiguous cases and increase the representation of the minority class \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12470)\]. 

Additionally, there has been growing interest in the use of multi-channel data and probabilistic segmentation maps to enhance the accuracy of lesion detection. For example, some methods have integrated T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences with QSM data to improve the distinction between rim and non-rim lesions \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.02001)\]. These multi-modal approaches have shown improvements in both segmentation accuracy and robustness to variations in image acquisition protocols \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07950)\]. 

Recent advances in deep learning have also focused on improving the generalization of models across different datasets and clinical settings. Techniques such as domain adaptation and transfer learning have been explored to enable models trained on one dataset to perform well on unseen data \[[20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3fe6e8e47663a38c037813bc35524fe0931b31b2)\]. These approaches are particularly important for clinical applications where the availability of labeled data is limited \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01200)\].
