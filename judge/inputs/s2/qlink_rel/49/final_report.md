## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in web search and open-domain question answering. These models typically rely on dense representations to compute relevance scores between queries and documents \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. However, training such models requires a large amount of labeled query-document pairs, which are often expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. To address this challenge, synthetic queries generated by large language models have emerged as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, the quality of synthetic queries can vary, leading to suboptimal retrieval performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Existing methods often filter out noisy query-document pairs using external re-rankers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, this work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO is a method that simplifies the alignment of large language models with human preferences by eliminating the need for reward modeling and reinforcement learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Instead, DPO uses a simple classification loss to directly optimize the model's policy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach is stable, computationally lightweight, and performs well on tasks such as summarization and dialogue \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Several studies have explored the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align language models with human preferences \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. RLHF typically involves training a reward model to reflect human preferences and then fine-tuning the language model using reinforcement learning to maximize this estimated reward \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, RLHF is complex and often unstable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. DPO addresses these limitations by directly optimizing the policy using preference data without the need for a reward model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach is simpler to implement and train, and it has been shown to outperform existing methods in terms of alignment with human preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the quality of synthetic queries by incorporating preference signals during training \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14734)\]. SimPO, a new approach that uses a reference-free reward, has been shown to outperform DPO in several tasks \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14734)\]. ### <a id='category-reward-modeling-and-reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback-(rlhf)'></a>Reward Modeling and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF): 10 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a significant advancement in aligning large language models with human preferences, offering a more stable and efficient alternative to traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Unlike conventional approaches that require a separate reward modeling stage and complex hyperparameter tuning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)\], DPO simplifies the fine-tuning process by directly optimizing the policy using a classification loss, eliminating the need for sampling from the language model during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach has been shown to outperform PPO-based RLHF in controlling the sentiment of generated text and matches or improves response quality in summarization and dialogue tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Furthermore, DPO's simplicity and computational efficiency make it a compelling choice for practical deployment \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, DPO still relies on the assumption that pairwise preferences can be substituted with pointwise rewards, a limitation that recent work has sought to address by proposing new objectives like $\Psi$PO, which bypasses both approximations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. Additionally, methods like Direct Nash Optimization (DNO) have been introduced to further enhance the alignment process by directly optimizing over pairwise preferences, achieving state-of-the-art results in tasks such as summarization and dialogue \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of RLHF techniques, emphasizing the importance of integrating human feedback directly into the training process to improve model performance and alignment with user intent \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325)\].

### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 2 papers found.
Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a promising approach for aligning large language models with human preferences without requiring reward models or reinforcement learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. DPO fine-tunes models using preference data, increasing the likelihood of preferred responses over dispreferred ones \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. However, not all preference pairs are equally informative, and some may require stronger likelihood differences to be effectively modeled \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed DPO with an offset (ODPO), which introduces an offset value to ensure that the likelihood difference between preferred and dispreferred responses meets a minimum threshold \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. This approach improves alignment, particularly when the number of preference pairs is limited \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. Additionally, DPO has been extended to DPO-Positive (DPOP), a loss function that avoids a known failure mode where standard DPO can reduce the likelihood of preferred examples \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. DPOP has been shown to outperform DPO and other fine-tuning methods across various datasets and tasks, including those with high edit distances between completions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. These advancements highlight the flexibility and effectiveness of DPO-based methods in improving model performance and alignment with human preferences.
