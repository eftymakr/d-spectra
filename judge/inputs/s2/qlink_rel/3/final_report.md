## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9f2dd5cc190fc713f1339fca838a5537931744f8)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Similarly, conventional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are impractical in low-resource settings \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08947)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges, focusing on methods for prosody control, TTS quality improvement, and controllable speech synthesis.

Several studies have explored prosody modeling in TTS systems. For example, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\] introduced a fully automatic method that makes the system aware of prosody, enabling sentence-wise control of speaking pace and expressiveness. This approach improves the overall quality and expressiveness of synthesized speech. Similarly, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02479)\] proposed a system for fine-grained prosody transfer from one speaker to another, addressing the issue of speaker variability in single-speaker datasets. The method uses a variational autoencoder to enhance the latent representation of prosody embeddings, leading to more stable and reliable prosody transfer.

In the context of controllable TTS, \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\] introduced "global style tokens" (GSTs), a bank of embeddings trained within Tacotron to model a wide range of acoustic expressiveness. These embeddings allow for the control of speaking style and speed independently of text content, offering interpretable and flexible control. Additionally, \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08352)\] proposed a model that explicitly conditions speech generation on acoustic correlates of prosody, such as $F_0$, energy, and duration. This model enables flexible and interpretable control over prosody, improving the naturalness of generated speech compared to Tacotron 2.

Non-autoregressive TTS models have also been explored for their efficiency and parallelism. \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\] proposed Parallel Tacotron, which incorporates a variational autoencoder-based residual encoder to improve naturalness and enable parallel processing. This model significantly reduces inference time while maintaining high-quality speech synthesis. Similarly, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\] introduced Glow-TTS, a flow-based generative model that enables parallel TTS without requiring external aligners. This model enforces monotonic alignments and uses generative flows to achieve fast and diverse speech synthesis.

For prosody control in end-to-end TTS, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\] proposed a fully hierarchical fine-grained prosody modeling approach, enabling interpretable and multi-resolution prosody modeling

### <a id='category-prosody-control-in-tts-systems'></a>Prosody Control in TTS Systems: 2 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly improved speech naturalness, but precise prosody control remains a challenge, particularly in long, multi-sentence inputs \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. While modern sequence-to-sequence neural TTS systems generate high-quality speech, the prosody—encompassing phoneme durations, pitch, and loudness—is implicitly encoded in acoustic features and difficult to manipulate without additional training or specialized modules \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. This limitation is exacerbated when no prosodic labeling is available in the training data, making post-hoc adjustments challenging \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. To address this, researchers have explored unsupervised methods for speaking style learning and generation, though human inspection is still required for interpretation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Recent work has focused on improving prosody through long context modeling, powerful text features, and multi-speaker training, which enhance coherence and disambiguate prosody \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. These approaches leverage the strengths of Transformer architectures and fine-tune word-level features from language models like BERT, leading to statistically significant improvements in speech naturalness \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14643)\]. Additionally, novel attention mechanisms have been proposed to improve pace control sensitivity and attention convergence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance prosody control in TTS systems, paving the way for more flexible and editable speech synthesis.

The training of FastSpeech model relies on an autoregressive teacher model for duration prediction (to provide more information as input) and knowledge distillation (to simplify the data distribution in output), which can ease the one-to-many mapping problem (i.e., multiple speech variations correspond to the same text) in TTS. We further design FastSpeech 2s, which is the first attempt to directly generate speech waveform from text in parallel, enjoying the benefit of full end-to-end training and even faster inference than FastSpeech. Experimental results show that 1) FastSpeech 2 and 2s outperform FastSpeech in voice quality with much simplified training pipeline and reduced training time; 2) FastSpeech 2 and 2s can match the voice quality of autoregressive models while enjoying much faster inference speed.»

### <a id='category-controllable-speech-synthesis-methods'></a>Controllable Speech Synthesis Methods: 17 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) have significantly improved speech naturalness, increasing the demand for precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05884)\]. Existing approaches for prosody manipulation often depend on specialized modules or additional training, limiting their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.02479)\]. Similarly, traditional mispronunciation correction relies on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making it less practical in low-resource settings \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05884)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed various controllable speech synthesis methods that enable fine-grained control over prosody and other speech attributes. For instance, prosody embeddings have been introduced to enable emotional and expressive speech synthesis by learning to extract and adjust prosodic features such as pitch and amplitude at both frame and phoneme levels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. These embeddings are often integrated into end-to-end speech synthesis networks, allowing for robust prosody transfer even across speakers \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\]. Additionally, models like FastPitch and Non-Attentive Tacotron have been developed to condition on fundamental frequency contours and duration predictors, respectively, enabling more expressive and semantically aligned speech generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.02122)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. Hierarchical latent variable models, such as those based on variational autoencoders, have also been proposed to disentangle and control different aspects of prosody, including pitch, duration, and energy, while maintaining high synthesis quality \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]. Furthermore, methods like Global Style Tokens (GSTs) and Text-Predicting Global Style Tokens (TP-GST) have been introduced to learn interpretable and disentangled representations of speaking style, allowing for style transfer and post-hoc adjustments without retraining \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11439)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the growing trend toward more flexible and controllable TTS systems that can adapt to diverse speaking styles and correct pronunciation issues during inference \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.05884)\].

### <a id='category-parallel-processing-in-tts'></a>Parallel Processing in TTS: 2 papers found.
Recent advances in Text-to-Speech (TTS) have focused on improving speech naturalness and enabling more flexible prosody control, with several studies addressing the limitations of traditional two-stage TTS systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. For instance, parallel end-to-end TTS methods have been proposed to enable single-stage training and parallel sampling, although their sample quality often lags behind two-stage models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. To address this, researchers have introduced variational inference combined with normalizing flows and adversarial training, enhancing the expressive power of generative modeling and allowing for more natural-sounding audio \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Additionally, stochastic duration predictors have been developed to synthesize speech with diverse rhythms, capturing the one-to-many relationship in speech generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Meanwhile, the adoption of Transformer networks has significantly improved training efficiency and long-range dependency modeling in TTS, with multi-head attention mechanisms enabling parallel processing and better performance compared to recurrent neural networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to balance efficiency, quality, and flexibility in TTS systems.
