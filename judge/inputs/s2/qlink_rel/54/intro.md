## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early works such as Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) aimed to integrate user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, these models often included unnecessary nonlinearities and feature transformations, which hindered training efficiency and performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. LightGCN simplified GCN by removing these components, focusing only on neighborhood aggregation, which led to substantial improvements in recommendation accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. 

The use of graph convolutions in recommendation systems has also been explored through the lens of spectral graph theory. For example, Spectral Collaborative Filtering (SpectralCF) introduced a new spectral convolution operation that directly operates in the spectral domain, capturing both proximity and connectivity information \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. This approach enabled the discovery of deep connections between users and items, effectively alleviating the cold-start problem \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. Similarly, the work on Graph Attention Networks (GATs) introduced masked self-attentional layers to address the shortcomings of prior methods based on graph convolutions, allowing for more flexible and interpretable models \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\].

In the context of large-scale recommendation systems, the challenge of scalability has been addressed through efficient graph convolutional algorithms. For instance, the work on GraphSAGE introduced an inductive framework that generates embeddings for previously unseen data by sampling and aggregating features from a node's local neighborhood \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. This approach is particularly useful for evolving graphs and large datasets. Additionally, the paper on UltraGCN proposed an ultra-simplified formulation of GCNs by skipping infinite layers of message passing, leading to faster training and improved performance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\].

Another important line of research focuses on the modeling of high-order relationships in user-item graphs. Knowledge Graph Attention Networks (KGAT) explicitly model high-order connectivities in knowledge graphs, using an attention mechanism to discriminate the importance of neighbors \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. This approach allows for more accurate and interpretable recommendations by leveraging the rich relational structure of knowledge graphs. Similarly, the work on Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) aimed to disentangle user intents by modeling a distribution over intents for each user-item interaction, leading to more refined and interpretable representations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\].
