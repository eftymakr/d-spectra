## Related Works

The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) has underscored the critical need for benchmarking their fairness, as these models are increasingly used in high-stakes applications \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations and fail to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty, such as higher confidence in predictions for certain groups despite similar accuracy \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. This limitation has been widely recognized in the literature, where researchers have emphasized the importance of incorporating uncertainty into fairness assessments \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\]. For instance, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.07593)\] presents a framework that mitigates biases by including a variable for the group of interest and simultaneously learning a predictor and an adversary, which helps reduce stereotyping in predictions. This approach aligns with the idea that fairness should not only consider accuracy but also the confidence associated with predictions \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\].

The need for diverse and representative datasets for evaluating fairness in LLMs has also been highlighted. Current datasets often suffer from issues related to data size, diversity, and clarity, which can lead to biased evaluations \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. To address this, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00133)\] introduces the CrowS-Pairs dataset, which measures social biases in masked language models by presenting models with pairs of sentences that reflect different levels of stereotyping. This dataset is particularly useful for evaluating biases related to protected demographic groups in the US. Similarly, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\] proposes new fairness metrics for recommendation systems that account for differences in user groups and show that these metrics can better measure fairness than existing ones. These datasets and metrics are crucial for developing more accurate and representative evaluations of LLMs.

In addition to dataset improvements, there is a growing body of work on fairness definitions and frameworks. \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\] introduces the concept of counterfactual fairness, which requires that a decision be the same in the actual world and a counterfactual world where the individual belongs to a different demographic group. This definition has been influential in shaping the discourse around fairness in machine learning. \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\] further explores fairness through awareness, proposing a framework that ensures similar individuals are treated similarly while maintaining utility for the classifier. These approaches provide foundational insights into how fairness can be operationalized in different contexts.
