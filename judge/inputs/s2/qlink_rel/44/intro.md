## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching. Early approaches primarily relied on matrix factorization techniques to learn latent representations of users and items \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, these methods were limited in capturing complex interactions between users and items. More recently, deep learning techniques have been introduced to model such interactions, leading to the development of Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. NCF replaces the inner product used in matrix factorization with a neural network architecture, allowing for non-linear modeling of user-item interactions. This approach has shown significant improvements over traditional methods, particularly when deeper neural networks are employed.

Graph-based methods have further advanced the field by incorporating the bipartite graph structure of user-item interactions. Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the graph, thereby capturing high-order connectivity. LightGCN \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] simplifies this process by removing unnecessary non-linearities and focusing on neighborhood aggregation, achieving substantial improvements over NGCF. Similarly, Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF) \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\] introduces a method to disentangle user intents, leading to more interpretable and diverse representations.

The importance of graph convolutional networks (GCNs) in recommendation systems has also been highlighted. Graph Convolutional Neural Networks for Web-Scale Recommender Systems \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\] demonstrate how GCNs can be scaled to handle massive graphs with billions of nodes and edges. This work introduces an efficient training strategy and inference algorithm, making GCNs practical for real-world applications. Simplifying Graph Convolutional Networks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\] further reduces complexity by removing non-linearities and collapsing weight matrices, resulting in a linear model that is easier to train and scales better to large datasets.

In addition to GCNs, other approaches such as Variational Autoencoders for Collaborative Filtering \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\] have been proposed to model latent factors in a probabilistic framework. These models leverage Bayesian inference and regularization to improve performance. Similarly, Self-supervised Graph Learning \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\] introduces auxiliary tasks to enhance the robustness of GCNs by mitigating the impact of noisy interactions and high-degree nodes.

Overall, these works highlight the evolving landscape of recommendation systems, where graph-based methods and deep learning techniques are increasingly being combined to capture complex user-item interactions.
