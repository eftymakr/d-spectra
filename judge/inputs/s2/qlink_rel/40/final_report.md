Recent efforts have focused on accelerating the key primitives of ZKP protocols using hardware, particularly GPUs and ASICs, to reduce the time required for proof generation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\]. However, existing accelerations often face limitations such as requiring a trusted setup for each application or generating proofs with larger sizes and higher verification costs, which restrict their use in scenarios with numerous verifiers or strict time constraints \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\].

Several works have explored hardware acceleration for ZKP protocols. For instance, GZKP leverages GPU acceleration to improve performance for polynomial computations and multi-scalar multiplications, achieving speedups of up to 48.1× and 17.6× over existing systems \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\]. Similarly, PipeZK introduces a pipelined architecture with two subsystems to handle intensive compute tasks, achieving 10× speedup on cryptographic benchmarks and 5× on Zcash \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of optimizing memory access patterns and parallelism for efficient ZKP generation \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\].

The challenge of accelerating entire ZKP protocols, rather than just individual primitives, has also been addressed. It achieves over 400× speedup over CPU implementations and 12× over GPU implementations \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\]. These results underscore the potential of hardware acceleration to significantly reduce proof generation time, making ZKPs more practical for real-world applications \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e149d93531d8c07cf43f6cae327a4795144d1304)\].

The main bottleneck is typically the extra effort required by the prover to return an answer with a guarantee of correctness, compared to returning an answer with no guarantee. 
We describe a refinement of a powerful interactive proof protocol originally due to Goldwasser, Kalai, and Rothblum. Cormode, Mitzenmacher, and Thaler show how to implement the prover in this protocol in time O(S log S), where S is the size of an arithmetic circuit computing the function of interest. Our refinements apply to circuits whose wiring pattern is sufficiently "regular"; for these circuits, we bring the runtime of the prover down to O(S). That is, our prover can evaluate the circuit with a guarantee of correctness, with only a constant-factor blowup in work compared to evaluating the circuit with no guarantee. 
We argue that our refinements capture a large class of circuits, and prove some theorems formalizing this. Experimentally, our refinements yield a 200x speedup for the prover over the implementation of Cormode et al., and our prover is less than 10x slower than a C++ program that simply evaluates the circuit. Along the way, we describe a special-purpose protocol for matrix multiplication that is of interest in its own right. 
Our final contribution is a protocol targeted at general data parallel computation. Compared to prior work, this protocol can more efficiently verify complicated computations as long as that computation is applied independently to many pieces of data.»
