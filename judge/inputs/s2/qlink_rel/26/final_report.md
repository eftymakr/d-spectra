## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has become the de facto standard for ensuring privacy in data analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. However, its implementation in distributed settings is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, such as incorrect noise sampling or correlated noise generation \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. These issues have been explored in several prior works. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\] discusses the vulnerabilities of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols to manipulation attacks, where adversaries can obscure the true distribution of user inputs by injecting fake users. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\] highlights the risks of data poisoning attacks in LDP protocols, where attackers inject fake data to manipulate the output of frequency estimation algorithms. These studies emphasize the need for robustness in LDP protocols against adversarial manipulation.

Several works have focused on improving the efficiency and accuracy of LDP mechanisms. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\] introduces a new approach to frequency estimation under LDP by leveraging post-processing steps that exploit the non-negativity and normalization of frequency distributions. This leads to significant improvements in accuracy for a wide range of tasks. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\] proposes the use of the discrete Gaussian distribution for differential privacy, which provides comparable accuracy to continuous Gaussian noise while being more interpretable for discrete data. This is particularly relevant for applications such as counting queries and low-sensitivity integer-valued queries.

The shuffled model of differential privacy, introduced in \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0023582fde36430c7e3ae81611a14e558c8f4bae)\], offers a middle ground between the local and central models by introducing an anonymous channel that randomly permutes user messages. This model allows for greater accuracy than the local model while avoiding the need for a trusted server. Additionally, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07387)\] presents a novel approach to discrete distribution estimation under local privacy, demonstrating that mechanisms like hashed K-ary Randomized Response (KRR) can achieve better utility than existing methods across all privacy levels.

The importance of robustness and verification in DP mechanisms has also been explored. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\] discusses the use of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) in achieving verifiable differential privacy, showing that while ZKPs are sufficient for ensuring privacy under verifiability requirements, they are not strictly necessary. This insight is crucial for the development of verifiable differentially private mechanisms. ### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-(ldp)-challenges'></a>Local Differential Privacy (LDP) Challenges: 5 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) has been widely studied for its ability to protect user privacy while enabling the collection of useful statistics. Existing research highlights the challenges of repeated data collection under LDP, particularly for counter data such as daily app usage, where privacy guarantees degrade over time \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. To address this, new mechanisms have been developed that provide formal privacy guarantees even after long-term use, achieving comparable or better accuracy than single-round LDP methods \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. Additionally, the privacy cost of LDP algorithms can be lower in the central model, as demonstrated by a new privacy amplification technique that shows certain LDP algorithms satisfy central differential privacy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. This suggests that some LDP-based deployments may have lower privacy costs than previously thought, especially when reports are anonymized \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Furthermore, LDP is being applied to multidimensional data, with novel mechanisms that outperform existing solutions in terms of noise variance, enabling tasks like stochastic gradient descent in machine learning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\]. RAPPOR, another LDP-based approach, allows for the collection of population statistics on strings with strong privacy guarantees, supporting both data collection and analysis \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. These developments highlight ongoing efforts to improve the practicality and efficiency of LDP while maintaining strong privacy protections.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-foundations'></a>Differential Privacy Foundations: 12 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy Foundations" encompasses foundational research on differential privacy (DP), including its theoretical underpinnings, practical implementations, and extensions to address privacy in complex scenarios. Early work established DP as a rigorous framework for ensuring privacy in statistical databases by introducing the concept of adding noise to query results to obscure individual contributions \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/0811.2841)\]. This foundational idea was later refined with the introduction of the Rényi divergence-based relaxation of DP, which allows for tighter analysis of composite mechanisms and better tail bounds on privacy loss \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.07476)\]. The notion of sequential querying and its impact on privacy degradation was also explored, with a complete characterization of privacy loss as a function of query count and individual privacy levels \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1311.0776)\]. Furthermore, the category includes studies on the limitations of uniform privacy guarantees, leading to the development of personalized differential privacy (PDP), which allows for varying privacy levels based on user-specific requirements \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00133)\]. The category also addresses the trade-off between utility and privacy, proposing mechanisms such as the geometric mechanism that achieve near-optimal utility while maintaining differential privacy \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/0803.0924)\]. Additionally, research has been conducted on the relationship between zero-knowledge proofs (ZKP) and DP, showing that while ZKPs can be used to achieve verifiable DP, they are not strictly necessary \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00133)\]. These foundational contributions have laid the groundwork for more advanced applications, such as verifiable distributed differential privacy (VDDP), which incorporates verification mechanisms to address adversarial behaviors in distributed settings \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00133)\].

### <a id='category-data-poisoning-and-manipulation-attacks'></a>Data Poisoning and Manipulation Attacks: 2 papers found.
Local differential privacy (LDP) protocols, which enable privacy-preserving data analytics by allowing users to perturb their data before sending it to an untrusted collector, have been extensively studied for their utility and deployment in large-scale systems \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. However, these protocols are inherently vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can significantly distort the aggregated results, especially when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This vulnerability is exacerbated by the fact that any noninteractive LDP protocol can be manipulated to a greater extent than non-malicious users can influence the outcome \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Additionally, attackers can inject fake users who send carefully crafted data to the collector, leading to inaccurate frequency estimates or false heavy hitter identifications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These data poisoning attacks highlight critical security gaps in existing LDP protocols, as the focus of prior research has primarily been on improving utility rather than addressing security concerns \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. While some countermeasures have been proposed, such as those that introduce verification mechanisms or leverage cryptographic techniques like zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs), these solutions are not universally effective and often come with trade-offs in efficiency or accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. The need for more robust and efficient defenses against such attacks remains a key challenge in the field of LDP.

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-of-differential-privacy'></a>Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy: 1 papers found.
The Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy represents a middle ground between the central and local models, offering a balance between accuracy and trust assumptions \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Unlike the central model, which requires a trusted server to aggregate data, and the local model, where each user individually randomizes their data before sending it, the shuffled model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes messages before they are aggregated \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This approach allows for greater accuracy than the local model while avoiding the need for a fully trusted central server \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. The shuffled model is a special case of the ESA framework \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\], and it has been shown to provide the power of the central model in certain scenarios, such as sum queries, without requiring a trusted server or complex cryptographic protocols \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. However, the model's power is not as strong as the central model in all cases; for instance, in a restricted setting, shuffled protocols for selection problems require exponentially higher sample complexity than central-model protocols \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. This highlights the trade-offs inherent in the shuffled model and underscores the need for further research into its capabilities and limitations. The model's simplicity and efficiency make it a promising direction for achieving scalable and robust privacy-preserving computation in distributed settings.
