## Related Works

3D human pose estimation has been extensively studied, with a variety of approaches addressing the challenges of depth ambiguity, self-occlusion, and temporal inconsistency \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/50167a9e6bdc1f64a58fe8a3c990105fec0bd2bc)\]. Early methods relied on hand-crafted features and probabilistic models, but recent advances have shifted towards deep learning-based approaches \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/555a23a05777a0b03614b195c7c72f7c626a97e9)\]. These methods typically leverage either 2D pose detections or direct 3D regression, often incorporating spatial and temporal dependencies to improve accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.10455)\].

A significant body of work focuses on modeling spatial relationships using graph convolutional networks (GCNs) \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/30de7fecb146356d7ae6cafa072997674601e0f5)\]. These networks exploit the human skeleton's structure to capture local and global dependencies, often incorporating domain knowledge about body configurations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.03098)\]. For instance, the work by \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06937)\] introduces a local-to-global network architecture that learns multi-scale features for graph-based representations, while \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.09389)\] proposes a bone-based pose decomposition approach that separates the estimation of bone directions and lengths. This method leverages the consistency of bone lengths across time to improve accuracy \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.00159)\].

Temporal modeling is another critical aspect of 3D human pose estimation, especially when dealing with video inputs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16385)\]. Several approaches utilize recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or long short-term memory (LSTM) units to model temporal coherence \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12707)\]. However, these methods often suffer from high computational costs and limited receptive fields \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08585)\]. In contrast, attention-based mechanisms have emerged as a promising alternative, allowing for more flexible and efficient modeling of temporal dependencies \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/db466d02ec9f7f47ee11590d0feeb6330256c98d)\]. For example, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14304)\] introduces an attentional mechanism that adaptively identifies significant frames and tensor outputs, leading to more accurate and temporally consistent estimations \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13985)\].

Transformers have also gained traction in 3D human pose estimation due to their ability to model long-range dependencies \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.03345)\]. The PoseFormer \[[16](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0646857f867d9a624f2b53d1ffb4de99e225b2d3)\] and GraFormer \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07319)\] approaches demonstrate that transformer-based architectures can effectively capture both spatial and temporal relationships without relying on convolutional operations. These models often incorporate multi-head attention mechanisms to enhance feature interactions \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/442928cd95d7bca15800b29ce2e2f8db5461bd58)\].

Another line of research focuses on improving generalization, especially for rare or unseen poses \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/22e305b7b66bdedf379fa678802cb46e8bb546b1)\]. The SRNet \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04095)\] introduces a split-and-recombine approach that processes local body regions separately before recombining global context, leading to better performance on rare poses \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07181)\]. Similarly, \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.09813)\] proposes a hierarchical channel-squeezing fusion layer to extract relevant information from neighboring nodes while suppressing noise in graph neural networks (GNNs) \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07628)\].
