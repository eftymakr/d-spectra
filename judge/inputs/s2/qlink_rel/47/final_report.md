## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to enhance recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge transfer across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Traditional MDR methods often assume a uniform structure for knowledge transfer, which may not be suitable for all scenarios due to the heterogeneity of domains and the potential for negative transfer \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] highlights that existing MDR methods typically share parameters between domains, which can lead to mutual interference and poor generalization. Similarly, \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\] points out that static parameter settings limit the flexibility of models to adapt to diverse domains, especially when data distributions differ significantly.

To address these limitations, several studies have proposed alternative frameworks. For example, \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\] introduces a probabilistic framework that allows for adaptive knowledge transfer by learning domain correlations. This approach enables the model to adjust the weight of information from different domains based on their relevance. Additionally, \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\] proposes a multi-task learning approach that shares embeddings of common users across domains, thereby improving performance on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously. This aligns with the idea of dual-target cross-domain recommendation, where the goal is to improve performance on multiple domains rather than a single one \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59)\].

Another line of research focuses on the challenges of knowledge transfer and how to avoid the negative transfer problem (NTP). \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.3535)\] emphasizes that unselectively leveraging information from all domains can lead to NTP, especially when there is a conflict between domains or when a domain has poor quality data. To mitigate this, \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb919954e93726aff490458bd61d6b3d8155fbd7)\] proposes a method that dynamically selects relevant domains based on domain-specific metrics and unsupervised distance measures. This is closely related to the Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) introduced in this paper, which aims to explicitly measure domain-level gaps and dynamically select appropriate domains for knowledge transfer \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\].

Moreover, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\] introduces a novel framework that uses adversarial feature translation to capture domain-specific features and improve cross-domain recommendations. This approach is particularly useful in scenarios where domains have different types of user-item interactions and content information. Similarly, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\] proposes a model that disentangles generalizable knowledge from domain-specific knowledge, which is crucial for improving performance across multiple domains with overlapping users and items.

In addition to these methods, \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\] explores the use of graph-based models to capture high-order information propagation across domains, which can enhance the accuracy of recommendations. These approaches highlight the importance of modeling complex domain relationships and adapting to the unique characteristics of each domain.

### <a id='category-cross-domain-recommendation-paradigms'></a>Cross-Domain Recommendation Paradigms: 5 papers found.
Cross-domain recommendation (CDR) has emerged as a critical paradigm to address data sparsity in recommender systems by leveraging knowledge from multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Various approaches have been proposed to enhance transfer learning between domains, such as collaborative cross networks (CoNet), which utilize deep neural networks to model complex user-item interactions through cross-mappings between domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Additionally, the Deep Dual Transfer Cross Domain Recommendation (DDTCDR) model introduces a dual learning mechanism to iteratively transfer information between domains while preserving latent relations between users and items \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods aim to improve recommendation accuracy by effectively managing knowledge transfer and mitigating the negative transfer problem, which occurs when conflicting or low-quality domain information degrades performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Recent work has also focused on sequential recommendation in shared-account and cross-domain settings, introducing models like π-Net that address challenges such as identifying user-specific behaviors and transferring information across domains \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\]. These studies collectively highlight the importance of dynamic knowledge selection and efficient transfer mechanisms in multi-domain recommendation systems.

[Snippet]: «In order to address the data sparsity problem in recommender systems, in recent years, Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR) leverages the relatively richer information from a source domain to improve the recommendation performance on a target domain with sparser information. However, each of the two domains may be relatively richer in certain types of information (e.g., ratings, reviews, user profiles, item details, and tags), and thus, if we can leverage such information well, it is possible to improve the recommendation performance on both domains simultaneously (i.e., dual-target CDR), rather than a single target domain only. To this end, in this paper, we propose a new framework, DTCDR, for Dual-Target Cross-Domain Recommendation. In DTCDR, we first extensively utilize rating and multi-source content information to generate rating and document embeddings of users and items. Then, based on Multi-Task Learning (MTL), we design an adaptable embedding-sharing strategy to combine and share the embeddings of common users across domains, with which DTCDR can improve the recommendation performance on both richer and sparser (i.e., dual-target) domains simultaneously. [Snippet]: «Recommendation can be framed as a graph link prediction task naturally. The user-item interaction graph built within a single domain often suffers from high sparsity. Thus, there has been a surge of approaches to alleviate the sparsity issue via cross-domain mutual augmentation. The SOTA cross-domain recommendation algorithms all try to bridge the gap via knowledge transfer in the latent space. We find there are mainly three problems in their formulations: 1) their knowledge transfer is unaware of the cross-domain graph structure. 2) their framework cannot capture high-order information propagation on the graph. 3) their cross-domain transfer formulations are generally more complicated to be optimized than the unified methods. In this paper, we propose the Preference Propagation GraphNet (PPGN) to address the above problems. Specifically, we construct a Cross-Domain Preference Matrix (CDPM) to model the interactions of different domains as a whole. Through the propagation layer of PPGN, we try to capture how user preferences propagate in the graph. Consequently, a joint objective for different domains is defined, and we simplify the cross-domain recommendation into a unified multi-task model. Extensive experiments on two pairs of real-world datasets show PPGN outperforms the SOTA algorithms significantly.»

By leveraging the knowledge from relevant domains, the cross-domain recommendation technique can be an effective way of alleviating the data sparsity problem.
