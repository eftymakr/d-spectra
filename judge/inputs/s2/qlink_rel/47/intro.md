## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to enhance recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge transfer across different domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01696)\]. Traditional MDR methods often assume a uniform structure for knowledge transfer, which may not be suitable for all scenarios due to the heterogeneity of domains and the potential for negative transfer \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] highlights that existing MDR methods typically share parameters between domains, which can lead to mutual interference and poor generalization. Similarly, \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\] points out that static parameter settings limit the flexibility of models to adapt to diverse domains, especially when data distributions differ significantly.

To address these limitations, several studies have proposed alternative frameworks. For example, \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\] introduces a probabilistic framework that allows for adaptive knowledge transfer by learning domain correlations. This approach enables the model to adjust the weight of information from different domains based on their relevance. Additionally, \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d5630291a4ba3f6266c77e6d00920502f986afa6)\] proposes a multi-task learning approach that shares embeddings of common users across domains, thereby improving performance on both richer and sparser domains simultaneously. This aligns with the idea of dual-target cross-domain recommendation, where the goal is to improve performance on multiple domains rather than a single one \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7807d924fa46caec7b706b98156962caf8458d59)\].

Another line of research focuses on the challenges of knowledge transfer and how to avoid the negative transfer problem (NTP). \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.3535)\] emphasizes that unselectively leveraging information from all domains can lead to NTP, especially when there is a conflict between domains or when a domain has poor quality data. To mitigate this, \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/cb919954e93726aff490458bd61d6b3d8155fbd7)\] proposes a method that dynamically selects relevant domains based on domain-specific metrics and unsupervised distance measures. This is closely related to the Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) introduced in this paper, which aims to explicitly measure domain-level gaps and dynamically select appropriate domains for knowledge transfer \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\].

Moreover, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\] introduces a novel framework that uses adversarial feature translation to capture domain-specific features and improve cross-domain recommendations. This approach is particularly useful in scenarios where domains have different types of user-item interactions and content information. Similarly, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\] proposes a model that disentangles generalizable knowledge from domain-specific knowledge, which is crucial for improving performance across multiple domains with overlapping users and items.

In addition to these methods, \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\] explores the use of graph-based models to capture high-order information propagation across domains, which can enhance the accuracy of recommendations. These approaches highlight the importance of modeling complex domain relationships and adapting to the unique characteristics of each domain.
