## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising pre-training objective for language models, offering potential improvements in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency compared to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach involves predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple prediction heads, which can enhance the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies and improve generative capabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) often struggle with the MTP objective, necessitating the development of curriculum learning strategies to facilitate effective training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Several studies have explored the challenges and benefits of MTP. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\] highlights that teacher-forcing, a common training technique, can fail to learn an accurate next-token predictor in certain tasks, leading to compounded errors during autoregressive inference. This issue is further exacerbated by the reliance on single-token prediction, which limits the model's ability to plan for future tokens \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. In contrast, MTP explicitly encourages the model to consider multiple future tokens, potentially mitigating such issues \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The use of pause tokens to delay inference has also been investigated, with results showing that models trained with such techniques can achieve better performance on reasoning and question-answering tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02226)\]. This approach allows the model to process more computation before committing to an answer, which may be beneficial for tasks requiring careful planning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02226)\]. Similarly, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\] introduces Medusa, a framework that enables parallel decoding by predicting multiple subsequent tokens using multiple decoding heads, resulting in significant speedups without compromising generation quality \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\].

The effectiveness of MTP has also been evaluated in the context of transfer learning and few-shot learning. For example, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\] demonstrates that large language models like GPT-3 can achieve strong performance on a variety of tasks without fine-tuning, leveraging their ability to generalize from large-scale pre-training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. However, the few-shot learning capability of these models is still limited on certain datasets, highlighting the need for further improvements \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\].

Additionally, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\] presents a dataset of math word problems, GSM8K, to evaluate the ability of models to perform multi-step reasoning. The results show that even the largest models struggle with this task, prompting the development of verification strategies to improve performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14168)\]. This aligns with the findings of \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\], which shows that process supervision, where feedback is provided for each intermediate reasoning step, significantly outperforms outcome supervision in training models to solve complex problems \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\].

Additionally, transfer learning has been further explored through unified frameworks that convert diverse language problems into text-to-text formats, enabling systematic comparisons of pre-training objectives, architectures, and datasets \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of transfer learning techniques and their potential to improve both efficiency and performance in NLP.

We evaluate Medusa on models of various sizes and training procedures.
