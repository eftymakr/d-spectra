Taxation policy design has been extensively studied, with a focus on both theoretical models and empirical implementations. Traditional approaches, such as the U.S. federal income tax, aim to reduce economic inequality but often lack adaptability to dynamic economic conditions \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]. More sophisticated models, like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework, attempt to dynamically adjust tax rates based on economic indicators, yet they struggle to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more flexible and adaptive taxation models that can better reflect real-world complexities.

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has emerged as a promising approach to simulate and analyze economic systems, including taxation. ABMs allow for the representation of heterogeneous agents and their interactions, which is critical for modeling taxpayer behavior \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1101.1847)\]. For instance, studies have explored the use of ABMs to simulate tax evasion dynamics, incorporating enforcement mechanisms such as audits to curb evasion \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. These models demonstrate that even minimal audit rates can significantly reduce tax evasion, suggesting that policy interventions can have a measurable impact on taxpayer behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. Similarly, the Ising model has been used to analyze tax evasion dynamics, showing that punishment-based enforcement mechanisms can effectively control evasion \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/0801.2980)\].

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have also influenced the development of more sophisticated policy-making tools. LLMs are capable of generating reasoning traces and task-specific actions, which can be leveraged to improve decision-making in complex environments \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. Similarly, opinion dynamics models have been analyzed using Markov chains, providing a rigorous framework for understanding the evolution of opinions and behaviors in social systems \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.1716)\]. ### <a id='category-large-language-models-in-policy-making'></a>Large Language Models in Policy Making: 4 papers found.
The integration of large language models (LLMs) into policy-making processes has gained significant attention, particularly in enhancing reasoning and decision-making capabilities. Recent studies have demonstrated that fine-tuning LLMs on chain-of-thought (COT) reasoning data can significantly improve their reasoning abilities, although challenges such as assessment misalignment persist, where models may overrate subpar COTs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. To address this, Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) has been proposed, involving a three-step process that includes COT training, generating and categorizing COT responses, and calibrating scores with a constraint alignment loss to ensure positive responses outperform negative ones \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Additionally, the Experiential Learning (ExpeL) agent has been introduced to enable LLMs to learn from agent experiences without requiring parametric updates, allowing for more flexible and adaptive decision-making \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10144)\]. Furthermore, the ReAct approach has been developed to interleave reasoning and action generation, enabling LLMs to produce both reasoning traces and task-specific actions, thereby improving interpretability and performance in diverse tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in leveraging LLMs for policy-making, emphasizing the need for robust, adaptable, and interpretable models that can handle complex real-world scenarios.
