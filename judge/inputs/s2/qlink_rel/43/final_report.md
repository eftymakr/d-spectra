## Related Works

Large language models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to perform complex tasks with minimal examples or instructions, a capability often referred to as few-shot or zero-shot learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. Early work demonstrated that pre-training on large corpora followed by fine-tuning on specific tasks significantly improves performance on NLP benchmarks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. However, these models still require extensive task-specific datasets, unlike humans who can learn from just a few examples \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. Recent advances, such as GPT-3, have shown that scaling up LLMs can improve few-shot performance, sometimes reaching competitiveness with fine-tuning approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. GPT-3, with 175 billion parameters, was tested on various tasks, including translation, question-answering, and arithmetic, without any fine-tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. 

Instruction tuning has also been explored as a method to improve zero-shot learning abilities. FLAN, an instruction-tuned model, outperformed GPT-3 on 20 out of 25 tasks and even surpassed few-shot GPT-3 on several benchmarks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. This suggests that instruction tuning can significantly enhance a model's ability to generalize to new tasks without explicit fine-tuning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Similarly, WizardLM was developed by generating complex instructions using an initial set of instructions and fine-tuning LLaMA, leading to superior performance compared to human-created instructions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. 

The importance of scale in improving few-shot learning has also been highlighted. PaLM, a 540-billion parameter model, demonstrated continued benefits of scaling, achieving state-of-the-art results on hundreds of benchmarks and outperforming fine-tuned models on multi-step reasoning tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. This aligns with findings that larger models can better capture the underlying patterns needed for few-shot learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. 

Efficient fine-tuning methods, such as QLoRA, have also been introduced to reduce memory usage while preserving performance. This is particularly important for deploying LLMs in resource-constrained environments.

### <a id='category-model-generalization-capabilities'></a>Model Generalization Capabilities: 5 papers found.
The category "Model Generalization Capabilities" explores how large language models (LLMs) can perform new tasks with minimal or no fine-tuning, leveraging their pre-trained knowledge. Research has shown that scaling up LLMs significantly enhances their few-shot learning abilities, enabling them to achieve competitive performance on a wide range of NLP tasks, including translation, question-answering, and reasoning, without requiring task-specific fine-tuning datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. For example, GPT-3, with 175 billion parameters, demonstrates strong performance in few-shot settings, often rivaling prior state-of-the-art fine-tuning approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. Similarly, BLOOM, a 176B-parameter open-access model, achieves competitive results across various benchmarks, especially after multitask prompted fine-tuning \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05100)\]. Llama 3 further advances this capability by supporting multilinguality, coding, and reasoning, with its 405B parameter version showing comparable quality to leading models like GPT-4 \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783)\]. Additionally, studies have explored zero-shot reasoning, where models can perform complex tasks by simply adding prompts like "Let's think step by step," significantly improving performance on arithmetic and symbolic reasoning tasks without any hand-crafted examples \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)\]. These findings highlight the growing potential of LLMs to generalize across tasks through prompt-based strategies, suggesting that their underlying knowledge can be effectively harnessed with minimal additional input \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)\].

### <a id='category-instruction-tuning-approaches'></a>Instruction Tuning Approaches: 3 papers found.
Instruction Tuning Approaches have been increasingly explored to enhance the performance of large language models (LLMs) in various tasks. Researchers have demonstrated that instruction tuning, which involves fine-tuning models on a collection of tasks described via natural language instructions, significantly improves zero-shot learning abilities and task performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. For instance, FLAN, a 137B parameter model instruction-tuned on over 60 NLP tasks, outperforms GPT-3 on multiple benchmarks, showcasing the effectiveness of this approach \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Similarly, the WizardLM model was developed by evolving instructions using an LLM, leading to superior instruction data compared to human-created ones, and achieving performance comparable to ChatGPT on several skills \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. In the domain of mathematical reasoning, MAmmoTH models, trained on a curated dataset with hybrid chain-of-thought and program-of-thought rationales, outperform existing models, achieving notable accuracy on benchmark datasets \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. These studies highlight the importance of diverse instruction data and the potential of instruction tuning to enhance LLM capabilities across different domains.

### <a id='category-model-scaling-and-parameter-effects'></a>Model Scaling and Parameter Effects: 3 papers found.
The category "Model Scaling and Parameter Effects" explores how increasing the scale of large language models (LLMs) impacts their performance across various tasks. Research indicates that larger models, such as the 540-billion parameter PaLM, achieve state-of-the-art results in few-shot learning, outperforming finetuned models and even human performance on certain benchmarks like BIG-bench \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. Similarly, GPT-4, a multimodal model, demonstrates human-level performance on professional and academic benchmarks, such as passing a simulated bar exam \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\]. However, scaling does not uniformly improve all tasks; for instance, mathematical reasoning remains challenging, with even large models achieving relatively low accuracy on the MATH dataset \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03874)\]. These findings highlight that while scaling can enhance many text-based tasks, it may not be sufficient for complex reasoning problems without new algorithmic advancements. Additionally, the study of bias, toxicity, and data memorization becomes more critical as models grow in scale \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311)\]. The development of efficient training infrastructure and performance modeling tools, such as Lumos, is essential to guide the optimization of large-scale LLMs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\].

[Snippet]: «We present QLoRA, an efficient finetuning approach that reduces memory usage enough to finetune a 65B parameter model on a single 48GB GPU while preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance. QLoRA backpropagates gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantized pretrained language model into Low Rank Adapters~(LoRA). Our best model family, which we name Guanaco, outperforms all previous openly released models on the Vicuna benchmark, reaching 99.3% of the performance level of ChatGPT while only requiring 24 hours of finetuning on a single GPU. QLoRA introduces a number of innovations to save memory without sacrificing performance: (a) 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), a new data type that is information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights (b) double quantization to reduce the average memory footprint by quantizing the quantization constants, and (c) paged optimziers to manage memory spikes. We use QLoRA to finetune more than 1,000 models, providing a detailed analysis of instruction following and chatbot performance across 8 instruction datasets, multiple model types (LLaMA, T5), and model scales that would be infeasible to run with regular finetuning (e.g. 33B and 65B parameter models). Our results show that QLoRA finetuning on a small high-quality dataset leads to state-of-the-art results, even when using smaller models than the previous SoTA. We provide a detailed analysis of chatbot performance based on both human and GPT-4 evaluations showing that GPT-4 evaluations are a cheap and reasonable alternative to human evaluation. Furthermore, we find that current chatbot benchmarks are not trustworthy to accurately evaluate the performance levels of chatbots. A lemon-picked analysis demonstrates where Guanaco fails compared to ChatGPT. We release all of our models and code, including CUDA kernels for 4-bit training.»
