## Related Works

In-context learning (ICL) has gained significant attention as a paradigm where large language models (LLMs) perform tasks by conditioning on a few input-output pairs, known as demonstrations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12685)\]. Early studies highlighted the critical role of high-quality demonstrations in ICL, emphasizing that the performance of the model is highly dependent on the provided examples \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\]. However, recent research has shown that the quality of these demonstrations can be compromised by noisy labels, which can significantly degrade the model's performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19581)\]. 

The assumption that noisy samples yield higher perplexity than clean ones has been widely used to detect noisy annotations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17264)\]. However, this approach is not robust when the noise ratio is high and many demonstrations are flawed \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19581)\]. This limitation has led to the development of alternative methods that aim to improve the reliability of ICL under noisy conditions. For instance, the work by \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17264)\] proposed Local Perplexity Ranking (LPR), which replaces noisy candidates with their nearest neighbors in semantic space, thereby improving the performance of ICL on tasks with noisy annotations. This method is motivated by the analysis of perplexity deviation caused by noisy labels and decomposes perplexity into inherent and matching components \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17264)\].

Another line of research focuses on the role of demonstrations in ICL, showing that ground-truth labels are not always necessary for achieving good performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\]. Instead, the format, distribution, and structure of the demonstrations play a crucial role in determining the model's ability to generalize \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\]. This insight has led to the development of methods that aim to improve the effectiveness of ICL by focusing on the structure and content of the demonstrations rather than their correctness \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\].

Furthermore, the importance of controlling the verbosity of prompt templates and the size of the language model has been identified as a key factor in achieving more noise-resilient ICL \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12685)\]. This highlights the need for a more nuanced understanding of how different components of the ICL setup affect the final performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12685)\].

In addition to improving the quality of demonstrations, recent studies have explored methods to enhance the robustness of ICL by introducing new decoding strategies. Contrastive decoding, for example, has been shown to improve reasoning tasks by maximizing the weighted difference in likelihood between strong and weak models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09117)\]. This method has been applied to various tasks, including commonsense reasoning and math word problems, demonstrating its effectiveness in generating more accurate and coherent outputs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09117)\].

### <a id='category-contrastive-decoding-techniques'></a>Contrastive Decoding Techniques: 3 papers found.
Contrastive decoding techniques have emerged as a promising approach to improve the quality and reasoning capabilities of text generation from large language models. These methods leverage a contrastive objective that compares the likelihood of generated text under a large "expert" model and a smaller "amateur" model, ensuring outputs are both plausible and optimized for reasoning tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15097)\]. This approach is particularly effective in reducing issues such as repetition, incoherence, and failure to integrate contextual information from input prompts, which are common in traditional decoding strategies like greedy decoding or nucleus sampling \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12685)\]. By focusing on the difference in likelihood between models, contrastive decoding not only enhances the perceived quality of long-form text generation but also improves performance on commonsense reasoning benchmarks such as HellaSwag and GSM8K, outperforming models like LLaMA 2, GPT-3.5, and PaLM 2-L \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12685)\]. Additionally, contrastive decoding has been adapted to address challenges in open-domain question answering by incorporating adversarial irrelevant passages as negative samples, further strengthening context grounding during generation without requiring additional training \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02750)\]. These techniques demonstrate robustness across different model scales and domains, offering a general-purpose solution for generating high-quality, contextually accurate text \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15097)\].

### <a id='category-in-context-learning-(icl)-frameworks'></a>In-context Learning (ICL) Frameworks: 2 papers found.
In-context learning (ICL) has gained significant attention as a paradigm where language models leverage a few input-output pairs to make predictions on new tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04320)\]. This approach heavily relies on the quality of demonstrations, prompting research into demonstration retrieval mechanisms that can efficiently select relevant examples from training data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04320)\]. While prior work has focused on task-specific retrievers, these methods often struggle with transferability and scalability across diverse tasks, as well as incurring high computational costs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04320)\]. To address these limitations, the Unified Demonstration Retriever (UDR) was introduced as a single model capable of retrieving demonstrations for a wide range of tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04320)\]. UDR employs a multi-task list-wise ranking framework, incorporating iterative mining strategies to enhance the quality of retrieved demonstrations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04320)\]. Additionally, alternative approaches such as in-context vectors (ICV) have been proposed to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of ICL by encoding task-specific information into latent vectors, which can then be applied to new queries without requiring explicit demonstration inclusion \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06668)\]. These innovations highlight ongoing efforts to refine ICL frameworks, addressing challenges such as context window limitations, control over model behavior, and the integration of diverse task signals \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06668)\].

### <a id='category-noise-in-demonstrations-and-its-impact'></a>Noise in Demonstrations and Its Impact: 2 papers found.
In the context of in-context learning (ICL), recent studies have highlighted the critical role of demonstration quality in achieving robust performance, particularly in the presence of noisy labels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12685)\]. To address this issue, researchers have proposed methods such as Local Perplexity Ranking (LPR), which leverages semantic space analysis to replace noisy candidates with more reliable, clean examples \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.06668)\]. This approach aims to mitigate the impact of noisy labels by focusing on perplexity deviations and decomposing them into inherent and matching components \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837)\]. These efforts underscore the growing recognition of the challenges posed by noisy demonstrations and the need for more robust strategies to enhance ICL performance in real-world scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19581)\].
