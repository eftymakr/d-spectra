## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and exhibit behaviors such as refusal within their activation space. These behaviors are often the result of safety alignment techniques like reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\], which aim to make models act in a helpful and harmless manner. However, identifying and modifying such behaviors remains a significant challenge, as existing methods often rely on predefined refusal templates detectable in output tokens or require manual analysis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07858)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more automated and robust methods for steering LLM behavior.

Recent work has explored various approaches to steer LLMs, including fine-tuning, prompt engineering, and activation engineering. For instance, LoRA fine-tuning has been shown to effectively undo safety training in models like Llama 2-Chat \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20624)\]. Similarly, methods such as activation addition and contrastive activation addition (CAA) have been proposed to modify model behavior during inference by manipulating activations \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02949)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02151)\]. These techniques demonstrate the potential for inference-time steering, which can be more efficient and flexible than traditional training-based methods.

However, these approaches often require assumptions about the model's behavior, such as the presence of specific refusal tokens or the use of directional ablation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.04359)\]. Additionally, some methods, like red teaming, have been used to evaluate the robustness of safety alignment by attempting to subvert models into generating harmful content \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717)\]. These studies reveal that even well-aligned models can be vulnerable to adversarial attacks, especially when they are not explicitly trained to resist such inputs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09003)\].

By leveraging cosine similarity, COSMIC avoids the need for predefined refusal templates or assumptions about the model's behavior, making it more robust and generalizable.

The effectiveness of COSMIC is supported by recent studies on activation engineering, which have shown that modifying activations during inference can significantly alter model behavior while preserving other capabilities \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01174)\]. Furthermore, the use of spectral editing of activations has been demonstrated to improve truthfulness and reduce bias in LLMs \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03827)\].
