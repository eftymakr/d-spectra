## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images, leveraging the ability to model complex geometry and appearance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\]. The core idea involves optimizing a 3D scene representation by minimizing the photometric discrepancy between input views and differentiable renderings \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12231)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often limited by the number of input views, especially in dynamic or complex scenes where certain angles are never captured \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\]. To address this limitation, recent works have explored data augmentation techniques, including video frame interpolation (VFI), to generate additional views and improve the robustness of neural rendering models \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\].

Video frame interpolation has evolved from classical motion compensation-based methods to deep learning-based approaches, including kernel-, flow-, hybrid-, phase-, GAN-, Transformer-, Mamba-, and diffusion model-based approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\]. These methods aim to synthesize intermediate frames between existing ones while maintaining spatial and temporal coherence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01061)\]. Several approaches have focused on improving the quality of interpolation in the presence of large object motion, occlusion, and lighting variation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. For instance, the Depth-Aware Video Frame Interpolation method explicitly detects occlusion by leveraging depth information to synthesize intermediate flows that preferentially sample closer objects \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.00830)\]. Similarly, the Many-to-Many (M2M) splatting framework directly forward warps pixels to the desired time step and fuses overlapping pixels, achieving fast multi-frame interpolation with robustness to artifacts \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03513)\].

Real-time video frame interpolation has also seen significant progress, with methods like RIFE achieving state-of-the-art performance while maintaining high efficiency \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\]. RIFE uses a neural network named IFNet to estimate intermediate flows end-to-end, and it supports arbitrary-timestep frame interpolation with temporal encoding \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\]. This enables efficient and high-quality video frame interpolation for real-time applications \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06294)\].

Recent advances in neural rendering have also explored the integration of video frame interpolation as a data augmentation strategy. For example, the paper proposes using video frame interpolation to generate additional views for training neural rendering models, thereby improving the reconstruction performance on both static and dynamic scenes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\]. This approach is inspired by the fact that video frame interpolation can implicitly model camera movement and real-world 3D geometry \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02048)\].
