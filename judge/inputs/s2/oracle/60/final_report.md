## Related Works

Calibration of machine learning models has been extensively studied, particularly in the context of classification and ranking tasks. Traditional calibration methods, such as binning and scaling, have been widely used to post-process model outputs to obtain calibrated probabilities \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. However, these methods often lack flexibility and expressiveness, especially in complex scenarios where the relationship between predicted values and true probabilities is non-linear \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. For instance, binning methods are distribution-free but may not capture fine-grained relationships due to their non-individual nature \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. Similarly, scaling methods, while simple, often fail to account for the variability in model outputs across different feature fields \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10713)\].

Recent work has focused on improving the flexibility of calibration methods by introducing more expressive models. For example, MBCT (Multiple Boosting Calibration Trees) proposes a feature-aware binning framework that leverages tree structures to achieve individual calibration, offering better performance in terms of both calibration error and order accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in applications where order sensitivity is crucial, such as in advertising and recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\].

In the context of ranking models, several studies have explored the integration of calibration with ranking objectives. For example, JRC (Joint Optimization of Ranking and Calibration) introduces a method that jointly optimizes ranking and calibration abilities by constraining predicted probabilities to be functions of logit differences, thereby improving both ranking and calibration performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.06164)\]. Similarly, the RCR (Regression Compatible Listwise Objectives) approach combines regression and ranking objectives to achieve a better trade-off, demonstrating improved performance in both aspects \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01494)\]. These methods highlight the importance of aligning calibration with ranking objectives to achieve more accurate and reliable predictions.

Field-aware calibration has also emerged as a critical area of research, especially in advertising and recommendation systems. Neural Calibration, for instance, introduces a field-level calibration error metric and proposes a simple yet effective post-hoc calibration method that leverages field-aware information during training \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10713)\]. This approach has been shown to significantly improve calibration performance across various metrics, including the Brier score and AUC \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10713)\]. ### <a id='category-field-aware-calibration-techniques'></a>Field-aware Calibration Techniques: 3 papers found.
Field-aware calibration techniques have gained significant attention in addressing the miscalibration issue in machine learning models, particularly in domains like online advertising where accurate probabilistic predictions are crucial for ranking and bidding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. Miscalibration often arises due to mismatches between predicted probabilities and true likelihoods, exacerbated by shifts in data distributions and model biases \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. Traditional calibration methods, which rely on fixed transformation functions such as piece-wise linear functions, lack the flexibility needed to handle complex calibration scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04348)\]. Recent approaches, such as Neural Calibration, leverage field-aware information during the validation phase to improve calibration accuracy, demonstrating improvements in metrics like negative log-likelihood and AUC \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07998)\]. Similarly, AdaCalib introduces a doubly-adaptive approach that incorporates field-specific mechanisms to ensure posterior probabilities are appropriate for specific field values, achieving significant calibration performance improvements \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07428)\]. These methods highlight the importance of adapting calibration strategies to the specific characteristics of the input data, enabling more accurate and reliable predictions in real-world applications.

[Snippet]: «In machine learning systems, privileged features refer to the features that
are available during offline training but inaccessible for online serving.
Previous studies have recognized the importance of privileged features and
explored ways to tackle online-offline discrepancies. A typical practice is
privileged features distillation (PFD): train a teacher model using all
features (including privileged ones) and then distill the knowledge from the
teacher model using a student model (excluding the privileged features), which
is then employed for online serving. In practice, the pointwise cross-entropy
loss is often adopted for PFD. However, this loss is insufficient to distill
the ranking ability for CTR prediction. First, it does not consider the
non-i.i.d. characteristic of the data distribution, i.e., other items on the
same page significantly impact the click probability of the candidate item.
Second, it fails to consider the relative item order ranked by the teacher
model's predictions, which is essential to distill the ranking ability. To
address these issues, we first extend the pointwise-based PFD to the
listwise-based PFD. We then define the calibration-compatible property of
distillation loss and show that commonly used listwise losses do not satisfy
this property when employed as distillation loss, thus compromising the model's
calibration ability, which is another important measure for CTR prediction. To
tackle this dilemma, we propose Calibration-compatible LIstwise Distillation
(CLID), which employs carefully-designed listwise distillation loss to achieve
better ranking ability than the pointwise-based PFD while preserving the
model's calibration ability. We theoretically prove it is
calibration-compatible. Extensive experiments on public datasets and a
production dataset collected from the display advertising system of Alibaba
further demonstrate the effectiveness of CLID.»
