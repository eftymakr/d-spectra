## Related Works

Economic inequality remains a critical challenge, influencing disparities in education, healthcare, and social stability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. Traditional tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, have been effective in reducing inequality but lack adaptability to changing economic conditions and heterogeneous taxpayer behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. The Saez Optimal Taxation framework has attempted to address these limitations by introducing dynamic tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. However, it fails to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior, which are essential for designing more equitable and efficient tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\].

Several studies have explored alternative approaches to taxation. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) has been examined for its impact on income distribution, showing potential to reduce inequality while maintaining productivity \[[2](http://www.nber.org/papers/w21340)\]. Similarly, the AI Economist introduces a novel approach using deep reinforcement learning to design tax policies that balance equality and productivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. This work demonstrates that AI-driven tax policies can improve the equality-productivity trade-off by 16% over baseline policies, including the Saez framework \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. The AI Economist also shows that AI-driven tax policies are qualitatively different, with higher top tax rates and greater net subsidies for low-income individuals \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\].

Optimal taxation theory has long been a focus of economic research, with studies exploring the use of elasticities to derive optimal income tax rates \[[7](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937X.00166)\]. These studies highlight the importance of understanding how income and labor supply respond to tax changes, which is crucial for designing effective tax policies \[[7](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-937X.00166)\]. Similarly, research on the optimal taxation of top labor incomes emphasizes the role of three key elasticities in determining tax rates \[[8](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.6.1.230)\]. These findings align with the need for more adaptive and data-driven approaches to taxation.

Agent-based modeling has also been used to simulate economic behavior and policy outcomes. The AI Economist employs an economic simulation environment that features competitive pressures and market dynamics, validating its approach by showing that baseline tax systems align with economic theory \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. This work also demonstrates that AI-driven tax policies are robust against emergent tax-gaming strategies learned by AI agents \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. Furthermore, the AI Economist shows that these policies are effective when tested with human participants, indicating their practical relevance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\].

In addition, the integration of large language models (LLMs) with agent-based modeling (ABM) represents a novel approach to designing adaptive tax policies. The TaxAgent framework, introduced in this study, combines LLMs with ABM to iteratively optimize tax rates, balancing equity and productivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13332)\]. This approach builds on prior work in machine learning and economics
