## Related Works

Transfer learning has been extensively studied, particularly in domains where data scarcity is a challenge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. In the context of medical imaging, transfer learning is used to improve generalization by reusing pre-trained models on related tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\]. This is especially relevant in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), where the variability in image acquisition and the limited availability of annotated data pose significant challenges \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\]. Transfer learning is also beneficial for reusing models trained on related tasks to improve performance on the target task \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530)\].

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have emerged as a powerful tool for data augmentation and synthetic data generation \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)\]. GANs consist of a generator and a discriminator that are trained in an adversarial manner, allowing the generator to produce realistic synthetic data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)\]. This framework has been applied in various domains, including medical imaging, to generate synthetic images that can be used for training machine learning models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661)\]. However, GANs can suffer from issues such as mode collapse, where the generator fails to produce diverse outputs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02340)\].

Conditional GANs extend the GAN framework by incorporating additional information, such as labels, to guide the generation process \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784)\]. This allows for the generation of images conditioned on specific attributes, making them useful for tasks such as image tagging and segmentation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784)\]. However, conditional GANs can be sensitive to noisy labels, which can lead to the generation of incorrect or low-quality images \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03205)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed robust conditional GAN architectures that can handle noisy labels by incorporating mechanisms such as label corruption during training \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03205)\].

In the context of medical imaging, synthetic data generation has been used to address class imbalance, a common issue in tasks such as lesion detection and segmentation \[[12](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0267976)\]. For example, the use of synthetic data generated by GANs has been shown to improve classifier performance by increasing the representation of underrepresented classes \[[12](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0267976)\]. This is particularly relevant in the case of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), which are rare and difficult to detect due to their low prevalence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. By generating synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps of PRLs, researchers can increase the minority class representation and improve detection accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\].

Additionally, GAN inversion techniques have been used to bridge the gap between real and synthetic images, enabling the use of pretrained GAN models for real-world image editing and restoration tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2101.05278)\]. ### <a id='category-data-augmentation-in-neuroimaging'></a>Data Augmentation in Neuroimaging: 2 papers found.
In the field of data augmentation for neuroimaging, several approaches have been proposed to address the challenge of imbalanced data, particularly in the context of generating synthetic lesions for improved machine learning performance. One notable method involves the use of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to create synthetic images, although these methods can suffer from issues such as mode collapse \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02340)\]. Another approach focuses on fully convolutional neural networks (FCNs) trained end-to-end to generate synthetic multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions on MRI images, using lesion masks as input and producing realistic synthetic lesions that can be used for data augmentation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. These synthetic lesions have been shown to improve the performance of supervised learning algorithms, particularly in scenarios with limited training data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733)\]. Additionally, generative networks have been utilized to produce synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps, which can help in distinguishing between different types of lesions, such as paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), and improve classifier performance by increasing the representation of minority classes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\]. The use of such synthetic data not only helps in addressing class imbalance but also enables the training of models on ambiguous cases, leading to better generalization and clinically interpretable results \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694)\].

[Snippet]: «We study the problem of learning conditional generators from noisy labeled
samples, where the labels are corrupted by random noise. A standard training of
conditional GANs will not only produce samples with wrong labels, but also
generate poor quality samples. We consider two scenarios, depending on whether
the noise model is known or not. When the distribution of the noise is known,
we introduce a novel architecture which we call Robust Conditional GAN (RCGAN).
The main idea is to corrupt the label of the generated sample before feeding to
the adversarial discriminator, forcing the generator to produce samples with
clean labels. This approach of passing through a matching noisy channel is
justified by corresponding multiplicative approximation bounds between the loss
of the RCGAN and the distance between the clean real distribution and the
generator distribution. This shows that the proposed approach is robust, when
used with a carefully chosen discriminator architecture, known as projection
discriminator. When the distribution of the noise is not known, we provide an
extension of our architecture, which we call RCGAN-U, that learns the noise
model simultaneously while training the generator. We show experimentally on
MNIST and CIFAR-10 datasets that both the approaches consistently improve upon
baseline approaches, and RCGAN-U closely matches the performance of RCGAN.»
