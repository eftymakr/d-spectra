## Related Works

Event cameras have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional frame-based cameras due to their high temporal resolution and ability to capture dynamic scenes efficiently \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. However, the asynchronous and sparse nature of event data presents significant challenges in processing and analysis. Prior work has explored various strategies to manage these challenges, including event downsampling, which aims to reduce the computational load while preserving essential information \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\].

Most existing approaches to event downsampling rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit their adaptability to different applications and scenarios \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c04c7d4abc5f0774b2dfd4e44ed183630da53e38)\]. These methods often fail to account for the structural importance of events, leading to suboptimal performance in tasks such as object classification and detection \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\]. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\] introduces a method that uses a fixed threshold to filter events, but this approach lacks the flexibility to adapt to varying scene dynamics.

In contrast, our proposed probabilistic framework, POLED, models event importance through an event-importance probability density function (ePDF), which can be arbitrarily defined and adapted to different applications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06283)\]. This approach enables online estimation of event importance from raw event streams, allowing for scene-specific adaptation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08336)\]. The ePDF is designed to prioritize structurally important events, ensuring that critical information is retained even under event-budget constraints \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\].

Additionally, we introduce zero-shot event downsampling, where downsampled events must remain usable for models trained on the original event stream without task-specific adaptation \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. This is a significant advancement over previous methods, which often require retraining or fine-tuning for new tasks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\]. Our approach is evaluated across four datasets and tasks—object classification, image interpolation, surface normal estimation, and object detection—demonstrating that intelligent sampling is crucial for maintaining performance under event-budget constraints \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07165)\].

The importance of preserving structural information in event data has also been highlighted in other works. For example, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09148)\] proposes a method that uses a contour-preserving ePDF to prioritize structurally important events, which aligns with our approach. Similarly, \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.00386)\] introduces a framework for motion estimation that leverages the temporal properties of event data, emphasizing the need for adaptive and context-aware processing.

In summary, while existing methods have made strides in managing event data, they often lack the flexibility and adaptability required for real-world applications. Our approach addresses these limitations by introducing a probabilistic framework that enables online, scene-specific adaptation and zero-shot event downsampling \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08298)\].

### <a id='category-comparative-analysis-with-existing-approaches'></a>Comparative Analysis with Existing Approaches: 17 papers found.
Event cameras, which capture asynchronous per-pixel brightness changes, offer significant advantages over traditional frame-based cameras in high-speed and high-dynamic-range scenarios \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. However, the sparse and asynchronous nature of event data poses challenges for existing vision algorithms, which are typically designed for dense, synchronous image inputs \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c04c7d4abc5f0774b2dfd4e44ed183630da53e38)\]. To address this, recent work has focused on converting event streams into grid-based representations for compatibility with standard vision pipelines, such as CNNs, while preserving the inherent sparsity and temporal dynamics of events \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\]. Several approaches have introduced end-to-end learning frameworks that jointly optimize event representation and task-specific networks, leading to improvements in tasks like optical flow estimation and object detection \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. For instance, one study demonstrated a 12% improvement in optical flow estimation by learning event representations end-to-end \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c04c7d4abc5f0774b2dfd4e44ed183630da53e38)\]. Additionally, efforts have been made to directly reconstruct intensity images from event streams without relying on hand-crafted priors, using recurrent networks trained on simulated data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06283)\]. These methods enable the use of off-the-shelf vision algorithms on event data, achieving performance comparable to frame-based systems \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08336)\]. Furthermore, some works have explored the fusion of event and frame data to leverage the complementary strengths of both modalities, enhancing performance in challenging conditions such as high-speed motion or low-light environments \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10552)\]. The development of self-supervised learning techniques has also been proposed to train models without ground-truth data, using the intrinsic properties of event cameras \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. Overall, these studies highlight the growing interest in adapting traditional vision methods to event-based data, with a focus on improving efficiency, accuracy, and adaptability to real-world scenarios \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\].

### <a id='category-event-camera-overview-and-advantages'></a>Event Camera Overview and Advantages: 6 papers found.
Event cameras, which capture asynchronous per-pixel brightness changes, offer significant advantages over traditional frame-based cameras, including high temporal resolution, high dynamic range, and low latency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\]. These sensors have been increasingly explored for applications in robotics and computer vision, particularly in scenarios requiring high-speed and high-dynamic-range performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.08156)\]. However, the unconventional output of event cameras—streams of asynchronous events—requires novel processing algorithms to fully exploit their capabilities \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c04c7d4abc5f0774b2dfd4e44ed183630da53e38)\]. Recent advancements have led to hybrid sensors like the DAVIS, which combine global-shutter intensity images with event-based streams, enabling the benefits of both traditional and event-based sensors \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\]. Additionally, color event cameras, such as the Color-DAVIS346, have been developed, expanding the range of applications by providing color information alongside event data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. Despite these advances, challenges remain in processing event streams efficiently, particularly in terms of computational demands and adaptability to different tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.06283)\].
