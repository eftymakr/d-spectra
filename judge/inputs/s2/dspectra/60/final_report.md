## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been extensively studied, with a primary focus on ensuring that predicted probabilities align with actual accuracy. Early work on calibration, such as \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01685)\], introduced the concept of expected calibration error (ECE) as a metric to evaluate the consistency between predicted confidence and actual correctness. However, ECE has been criticized for its sensitivity to binning choices and its inability to capture nuanced calibration behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01685)\]. 

Recent studies have explored various calibration techniques, including temperature scaling \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\], which has been shown to be effective in improving calibration without compromising accuracy. This approach, a single-parameter variant of Platt scaling, has become a popular post-hoc calibration method. However, temperature scaling is limited in its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships between confidence and accuracy \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. 

To address these limitations, several works have proposed more flexible calibration methods. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\] introduced focal loss, which not only improves accuracy but also leads to better-calibrated models when combined with temperature scaling. This approach has been shown to outperform traditional methods in various tasks, including image and NLP datasets. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\] proposed a method based on deep ensembles, which provides well-calibrated uncertainty estimates by leveraging multiple models. This approach is particularly effective in scenarios where predictive uncertainty is critical, such as in safety-critical applications.

Another line of research has focused on improving calibration through the use of more sophisticated calibration functions. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07923)\] introduced the AvUC loss, which leverages the relationship between accuracy and uncertainty to guide the learning process. This method has been shown to improve calibration while maintaining or even enhancing model accuracy. Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00263)\] proposed a calibration method based on calibrated regression, which ensures that any regression algorithm produces well-calibrated uncertainty estimates given sufficient data. This approach has been successfully applied to Bayesian and probabilistic models, including recurrent neural networks.

The importance of calibration in ranking models has also been highlighted in several works. For example, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03167)\] discussed the issue of scale calibration in deep ranking models, noting that many advanced ranking functions are not scale calibrated, which limits their practical deployment. The paper proposed a method to calibrate ranking models during training, leading to improved performance in both ranking quality and score scale calibration. This approach is particularly relevant to applications such as ads ranking, where calibrated scores are essential for downstream tasks like auction mechanisms.

In addition, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07329)\] introduced mixup training, which has been shown to improve calibration by encouraging the model to learn more robust and generalized representations.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Learning-to-Rank (LTR) systems are ubiquitous in web applications nowadays. The existing literature mainly focuses on improving ranking performance by trying to generate the optimal order of candidate items. However, virtually all advanced ranking functions are not scale calibrated. For example, rankers have the freedom to add a constant to all item scores without changing their relative order. This property has resulted in several limitations in deploying advanced ranking methods in practice. On the one hand, it limits the use of effective ranking functions in important applications. For example, in ads ranking, predicted Click-Through Rate (pCTR) is used for ranking and is required to be calibrated for the downstream ads auction. This is a major reason that existing ads ranking methods use scale calibrated pointwise loss functions that may sacrifice ranking performance. On the other hand, popular ranking losses are translation-invariant. We rigorously show that, both theoretically and empirically, this property leads to training instability that may cause severe practical issues. In this paper, we study how to perform scale calibration of deep ranking models to address the above concerns. We design three different formulations to calibrate ranking models through calibrated ranking losses. Unlike existing post-processing methods, our calibration is performed during training, which can resolve the training instability issue without any additional processing. We conduct experiments on the standard LTR benchmark datasets and one of the largest sponsored search ads dataset from Google. Our results show that our proposed calibrated ranking losses can achieve nearly optimal results in terms of both ranking quality and score scale calibration.»
