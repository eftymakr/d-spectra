## Related Works

Neural retrieval methods using transformer-based pre-trained language models have significantly advanced multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. However, their effectiveness for low-resource, morphologically rich languages such as Amharic remains underexplored due to data scarcity and suboptimal tokenization \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. This gap is addressed by introducing Amharic-specific dense retrieval models based on pre-trained Amharic BERT and RoBERTa backbones \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. These models demonstrate notable improvements over existing multilingual baselines, such as Arctic Embed 2.0 \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\].

The RoBERTa-Base-Amharic-Embed model (110M parameters) achieves a 17.6% relative improvement in MRR@10 and a 9.86% gain in Recall@10 over the strongest multilingual baseline \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. This performance is attributed to the fine-tuning of pre-trained models on Amharic-specific data, which better captures the linguistic nuances of the language \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. More compact variants, such as RoBERTa-Medium-Amharic-Embed (42M), remain competitive while being over 13x smaller, highlighting the trade-off between model size and performance \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\].

Additionally, a ColBERT-based late interaction retrieval model is trained, achieving the highest MRR@10 score (0.843) among all evaluated models \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18570)\]. This model leverages late interaction to model fine-grained similarity between queries and documents, which is particularly beneficial for languages with complex morphological structures \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. The effectiveness of late interaction models is further supported by their ability to pre-compute document representations offline, significantly speeding up query processing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\].

### <a id='category-language-specific-adaptation-in-multilingual-models'></a>Language-Specific Adaptation in Multilingual Models: 13 papers found.
Language-specific adaptation in multilingual models has been a focal point of recent research, particularly in addressing the challenges of low-resource languages and improving cross-lingual transfer performance \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15562)\]. Models like multilingual BERT (mBERT) have demonstrated state-of-the-art results in cross-lingual tasks, yet they face significant performance gaps for under-resourced languages due to limited pretraining data and script differences \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15562)\]. To mitigate these issues, researchers have proposed data-efficient adaptation techniques that leverage existing latent knowledge in pretrained models, such as matrix factorization and lexically overlapping tokens \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15562)\]. These methods enable quick and effective adaptation to low-resource languages and unseen scripts, significantly improving performance for such cases \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15562)\]. Furthermore, the importance of language-specific adaptation is underscored by studies showing that fine-tuning models on language-specific data can yield better results than multilingual approaches alone \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. For instance, extending mBERT to new languages through effective adaptation strategies has led to notable improvements in tasks like Named Entity Recognition (NER) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385)\]. Additionally, the use of contextual language models such as BERT has shown superior performance in information retrieval tasks compared to traditional models, as they better capture language structures and query-document relevance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. These findings highlight the critical role of language-specific adaptation in enhancing model performance across diverse linguistic settings \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15562)\].

In the context of sparse versus dense retrieval baselines, recent research has highlighted the trade-offs between classical term-frequency methods and modern neural approaches. Traditional sparse methods like BM25 \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04648)\] and TF-IDF \[[30](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/164125a65d42a791d2c1e108559344caef96d08b)\] remain competitive, particularly in low-resource settings where training data is scarce, as they do not require large datasets to function effectively \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. However, dense retrievers, which leverage neural networks to learn semantic representations, have shown significant improvements in various retrieval tasks when sufficient training data is available \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. These models often outperform sparse methods in settings with abundant data, but their performance can degrade when transferred to new, unseen domains \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. Contrastive learning has emerged as a promising approach to train unsupervised dense retrievers, enabling strong performance even without labeled data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]. Additionally, models like MatchPyramid \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04648)\] have demonstrated effectiveness in ad-hoc retrieval by using convolutional neural networks to capture interactions between queries and documents, although they still lag behind traditional models in certain scenarios. Meanwhile, dense retrievers have also been successfully applied in open-domain question answering, where they outperform sparse methods by learning embeddings from limited question-passage pairs \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. These findings underscore the ongoing debate between sparse and dense retrieval approaches, with each having distinct advantages depending on the availability of data and the specific retrieval task at hand \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09118)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04648)\]\[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]\[[30](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/164125a65d42a791d2c1e108559344caef96d08b)\].
