## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to detect objects from arbitrary categories at inference time without requiring retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. Early approaches focused on zero-shot learning, where models were trained on a fixed set of categories and then extended to detect novel objects using semantic embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. However, these methods often struggled with localization accuracy and generalization to unseen classes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. 

Several works have explored weakly supervised and pseudo-labeling strategies to reduce the dependency on extensive annotated data. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\] proposed a method to generate pseudo bounding-box labels from image-caption pairs, enabling training on a broader set of object categories. This approach significantly improved performance on benchmarks like COCO and LVIS \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06160)\] introduced a framework that leverages large-scale image-text pairs to scale detection models, achieving improvements in both detection and segmentation tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06165)\]. 

The use of vision-language pre-trained models, such as CLIP, has been pivotal in advancing OVD. These models provide rich semantic representations that can be used to align visual and textual features \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.12130)\]. However, challenges remain in aligning object-level features with text descriptions, especially for novel categories \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13076)\]. To address this, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09683)\] proposed a method that uses pseudo-labeling and self-training to expand the vocabulary of detection models, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00788)\]. 

Another line of research focuses on improving the alignment between visual and textual representations. For example, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16513)\] introduced a framework that learns object-language alignments directly from image-text pairs, enabling the detection of novel objects without requiring additional annotations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14940)\]. This approach was further refined in \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\], where a multi-modal classifier was developed to combine both language descriptions and image exemplars, leading to better performance than single-modal approaches \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. 

The importance of weak supervision in OVD has also been highlighted in several studies. \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11134)\] proposed a method that uses image captions to generate pseudo-labels, enabling the detection of novel objects with minimal manual effort \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16312)\]. ### <a id='category-open-vocabulary-object-detection-methods'></a>Open-vocabulary object detection methods: 18 papers found.
Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has emerged as a critical research direction, enabling detectors to recognize objects from novel categories beyond the training vocabulary, often guided by natural language or exemplar images \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. Recent methods leverage pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP to align semantic embeddings with visual features, allowing for zero-shot or one-shot detection \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05493)\]. However, challenges persist, including localization accuracy for novel objects, confidence bias toward base categories, and the need for efficient training strategies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. To address these, various approaches have been proposed. For instance, some methods focus on improving proposal-level vision-language alignment, such as through regional prompt learning or object-centric alignment of language embeddings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14843)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12914)\]. Others introduce novel training frameworks, such as self-training with pseudo-labels or conditional network designs, to enhance generalization and detection performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06160)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06165)\]. Additionally, methods like DETPro and MEDet aim to learn continuous prompt representations or perform proposal mining and prediction equalization to improve accuracy on novel categories \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.12130)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13076)\]. These approaches highlight the ongoing efforts to balance scalability, accuracy, and efficiency in open-vocabulary detection, with many achieving significant improvements on benchmarks like COCO and LVIS \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09683)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.00788)\].

### <a id='category-weakly-supervised-and-pseudo-labeling-strategies'></a>Weakly supervised and pseudo-labeling strategies: 4 papers found.
The category "Weakly supervised and pseudo-labeling strategies" focuses on methods that leverage large-scale unlabeled data and weak supervision signals to enhance object detection models, particularly in open-vocabulary settings. These approaches aim to overcome the limitations of traditional fully-supervised methods, which rely on expensive and limited annotated data. For instance, recent work has proposed generating pseudo bounding-box annotations from image-caption pairs using pre-trained vision-language models, enabling the training of detectors on a broader set of object categories \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. Similarly, self-training strategies have been introduced to scale detection data by using existing detectors to generate pseudo-box annotations on image-text pairs, addressing challenges such as label space selection and training efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09683)\]. These methods demonstrate that pseudo-labeling can significantly improve performance, as evidenced by improvements in AP scores on benchmarks like COCO, PASCAL VOC, and LVIS \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. Additionally, the use of vision and language models to categorize regions in unlabeled images has shown promise in generating pseudo labels for both open-vocabulary detection and semi-supervised settings \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. Overall, these strategies highlight the potential of weakly supervised learning to expand the capabilities of object detection models, enabling them to generalize to unseen categories without requiring extensive manual annotations \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09220)\].

### <a id='category-vision-language-pre-trained-models'></a>Vision-language pre-trained models: 3 papers found.

To address this, knowledge distillation from pre-trained vision-language models is employed to generate semantically rich captions from multi-view 3D data, enabling better alignment between 3D scenes and text. Another notable approach, VisualBERT \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03557)\], utilizes Transformer layers to align text and image regions through self-attention, further demonstrating the effectiveness of pre-training on image caption data for vision-and-language tasks. These methods collectively highlight the growing trend of using pre-trained models to improve cross-modal understanding, with a focus on both alignment mechanisms and the use of large-scale data for robust semantic representation.
