## Related Works

The integration of large language models (LLMs) with agent-based modeling (ABM) to design adaptive tax policies represents a novel approach to addressing economic inequality. Traditional tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, aim to reduce inequality but lack adaptability to dynamic economic conditions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\]. Similarly, models like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework provide dynamic adjustments but fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more sophisticated models that can simulate diverse taxpayer behaviors and iteratively optimize tax policies \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\].

Recent advances in LLMs have demonstrated their potential to enhance decision-making processes in complex environments. For instance, the ReAct framework synergizes reasoning and acting by generating both reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner, improving human interpretability and trustworthiness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. This approach allows LLMs to interface with external sources, such as knowledge bases or environments, to gather additional information, which is crucial for accurate policy modeling \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. Similarly, the Toolformer model enables LLMs to teach themselves to use external tools via simple APIs, achieving improved zero-shot performance across various tasks without sacrificing core language modeling abilities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. These developments underscore the potential of LLMs to interact with and adapt to external environments, a key requirement for effective tax policy design.

The concept of emergent abilities in LLMs further supports the idea that scaling up language models can lead to new qualitative capabilities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682)\]. This aligns with the notion that LLMs can simulate complex behaviors, such as those of heterogeneous taxpayers, when trained on diverse and extensive datasets. Furthermore, the ExpeL agent demonstrates that LLMs can learn from agent experiences without requiring parametric updates, which is particularly relevant for maintaining and updating tax software in response to evolving regulations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10144)\]. This capability is essential for ensuring that tax policies remain relevant and effective over time.

In addition, the issue of evaluation bias in LLMs has been addressed through calibration frameworks that ensure fair and accurate assessment of model outputs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. This is particularly important in the context of tax policy, where the accuracy of model evaluations can directly impact the effectiveness of policy recommendations. The use of multiple evidence calibration, balanced position calibration, and human-in-the-loop calibration strategies helps mitigate such biases, ensuring that model outputs are aligned with human judgments \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\].

Finally, the importance of incorporating negative examples during fine-tuning has been highlighted in recent studies, demonstrating that unsuccessful trajectories can provide valuable insights for improving model performance

### <a id='category-llm-calibration-and-evaluation-bias-mitigation'></a>LLM Calibration and Evaluation Bias Mitigation: 2 papers found.
The category "LLM Calibration and Evaluation Bias Mitigation" focuses on addressing biases and calibration issues in the evaluation of large language models (LLMs). Recent studies have identified systematic biases in using LLMs as evaluators, where the quality ranking of responses can be manipulated by altering the order of appearance in the context, leading to skewed evaluation results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed calibration frameworks that include strategies such as Multiple Evidence Calibration, which requires evaluators to generate multiple pieces of evidence before assigning scores, and Balanced Position Calibration, which aggregates results across different orders to determine a more accurate final score \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. Additionally, Human-in-the-Loop Calibration introduces measures to detect difficult examples and seek human assistance when necessary \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. These approaches aim to improve the fairness and reliability of LLM evaluations. Furthermore, the concept of Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) has been introduced to address the Assessment Misalignment problem, where fine-tuned LLMs may assign higher scores to subpar chain-of-thought (COT) responses \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. AFT involves three steps: fine-tuning with COT data, generating multiple COT responses, and calibrating scores using a constraint alignment loss to ensure positive responses outperform negative ones while keeping negative scores within a reasonable range \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. These methods highlight the importance of calibration and bias mitigation in ensuring the accuracy and fairness of LLM evaluations.

### <a id='category-emergent-capabilities-in-large-language-models'></a>Emergent Capabilities in Large Language Models: 3 papers found.
The concept of emergent abilities in large language models (LLMs) refers to capabilities that arise in larger models but are absent in smaller ones, indicating that performance cannot be reliably predicted by scaling up smaller models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. These emergent abilities suggest that as models grow in scale, they may develop new functionalities that were not previously observed, such as complex reasoning or multi-step problem-solving \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682)\]. Research has shown that while some tasks improve predictably with scale, others exhibit "breakthrough" behavior at a critical point, where performance suddenly improves \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682)\]. This phenomenon underscores the need for benchmarks like the Beyond the Imitation Game (BIG-bench), which evaluates LLMs on tasks believed to be beyond their current capabilities, such as linguistics, math, and common-sense reasoning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682)\]. Additionally, studies highlight that while model performance generally improves with scale, it remains limited compared to human performance, especially in tasks requiring nuanced understanding or social context \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07682)\]. These findings emphasize the importance of understanding the limitations and potential of LLMs as they continue to evolve \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\].

### <a id='category-tool-usage-and-external-interaction-in-llms'></a>Tool Usage and External Interaction in LLMs: 2 papers found.
The category "Tool Usage and External Interaction in LLMs" highlights recent advancements in enabling large language models to interact with external tools and APIs to enhance their reasoning and decision-making capabilities. Research has shown that while LLMs excel at understanding and generating human-like text, they often struggle with factual accuracy and task-specific actions without external assistance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. To address this, methods like ReAct have been proposed, which integrate reasoning traces with task-specific actions, allowing models to dynamically update plans and handle exceptions by interacting with external sources such as knowledge bases or APIs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. Similarly, Toolformer demonstrates how models can be trained to autonomously decide when and how to use external tools, such as calculators, search engines, and translation systems, through self-supervised learning with minimal demonstrations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. These approaches improve zero-shot performance on diverse tasks and maintain core language modeling abilities, offering a more robust and adaptable framework for real-world applications \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. Both ReAct and Toolformer emphasize the importance of external interaction in overcoming limitations of pure language-based reasoning and enhancing the reliability and interpretability of model outputs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\].

### <a id='category-fine-tuning-with-negative-examples'></a>Fine-Tuning with Negative Examples: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Large language models (LLMs) have achieved success in acting as agents, which interact with environments through tools such as search engines. However, LLMs are optimized for language generation instead of tool use during training or alignment, limiting their effectiveness as agents. To resolve this problem, previous work has first collected interaction trajectories between LLMs and environments, using only trajectories that successfully finished the task to fine-tune smaller models, making fine-tuning data scarce and acquiring it both difficult and costly. Discarding failed trajectories also leads to significant wastage of data and resources and limits the possible optimization paths during fine-tuning. In this paper, we argue that unsuccessful trajectories offer valuable insights, and LLMs can learn from these trajectories through appropriate quality control and fine-tuning strategies. By simply adding a prefix or suffix that tells the model whether to generate a successful trajectory during training, we improve model performance by a large margin on mathematical reasoning, multi-hop question answering, and strategic question answering tasks. We further analyze the inference results and find that our method provides a better trade-off between valuable information and errors in unsuccessful trajectories. To our knowledge, we are the first to demonstrate the value of negative trajectories and their application in agent-tuning scenarios. Our findings offer guidance for developing better agent-tuning methods and low-resource data usage techniques.»
