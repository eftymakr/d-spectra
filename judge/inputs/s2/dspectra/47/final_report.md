## Related Works

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a promising approach to improve recommendation performance by leveraging transferable knowledge across multiple domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. However, existing MDR methods often adopt a single structure to transfer complex domain-shared knowledge, which may not be suitable for all domains due to varying knowledge requirements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. This limitation leads to the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP), where unselective knowledge transfer can degrade performance, especially when there are domain conflicts or poor-quality data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\].

Several works have attempted to address the challenges of MDR. For instance, HAMUR introduces a domain-specific adapter and a domain-shared hyper-network to dynamically generate parameters for the adapter, thereby reducing mutual interference between domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. Similarly, EDDA proposes embedding disentangling and domain alignment to better capture domain-specific and shared knowledge \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. These approaches focus on improving parameter sharing and knowledge disentanglement but do not explicitly address the issue of selecting appropriate domains for knowledge transfer.

In the realm of cross-domain recommendation (CDR), MSDCR treats multiple domains as sparse and simultaneously improves performance across all domains by transferring complementary aspect preferences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.05973)\]. DTCDR, on the other hand, focuses on dual-target CDR by utilizing rating and multi-source content information to enhance recommendation performance on both richer and sparser domains \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b031c7170c9dccbe506f3d3a1d6104f65e835ae)\]. DDTCDR extends this idea by introducing a dual learning mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains until the learning process stabilizes \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods emphasize knowledge transfer but often lack dynamic domain selection strategies.

Recent work on cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) has also explored the challenges of negative transfer, particularly when dealing with multiple domains \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. This paper proposes a framework that estimates the extent of negative transfer and adaptively assigns low weights to prediction losses, thereby improving performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.13188)\]. Additionally, PPGN introduces a Cross-Domain Preference Matrix (CDPM) to model interactions across domains and captures high-order information propagation through a propagation layer \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\]. These methods highlight the importance of structured knowledge transfer but do not explicitly address domain selection.

PLATE proposes a prompt-enhanced paradigm for multi-scenario recommendations, combining a unified deep recommender system (DRS) backbone with prompt modules to capture domain-specific characteristics \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/884f8a29a7979a1f05223f432d9a149ac4dcc6b4)\]. MAMDR, on the other hand, introduces a model-agnostic learning framework that enables scalability across thousands of domains by incorporating domain negotiation and regularization strategies \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12524)\]. These approaches emphasize scalability and generalization but may not directly address the issue of dynamic domain selection

### <a id='category-cross-domain-recommendation-frameworks'></a>Cross-Domain Recommendation Frameworks: 7 papers found.
Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR) has been a focal point in improving recommendation performance by leveraging information across domains to alleviate data sparsity and cold-start issues \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.05508)\]. Traditional CDR methods often assume at least one rich domain and rely heavily on domain-invariant preferences, which may not hold in real-world scenarios where sparsity is common \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. To address these limitations, recent approaches have focused on multi-target CDR, such as the Multi-Sparse-Domain Collaborative Recommendation (MSDCR) model, which treats all domains as sparse and enhances domain-specific preferences through domain-invariant aspects \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06217)\]. Similarly, the Deep Dual Transfer Cross Domain Recommendation (DDTCDR) model introduces a dual learning mechanism to transfer information between domains iteratively, improving the modeling of user and item features \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Another advancement, Multi-Domain Sequential Recommendation via Domain Space Learning (MDSR-DSL), addresses the challenge of capturing accurate domain-specific sequential preferences by utilizing cross-domain items to supplement missing context and introducing a domain space for maintaining domain-specific characteristics \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/756e3e6c0956d1daa19367eb13115151b20eeb7f)\]. Additionally, the Preference Propagation GraphNet (PPGN) tackles cross-domain sparsity by constructing a Cross-Domain Preference Matrix (CDPM) and propagating user preferences across the graph, simplifying the recommendation task into a unified multi-task model \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/24a473700894dbfab0bfd9dc3d542acfeef66bca)\]. These methods collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve cross-domain recommendation by addressing domain-specific challenges and enhancing the transfer of knowledge between domains.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «In order to address the data sparsity problem in recommender systems, in recent years, Cross-Domain Recommendation (CDR) leverages the relatively richer information from a source domain to improve the recommendation performance on a target domain with sparser information. However, each of the two domains may be relatively richer in certain types of information (e.g., ratings, reviews, user profiles, item details, and tags), and thus, if we can leverage such information well, it is possible to improve the recommendation performance on both domains simultaneously (i.e., dual-target CDR), rather than a single target domain only. Then, based on Multi-Task Learning (MTL), we design an adaptable embedding-sharing strategy to combine and share the embeddings of common users across domains, with which DTCDR can improve the recommendation performance on both richer and sparser (i.e., dual-target) domains simultaneously. Extensive experiments conducted on real-world datasets demonstrate that DTCDR can significantly improve the recommendation accuracies on both richer and sparser domains and outperform the state-of-the-art single-domain and cross-domain approaches.»

### <a id='category-negative-transfer-problem-(ntp)-addressal'></a>Negative Transfer Problem (NTP) Addressal: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «This paper investigates Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR), a promising method that uses information from multiple domains (more than three) to generate accurate and diverse recommendations, and takes into account the sequential nature of user interactions. The effectiveness of these systems often depends on the complex interplay among the multiple domains. In this dynamic landscape, the problem of negative transfer arises, where heterogeneous knowledge between dissimilar domains leads to performance degradation due to differences in user preferences across these domains. As a remedy, we propose a new CDSR framework that addresses the problem of negative transfer by assessing the extent of negative transfer from one domain to another and adaptively assigning low weight values to the corresponding prediction losses. To this end, the amount of negative transfer is estimated by measuring the marginal contribution of each domain to model performance based on a cooperative game theory. In addition, a hierarchical contrastive learning approach that incorporates information from the sequence of coarse-level categories into that of fine-level categories (e.g., item level) when implementing contrastive learning was developed to mitigate negative transfer. Despite the potentially low relevance between domains at the fine-level, there may be higher relevance at the category level due to its generalised and broader preferences. We show that our model is superior to prior works in terms of model performance on two real-world datasets across ten different domains.»

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «This paper addresses the general problem of domain adaptation which arises in a variety of applications where the distribution of the labeled sample available somewhat differs from that of the test data. Building on previous work by Ben-David et al. (2007), we introduce a novel distance between distributions, discrepancy distance, that is tailored to adaptation problems with arbitrary loss functions. We give Rademacher complexity bounds for estimating the discrepancy distance from finite samples for different loss functions. Using this distance, we derive new generalization bounds for domain adaptation for a wide family of loss functions. We also present a series of novel adaptation bounds for large classes of regularization-based algorithms, including support vector machines and kernel ridge regression based on the empirical discrepancy. This motivates our analysis of the problem of minimizing the empirical discrepancy for various loss functions for which we also give several algorithms.
