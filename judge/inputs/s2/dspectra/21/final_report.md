## Related Works

Video frame interpolation and neural rendering have both seen significant advancements in recent years, with a growing emphasis on leveraging deep learning techniques to improve performance and efficiency. Video frame interpolation, which involves generating intermediate frames between existing ones to create smooth video sequences, has been extensively studied. Early methods relied on optical flow estimation and warping techniques \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\], but recent approaches have moved towards end-to-end learning frameworks. For example, Super SloMo \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\] introduces a convolutional neural network that jointly models motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning, using a U-Net architecture to compute bi-directional optical flow and refine intermediate frames. Similarly, Zoom-In-To-Check \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01210)\] proposes a lightweight algorithm that uses instance-level supervision to improve the quality of interpolated frames by focusing on high-resolution object details. These methods demonstrate the potential of deep learning to handle complex motion and occlusion scenarios effectively.

Neural rendering, on the other hand, focuses on reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images, often using implicit or explicit representations to model geometry and appearance. Techniques such as Deep Stereo \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\] and Neural 3D Mesh Renderer \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07566)\] have explored how deep networks can synthesize new views from existing ones, leveraging the spatial and temporal coherence of real-world scenes. More recently, methods like NeuS \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\] have introduced novel neural surface reconstruction techniques that enable high-fidelity 3D modeling without the need for explicit foreground masks. These approaches highlight the importance of learning robust scene representations that can be used for both novel view synthesis and 3D reconstruction tasks.

The integration of video frame interpolation into neural rendering pipelines has also been explored. For instance, the work in \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\] proposes a neural field-based approach for video interpolation, where the video is represented as a continuous function parameterized by a neural network. This method allows for the synthesis of high-quality intermediate frames while maintaining spatial and temporal consistency. Similarly, the use of video frame interpolation as a data augmentation technique for neural rendering has been proposed in \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1604.00449)\], where interpolated frames are used to enrich training data and improve the generalization of 3D reconstruction models. ### <a id='category-efficient-video-processing-architectures'></a>Efficient Video Processing Architectures: 5 papers found.
Efficient video processing architectures have been a focal point in recent research, with a particular emphasis on improving the scalability and performance of video frame interpolation methods. Traditional approaches often rely on optical flow estimation, which can be computationally expensive and prone to error propagation, especially under large motion or occlusion \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. To address these limitations, several studies have proposed deep learning-based solutions that either eliminate the need for explicit optical flow estimation or optimize the computational efficiency of the model. For instance, IM-Net introduces an economic structured architecture and end-to-end training with multi-scale tailored losses, achieving high performance on high-resolution benchmarks \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\]. Similarly, a method that formulates frame interpolation as local separable convolution using 1D kernels reduces parameter count and memory demand, enabling efficient kernel estimation across the entire video frame \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Another approach leverages splatting-based synthesis, which is not only faster but also achieves state-of-the-art results at high resolutions \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. Additionally, a lightweight model designed for video frame interpolation replaces optical flow computation with a feature reshaping operation and channel attention mechanism, enabling effective motion estimation at the pixel level \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\]. These advancements highlight a growing trend toward more efficient and scalable deep learning architectures for video processing, focusing on reducing computational overhead while maintaining or improving the quality of interpolated frames.
