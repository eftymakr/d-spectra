## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a powerful alternative to traditional sparse retrieval methods, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to generate dense vector representations of text that can be compared using simple similarity operations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This approach has been extensively studied, with several works focusing on improving the quality of dense representations through various pre-training and fine-tuning strategies. For instance, BERT, a foundational model in this area, was introduced as a bidirectional encoder that pre-trains on unlabeled text to capture contextualized language understanding \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. This pre-training allows BERT to be fine-tuned for downstream tasks, including dense retrieval, where it has demonstrated strong performance \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\].

Recent studies have questioned the role of fine-tuning in dense retrievers, arguing that much of the retrieval knowledge is acquired during pre-training rather than through fine-tuning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This idea was initially explored in the context of BERT-based dense retrievers, such as the DPR (Dense Passage Retrieval) model \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07577)\]. However, the analysis was limited to a specific encoder architecture and dataset. To extend this understanding, our study examines different representation approaches, including the use of CLS tokens versus mean pooling, and explores various backbone architectures, such as encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Additionally, we evaluate the performance across multiple datasets, including MSMARCO and Natural Questions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\].

The effectiveness of contrastive learning in dense retrieval has also been a focus of prior research. For example, the Condenser architecture was proposed to pre-train dense encoders by conditioning LM prediction on dense representations, improving retrieval performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, coCondenser introduced an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for heavy data engineering \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Another approach, ANCE, constructs negative instances from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index of the corpus, improving the alignment between training and testing data distributions \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\].

Moreover, the importance of pre-training for dense retrieval has been further emphasized by works such as SimLM, which employs a bottleneck architecture to compress passage information into dense vectors through self-supervised pre-training \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02578)\]. Similarly, Llama2Vec proposes an unsupervised adaptation of large language models (LLMs) for dense retrieval by introducing pretext tasks that reconstruct input sentences and predict the next sentence based on their embeddings \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15503)\]. These methods highlight the significance of pre-training in developing effective dense retrievers.
