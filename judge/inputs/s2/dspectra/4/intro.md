## Related Works

The problem of learning from noisy labels has been extensively studied in recent years, with a growing body of research focused on improving the robustness of deep learning models. Early approaches, such as those based on bootstrapping \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6596)\], attempted to mitigate the impact of noisy labels by enforcing consistency between predictions. These methods assume that similar inputs should yield similar outputs, which can help reduce the influence of incorrect labels. However, they often fail when the noise is not uniform across the dataset \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\].

More recently, researchers have explored the use of regularization to prevent models from memorizing noisy labels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00151)\]. This approach leverages the early learning phase, where models initially learn from clean examples before being exposed to noisy ones. By introducing a regularization term that encourages the model to align with these early predictions, the method effectively prevents memorization of false labels. This idea has been further extended to semi-supervised learning settings, where models are trained on both labeled and unlabeled data to improve robustness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.07394)\].

Another line of work focuses on the structural properties of noisy labels and how they can be modeled. For instance, some studies have shown that label noise can be instance-dependent, meaning that the probability of a label being incorrect varies based on the task difficulty or the data characteristics \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. This has led to the development of more sophisticated models that account for these dependencies, such as those based on second-order statistics \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. These methods estimate the covariance between noise rates and the Bayes optimal label, allowing for more accurate modeling of label noise.

In addition to these methods, there has been growing interest in using knowledge distillation to improve robustness against label noise \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02391)\]. This approach leverages the knowledge of a teacher model to guide the learning of a student model, even in the presence of noisy labels. By using a small clean dataset and label relations from a knowledge graph, the method effectively "hedges the risk" of learning from noisy labels. This approach has been successfully applied to various domains, including image classification and natural language understanding \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02391)\].

Furthermore, the problem of label noise has also been addressed in the context of scene graph generation, where the assumption that all manually annotated positive samples are equally correct is often invalid \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\]. To tackle this, researchers have proposed methods such as NICEST, which includes a novel label correction strategy to detect and correct noisy samples \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\]. This approach combines techniques like density-based clustering and weighted KNN to improve the quality of the labels used during training.
