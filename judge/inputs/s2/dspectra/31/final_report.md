## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning rich representations from unlabeled data. Several approaches have been proposed to leverage this paradigm in various domains, including computer vision and natural language processing. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\] explores the use of spatial context as a supervisory signal for training a rich visual representation, where a convolutional neural network is trained to predict the position of one patch relative to another. This approach demonstrates that such models can capture visual similarity across images and improve performance in object detection tasks. Similarly, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\] introduces Contrastive Predictive Coding, a universal unsupervised learning approach that leverages autoregressive models to predict future samples in latent space, achieving strong performance across speech, images, text, and reinforcement learning domains.

In the field of computer vision, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1206.5538)\] presents a method for unsupervised visual representation learning by solving jigsaw puzzles, where the model learns to recognize the geometric arrangement of image tiles. This approach outperforms existing unsupervised features in object detection and classification tasks. Additionally, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\] proposes a method for unsupervised feature learning by predicting image rotations, which provides a powerful supervisory signal for semantic feature learning. The model achieves state-of-the-art performance on various benchmarks, including PASCAL VOC 2007 detection, demonstrating that unsupervised pre-training can significantly close the gap with supervised learning.

In the context of natural language processing, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.09005)\] introduces SpanBERT, which improves pre-training by representing and predicting spans of text. This approach extends BERT by masking contiguous random spans and training the model to predict the entire content of the masked span, leading to substantial gains in tasks such as question answering and coreference resolution. Furthermore, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\] presents a robustly optimized BERT pretraining approach, highlighting the importance of hyperparameter choices and training data size in achieving state-of-the-art results on GLUE, RACE, and SQuAD benchmarks.

In the domain of semi-supervised learning, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\] explores techniques such as randomized data augmentation and dropout to improve generalization and stability for classifiers trained with limited labeled data. The proposed method minimizes the difference between predictions from multiple passes of a training sample through the network, leading to better performance on benchmark datasets. Additionally, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\] introduces a scalable approach for semi-supervised learning on graph-structured data using graph convolutional networks, which outperforms related methods by a significant margin.

### <a id='category-unsupervised-pre-training-paradigms'></a>Unsupervised Pre-training Paradigms: 13 papers found.
The category "Unsupervised Pre-training Paradigms" encompasses a variety of approaches aimed at learning robust feature representations without the need for explicit supervision. These methods often rely on self-supervised tasks, such as predicting the position of image patches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\], reconstructing input data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09272)\], or learning to predict future samples in latent space \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Such paradigms have been successfully applied to diverse domains, including computer vision, natural language processing, and reinforcement learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. For instance, contrastive predictive coding has shown strong performance across speech, images, text, and 3D environments by leveraging probabilistic contrastive losses \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Similarly, methods like jigsaw puzzles and rotation prediction have been used to train convolutional neural networks on unlabeled data, enabling the learning of semantically meaningful features \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\]. These approaches often emphasize the importance of capturing latent structures and relationships within data, such as the geometric arrangement of image tiles \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\] or the semantic clustering of prototypes \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\]. Additionally, unsupervised learning has been shown to improve downstream tasks, such as object detection and classification, by providing better initialization points than random weights \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\]. The effectiveness of these paradigms is further supported by their ability to generalize across different regions and tasks, even in the absence of labeled data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. Overall, these methods highlight the potential of unsupervised learning to capture rich, meaningful representations that can be transferred to a wide range of applications.

### <a id='category-data-augmentation-techniques'></a>Data Augmentation Techniques: 4 papers found.
In the context of data augmentation techniques, several approaches have been proposed to enhance model performance when labeled data is limited. Semi-supervised learning, for instance, leverages unlabeled data to improve classifier accuracy, often using techniques like randomized data augmentation, dropout, and random max-pooling to enhance generalization and stability \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\]. These methods introduce non-deterministic behavior, which can lead to varied predictions across multiple passes of the same sample, prompting the development of unsupervised loss functions that minimize prediction differences \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04586)\]. Similarly, domain adaptation techniques aim to transfer knowledge between different data distributions by promoting features that are discriminative for the source domain but indiscriminate between domains \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. This is achieved through architectural modifications such as gradient reversal layers, enabling models to be trained with standard backpropagation \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.07818)\]. In parallel, programmatic weak supervision (PWS) has emerged as a way to reduce manual labeling efforts by synthesizing training labels from multiple noisy sources, offering a flexible framework for handling limited labeled data \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.05433)\]. Additionally, self-supervised learning approaches, such as the PASE+ model, have been developed to extract meaningful representations from unlabelled audio by incorporating online distortion modules and refined neural network architectures \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09239)\]. These techniques collectively highlight the importance of leveraging unlabeled data and advanced architectural designs to overcome the challenges of limited supervision in machine learning.

Markov modeling has long been applied to DNA sequences, for example to find gene-coding regions. With the first studies came the discovery that DNA sequences are non-stationary: distinct regions require distinct model orders. Since then, Markov and hidden Markov models have been extensively used to describe the gene structure of prokaryotes and eukaryotes. However, to our knowledge, a comprehensive study about the potential of Markov models to describe complete genomes is still lacking. We address this gap in this paper. Our approach relies on (i) multiple competing Markov models of different orders (ii) careful programming techniques that allow orders as large as sixteen (iii) adequate inverted repeat handling (iv) probability estimates suited to the wide range of context depths used. To measure how well a model fits the data at a particular position in the sequence we use the negative logarithm of the probability estimate at that position. The measure yields information profiles of the sequence, which are of independent interest. The average over the entire sequence, which amounts to the average number of bits per base needed to describe the sequence, is used as a global performance measure. Our main conclusion is that, from the probabilistic or information theoretic point of view and according to this performance measure, multiple competing Markov models explain entire genomes almost as well or even better than state-of-the-art DNA compression methods, such as XM, which rely on very different statistical models. This is surprising, because Markov models are local (short-range), contrasting with the statistical models underlying other methods, where the extensive data repetitions in DNA sequences is explored, and therefore have a non-local character.»
