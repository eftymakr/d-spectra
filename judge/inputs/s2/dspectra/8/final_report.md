## Related Works

The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) has underscored the critical need for robust fairness benchmarks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, often overlooking the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. This limitation is well-documented in the literature, where studies have shown that conventional fairness measures fail to capture the nuanced ways in which models may exhibit bias \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. For instance, models may display high confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups, a phenomenon that is not detected by traditional fairness criteria \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. This gap in fairness evaluation has motivated the development of new metrics that incorporate uncertainty into fairness assessments.

Several recent works have explored fairness in LLMs, highlighting the importance of diverse and representative datasets for evaluating model behavior \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. The UCI Adult dataset, while widely used, has been criticized for its limited external validity and lack of diversity \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. In response, researchers have proposed new datasets that better reflect real-world scenarios and demographic diversity \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. For example, the Folktables dataset, derived from US Census surveys, provides a more comprehensive and diverse set of tabular data for fairness-aware machine learning research \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.04884)\]. These datasets are essential for evaluating the fairness of LLMs in a variety of contexts, including gender-occupation prediction \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\].

The proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metric, UCerF, builds on earlier work that has sought to integrate uncertainty into fairness evaluations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. For instance, the concept of counterfactual fairness, which considers the fairness of a decision in both the actual and a counterfactual world, has been explored in the context of predictive policing and hiring \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. Similarly, fairness through awareness, which emphasizes the importance of treating similar individuals similarly, has been applied in classification tasks to reduce discrimination \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1104.3913)\]. These approaches highlight the need for fairness metrics that go beyond simple accuracy and consider the broader implications of model decisions.

In addition to fairness metrics, the design of evaluation datasets plays a crucial role in assessing model behavior \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. The new gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset introduced in this work contains 31,756 samples, offering a more diverse and suitable benchmark for evaluating modern LLMs \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. This dataset addresses the limitations of existing benchmarks, which often lack sufficient diversity and clarity \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. By providing a more representative sample of real-world scenarios, this dataset enables a more accurate assessment of fairness in LLMs.

### <a id='category-traditional-fairness-metrics-and-limitations'></a>Traditional Fairness Metrics and Limitations: 5 papers found.
The category "Traditional Fairness Metrics and Limitations" highlights the shortcomings of existing fairness metrics in machine learning, particularly their inability to capture nuanced forms of bias and their susceptibility to manipulation. Traditional fairness metrics often rely on statistical parity or equalized odds, which enforce parity of certain statistics across predefined groups \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. However, these metrics are vulnerable to "fairness gerrymandering," where a classifier may appear fair on individual groups but fail to account for structured subgroups \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. Furthermore, these metrics often fail to address the implicit impact of model uncertainty, such as when a model exhibits higher confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups, even if accuracy is comparable \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.02012)\]. This limitation is particularly relevant in the context of large language models (LLMs), where conventional fairness metrics based on discrete accuracy fail to capture the internal bias reflected in model confidence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.09784)\]. Recent work has sought to address these limitations by proposing more robust fairness definitions that consider subgroup fairness and model uncertainty \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. These efforts underscore the need for more comprehensive fairness metrics that align with the complexities of real-world decision-making scenarios.
