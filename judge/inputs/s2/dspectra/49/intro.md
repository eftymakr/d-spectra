## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have shown significant promise in Web search tasks, particularly in improving the relevance of query-document pairs \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08136)\]. However, training these models typically requires a large amount of labeled query-document pairs, which are expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Recent advances have explored the use of synthetic queries generated by large language models as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, synthetic queries often vary in quality, leading to suboptimal performance in downstream retrieval tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Existing approaches typically filter out noisy query-document pairs using signals from an external re-ranker \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) has emerged as a promising technique for aligning large language models with human preferences without the need for a reward model or reinforcement learning \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. DPO fine-tunes the model to increase the likelihood of preferred responses over dispreferred ones, using only a simple classification loss \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. This approach has been shown to be stable, performant, and computationally lightweight \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. However, not all preference pairs are equal; some may require a larger margin between preferred and dispreferred responses \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14734)\]. To address this, DPO with an offset (ODPO) was proposed, which introduces an offset value to ensure the difference between the likelihood of the preferred and dispreferred responses is greater than a specified threshold \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\].

In the context of retrieval, several works have explored the use of neural ranking models that can effectively capture the relevance between queries and documents \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1610.08136)\]. For instance, K-NRM uses a kernel-based approach to extract multi-level soft match features and combines them into a final ranking score \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.06613)\]. Similarly, the Deep Relevance Matching Model (DRMM) employs a joint deep architecture at the query term level to handle exact matching signals, query term importance, and diverse matching requirements \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08611)\]. These models have shown significant improvements over traditional retrieval methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08611)\].

Another approach to improving retrieval effectiveness is through the use of weak supervision signals, such as those obtained from unsupervised ranking models like BM25 \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\]. Neural ranking models trained with weak supervision have demonstrated impressive performance, achieving over 13% and 35% improvements in MAP over BM25 on standard collections \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.08803)\]. This highlights the potential of using weakly labeled data to train effective retrieval models.
