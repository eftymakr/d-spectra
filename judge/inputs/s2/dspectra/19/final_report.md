## Related Works

Speech-driven facial animation has been a focal point of research in computer vision and machine learning, with numerous approaches proposed to synthesize realistic 3D facial expressions from audio inputs. Early methods often relied on handcrafted features and rule-based systems, which were limited in their ability to capture the complex dynamics of facial motion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\]. More recent approaches have leveraged deep learning, particularly neural networks, to learn mappings from audio signals to facial animations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. These methods typically involve training models on large datasets of audio-visual pairs, where each audio clip is associated with a corresponding video of a talking face \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\].

One notable approach is the use of autoencoders and generative adversarial networks (GANs) to learn the mapping between audio and facial expressions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. For instance, the work by \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02214)\] introduced a temporal GAN with multiple discriminators to achieve detailed frame generation and audio-visual synchronization. This approach demonstrated the potential of GANs in generating high-quality talking face videos. However, these models often struggle with maintaining consistent lip synchronization and facial expressions across different speakers and speech contexts \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03499)\].

Another line of research focuses on the integration of self-supervised learning techniques to improve the robustness of audio-visual feature extraction \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. For example, the WaveNet model \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/435b15745fb279db4ff8daf9d5facb9b21d58f3b)\] was introduced as a deep neural network for generating raw audio waveforms, which has since been adapted for speech-driven facial animation tasks. The ability of WaveNet to capture speaker-specific characteristics and generate natural-sounding speech has been a significant contribution to the field \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379)\].

Recent advances have also emphasized the importance of disentangling audio-visual representations to enable more controllable and realistic facial animations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12992)\]. The work by \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.06059)\] proposed a method to disentangle audio and visual features, allowing for more accurate lip synchronization and better generalization across different speakers. This approach was further refined in \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08223)\], where a categorical latent space was introduced to disentangle audio-correlated and audio-uncorrelated information, leading to more natural facial animations.

Additionally, the integration of linguistic and emotional cues has been explored to enhance the expressiveness of generated facial animations \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089)\]. For example, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990)\] introduced a method to disentangle emotional and content-related features in speech, enabling the generation of more expressive and emotionally rich facial animations. This approach was further extended in \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\], where a joint audio-text model was proposed to capture contextual information for expressive speech-driven 3D facial animation.

### <a id='category-speech-driven-facial-animation-models'></a>Speech-driven Facial Animation Models: 26 papers found.
In the field of speech-driven facial animation, recent works have focused on improving the synchronization between audio and visual outputs while addressing challenges such as lip motion accuracy, subject independence, and emotional expression \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.07860)\]. Many approaches rely on learning mappings from audio features to visual features, often using pre-trained self-supervised audio models as encoders \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329)\]. However, phonetically similar syllables with distinct lip shapes can lead to coupling in audio feature spaces, causing an averaging effect that degrades lip motion generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.06337)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed various modules, such as Wav2Sem, which introduces a semantic decorrelation mechanism to enhance audio feature expressiveness \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10799)\]. Additionally, methods like Face-Former utilize Transformer-based autoregressive models to encode long-term audio context and predict 3D facial animations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02214)\]. Other works, such as SadTalker, focus on generating 3D motion coefficients from audio and implicitly modulating a 3D-aware face render for more natural head and facial movements \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.03499)\]. Furthermore, some studies integrate emotional disentanglement, such as Emotalk, which separates emotional and content-related information to generate more expressive facial animations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11116)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve realism, synchronization, and personalization in speech-driven facial animation.

Additionally, there has been a growing emphasis on developing rigorous evaluation benchmarks and metrics to accurately measure lip synchronization in unconstrained videos \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010)\]. Furthermore, studies have explored the potential of combining lip reading with audio speech recognition, particularly in noisy environments, to improve overall performance \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02108)\].
