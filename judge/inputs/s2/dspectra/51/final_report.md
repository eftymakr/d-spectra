Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a promising approach to train machine learning models on decentralized data while preserving user privacy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. However, the increasing demand for data privacy, particularly under regulations such as the "right to be forgotten," has led to the development of federated unlearning (FU) techniques that allow users to remove their data from the global model \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. This section reviews related works in the areas of federated learning, machine unlearning, and evaluation practices for unlearning methods.

Federated learning enables collaborative training of models across multiple clients without sharing raw data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. However, existing FL approaches often fail to address the issue of data erasure, which is critical for compliance with privacy regulations \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. In response, federated unlearning has been introduced as a mechanism to remove the influence of specific clients or data samples from the global model \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Several methods have been proposed to achieve this, including retraining from scratch, which is computationally expensive \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\], and more efficient techniques such as local unlearning and parameter update reconstruction \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05521)\].

Machine unlearning, a broader concept, refers to the ability of a model to "forget" certain data points or classes \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\]. This is particularly important in the context of deep learning, where models can memorize sensitive information \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02299)\]. Recent approaches have focused on developing efficient unlearning methods that minimize the need for full retraining \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.09441)\]. Similarly, the SISA training framework improves unlearning efficiency by strategically limiting the influence of specific data points during training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\].

### <a id='category-federated-learning-foundations'></a>Federated Learning Foundations: 9 papers found.
Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a critical framework for collaborative training of machine learning models while preserving data privacy, particularly in scenarios where data is distributed across multiple parties \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. However, FL faces challenges such as suboptimal performance when client data distributions are non-iid \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\], and privacy risks due to potential data leakage during model update exchanges \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f2f8f7a2ec1b2ede48cbcd189b376ab9fa0735ef)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various techniques, including clustered FL (CFL), which groups clients based on data distribution to improve model accuracy without altering the FL communication protocol \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01991)\]. Additionally, methods like federated transfer learning (FTL) enable knowledge sharing between domains without compromising privacy, allowing target-domain parties to benefit from source-domain data \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03337)\]. Furthermore, local adaptation techniques such as fine-tuning, multi-task learning, and knowledge distillation have been explored to improve the performance of federated models for individual clients \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Communication efficiency remains a key concern, with approaches like sparse ternary compression (STC) offering significant improvements in reducing communication overhead while maintaining model accuracy \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.02891)\]. Despite these advancements, challenges such as membership inference attacks and the impact of entity resolution errors on learning outcomes persist, highlighting the need for robust privacy-preserving mechanisms and rigorous evaluation practices \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f2f8f7a2ec1b2ede48cbcd189b376ab9fa0735ef)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.04049)\]\[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10677)\]. These developments underscore the growing importance of FL in balancing data privacy, model performance, and system efficiency.

### <a id='category-machine-unlearning-methods'></a>Machine Unlearning Methods: 5 papers found.
The category "Machine Unlearning Methods" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at enabling machine learning models to "forget" specific data points or classes of data, primarily in response to privacy regulations like the "right to be forgotten" \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. This field has gained traction due to the increasing need for compliance with data privacy laws, which require services to allow users to remove their data from training sets \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]. Several methods have been proposed, including SISA training, which accelerates unlearning by limiting the influence of specific data points during training, thereby reducing computational overhead \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03817)\]. Linear filtration is another approach, offering a computationally efficient way to sanitize data and improve performance in adversarial settings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02730)\]. Additionally, a backdoor-based verification mechanism has been introduced to ensure that data deletion requests are effectively processed, using hypothesis testing to quantify the success of unlearning \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04247)\]. The concept of zero retrain forgetting (ZRF) has also been introduced as a metric to evaluate unlearning without requiring retraining from scratch, making it useful for post-deployment analysis \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.08096)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the difficulty in verifying whether unlearning has been successfully executed, as even with a given training trajectory, it is not possible to formally prove the absence of certain data points \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11891)\]. These methods collectively highlight the growing importance of machine unlearning in balancing data privacy with model performance.

### <a id='category-federated-unlearning-techniques'></a>Federated Unlearning Techniques: 14 papers found.
Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical area of research to address the "right to be forgotten" in federated learning (FL) systems, enabling users to remove their data contributions from global models while maintaining model utility \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. This is particularly important in FL, where data remains decentralized, and traditional unlearning techniques designed for centralized systems are not directly applicable \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07320)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to tackle this challenge, including methods that leverage historical parameter updates to reconstruct unlearned models without full retraining \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/eadeffdec9fac8fd7f9aea732ca410eb082b7dcf)\]. For instance, FedEraser reduces the time required to construct an unlearned model by trading server storage for faster reconstruction, using calibrated historical updates from clients \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Similarly, other works have introduced novel techniques such as subtracting accumulated updates from the model and using knowledge distillation to restore performance without client data \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.09441)\]. These methods aim to balance efficiency and effectiveness, as full retraining is computationally expensive and impractical in large-scale FL settings \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/39da2d48f2132b919405e3bb6df6ece44ac6e15a)\]. Additionally, some studies have focused on the evaluation of unlearning effectiveness, proposing metrics to measure success and addressing challenges like under-unlearning and over-unlearning \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. The need for robust and verifiable unlearning mechanisms has also led to the development of frameworks that consider privacy risks and information leakage during model updates \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a52c3ec7325e81bd87fdf8d74bf9acfd1e1ffe5f)\]. Overall, federated unlearning remains an active research area, with ongoing efforts to improve efficiency, accuracy, and compliance with legal and ethical standards \[[30](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17462)\].
