## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods relied on matrix factorization to learn low-dimensional embeddings, but recent advances have shifted toward deep learning-based approaches that better capture complex interactions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Among these, graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful tool for modeling user-item interactions through graph structures \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. However, GNNs often suffer from over-smoothing and scalability issues due to recursive message propagation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have proposed simpler variants such as LightGCN, which focuses on linear neighborhood aggregation and avoids unnecessary nonlinear operations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This simplification leads to better performance and easier training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

In addition to GNNs, contrastive learning has been explored to enhance representation quality by leveraging self-supervised tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]. For instance, Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning (NCL) incorporates structural and semantic neighbors into contrastive pairs, improving performance on sparse data \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]. Similarly, LightGCL introduces a novel contrastive learning paradigm using singular value decomposition for structural refinement, achieving robustness against data sparsity \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08191)\]. These methods highlight the importance of preserving semantic relationships while mitigating noise in the graph structure \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06200)\]\[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08191)\].

Another key area of research is the use of binary representations for efficient inference in recommendation systems \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Binarized collaborative filtering aims to reduce memory and computational overhead by converting embeddings into compact binary codes. However, this process often leads to information loss, which degrades performance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. To address this, the paper introduces BiGeaR++, which explicitly mitigates information loss during binarization through fine-grained inference distillation and effective embedding sample synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This approach improves performance by up to 10% compared to its predecessor BiGeaR \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Furthermore, graph embedding methods such as LINE and GraphSAGE have been widely used for learning node representations in large-scale networks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03578)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. LINE preserves both local and global structures by optimizing an objective function that captures edge relationships \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03578)\]. GraphSAGE, on the other hand, generates embeddings for unseen nodes by sampling and aggregating features from local neighborhoods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. These methods provide a foundation for learning robust and scalable representations in recommendation systems \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03578)\].

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-for-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph Neural Networks for Collaborative Filtering: 5 papers found.
Recent advancements in graph neural networks (GNNs) have significantly impacted collaborative filtering by enabling more effective modeling of user-item interactions. Early methods, such as matrix factorization, relied on pre-defined features to generate embeddings, but these approaches often failed to capture the collaborative signal inherent in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. To address this limitation, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) was introduced, which integrates the bipartite graph structure of user-item interactions into the embedding process, allowing for the explicit modeling of high-order connectivity \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, NGCF and similar models often incorporate complex neural network operations that may hinder training efficiency and performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. In response, LightGCN was proposed as a simplified variant of GCN, focusing only on the essential component of neighborhood aggregation, leading to significant improvements in performance and ease of training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Additionally, challenges such as over-smoothing and poor scalability in GNN-based models have been tackled through innovations like SimRec, which combines knowledge distillation and contrastive learning to enhance representation quality while maintaining efficiency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08537)\]. Furthermore, Multi-GCCF extends the use of GNNs by incorporating multiple graphs—user-user, item-item, and user-item—to better capture both high-order and proximal information \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of GNNs in collaborative filtering, with a focus on improving both the accuracy and efficiency of recommendation systems.

### <a id='category-representation-learning-in-recommender-systems'></a>Representation Learning in Recommender Systems: 4 papers found.
Representation learning in recommender systems has seen significant advancements, particularly in the areas of collaborative filtering and graph-based models. Recent works have focused on improving the modeling capacity beyond traditional linear factor models by introducing non-linear probabilistic frameworks, such as variational autoencoders (VAEs) with multinomial likelihoods, which enable more flexible and accurate representation of user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. These models leverage Bayesian inference for parameter estimation, offering information-theoretic benefits such as connections to maximum entropy discrimination and the information bottleneck principle \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Additionally, efforts have been made to enhance representation quality by focusing on alignment and uniformity on the hypersphere, leading to the development of learning objectives like DirectAU that directly optimize these properties \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Graph-based approaches, such as graph convolution networks (GCNs), have also gained traction for recommendation tasks, though they face challenges related to high-degree nodes and noisy interactions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. To address these limitations, self-supervised learning strategies, such as Self-supervised Graph Learning (SGL), have been proposed to improve the robustness and accuracy of GCNs by incorporating auxiliary tasks that reinforce node representation learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of representation learning techniques in recommender systems, aiming to balance efficiency, accuracy, and robustness.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-in-graph-based-models'></a>Contrastive Learning in Graph-Based Models: 2 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Recently, graph collaborative filtering methods have been proposed as an effective recommendation approach, which can capture users’ preference over items by modeling the user-item interaction graphs. Despite the effectiveness, these methods suffer from data sparsity in real scenarios. In order to reduce the influence of data sparsity, contrastive learning is adopted in graph collaborative filtering for enhancing the performance. However, these methods typically construct the contrastive pairs by random sampling, which neglect the neighboring relations among users (or items) and fail to fully exploit the potential of contrastive learning for recommendation. To tackle the above issue, we propose a novel contrastive learning approach, named Neighborhood-enriched Contrastive Learning, named NCL, which explicitly incorporates the potential neighbors into contrastive pairs. Specifically, we introduce the neighbors of a user (or an item) from graph structure and semantic space respectively. For the structural neighbors on the interaction graph, we develop a novel structure-contrastive objective that regards users (or items) and their structural neighbors as positive contrastive pairs. In implementation, the representations of users (or items) and neighbors correspond to the outputs of different GNN layers. Furthermore, to excavate the potential neighbor relation in semantic space, we assume that users with similar representations are within the semantic neighborhood, and incorporate these semantic neighbors into the prototype-contrastive objective. The proposed NCL can be optimized with EM algorithm and generalized to apply to graph collaborative filtering methods. Extensive experiments on five public datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed NCL, notably with 26% and 17% performance gain over a competitive graph collaborative filtering base model on the Yelp and Amazon-book datasets, respectively. Our implementation code is available at: https://github.com/RUCAIBox/NCL.»
