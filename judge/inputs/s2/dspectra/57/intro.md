## Related Works

Generative retrieval (GR) has recently gained traction as a promising paradigm in information retrieval (IR), offering a direct mapping from queries to document identifiers (docids) without the need for traditional indexing or reranking \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. While many GR models have been developed and evaluated using static document collections, their performance in dynamic environments where document corpora evolve continuously remains underexplored \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\]. This section reviews related works that address the challenges of GR in dynamic settings, as well as alternative approaches to docid design and retrieval efficiency.

Several studies have focused on the design of docids for GR models. For instance, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\] proposes GenRet, a method that learns to tokenize documents into short discrete representations (i.e., docids) via a discrete auto-encoding approach. This method enables end-to-end modeling of document retrieval tasks and achieves state-of-the-art results on benchmark datasets. Similarly, \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6682a3679a17d090830635f1c420126d20eae54a)\] introduces NOVO docids, which are unique n-gram sets that can be generated in any order to retrieve the corresponding document. These docids are optimized through training to better learn semantics and relationships between documents, leading to improved retrieval performance.

Efforts to improve the scalability of GR models have also been explored. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\] investigates how generative retrieval scales to millions of passages, revealing that synthetic queries are crucial for indexing, while existing architectural modifications often fail to account for computational costs. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\] introduces RIPOR, an optimization framework that enhances generative retrieval by introducing a novel prefix-oriented ranking optimization algorithm and constructing document IDs based on relevance associations between queries and documents. These approaches demonstrate the potential of GR to handle large-scale retrieval tasks.

In dynamic environments, the ability to adapt to evolving document collections is critical. \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\] addresses this by proposing CLEVER, a model that enables incremental indexing of new documents while preserving the ability to answer queries with both previously and newly indexed relevant documents. This work highlights the importance of incremental learning and memory-augmented mechanisms for continual learning in GR. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02152)\] further explores few-shot indexing, where an LLM is prompted to generate docids for all documents in a corpus without any training, offering a promising direction for reducing training costs.

Additionally, \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12499)\] introduces a listwise generative retrieval approach that optimizes the relevance of entire docid lists rather than individual docids. This method improves retrieval performance by considering the sequential nature of ranking, offering a more principled approach to GR.
