## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to perform complex reasoning tasks, often enhanced through techniques like chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. However, identifying specific behaviors such as refusal within LLMs remains a significant challenge, as existing methods often rely on predefined templates or manual analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This limitation motivates the development of automated frameworks for steering LLMs toward desired behaviors, such as safer or more aligned outputs.

Recent work has explored the use of alignment techniques to improve the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. For instance, Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) introduces a constraint alignment loss that ensures positive responses outperform negative ones while keeping negative scores within a reasonable range \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where models are expected to provide reliable reasoning, such as in mathematical reasoning tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09583)\]. Similarly, the ReAct framework synergizes reasoning and acting by allowing models to interact with external sources, such as knowledge bases, to gather additional information \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. This interaction helps mitigate issues like hallucination and error propagation, which are common in purely language-based reasoning approaches.

The importance of in-context learning has also been emphasized, as LLMs can utilize extensive background knowledge and task information without requiring explicit training data \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18223)\]. This capability is further extended in multimodal settings, where models like MMICL enhance vision-language understanding by efficiently handling complex multi-modal inputs \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07915)\]. These models leverage in-context learning to adapt to diverse tasks, demonstrating the versatility of LLMs in cross-modal reasoning.

Moreover, the evaluation of LLMs has raised concerns about fairness and bias. Studies have shown that the evaluation of LLMs as referees can be systematically biased, with results influenced by the order of responses in the context \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. To address this, calibration frameworks have been proposed, including Multiple Evidence Calibration and Balanced Position Calibration, which aim to reduce evaluation bias and improve alignment with human judgments \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\].

In addition, the role of pre-training and fine-tuning in enhancing LLM capabilities has been widely explored. For example, the LXMERT framework pre-trains on image-and-sentence pairs to learn cross-modal relationships, achieving state-of-the-art results on visual question answering tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. Similarly, ViLBERT extends BERT to a multi-modal model, enabling joint representations of image content and natural language \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02265)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of pre-training in developing robust and transferable representations across modalities.

### <a id='category-alignment-fine-tuning-(aft)'></a>Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT): 2 papers found.
The Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) paradigm addresses the challenge of improving reasoning capabilities in large language models (LLMs) by mitigating the Assessment Misalignment problem, where models tend to assign higher scores to subpar chain-of-thought (COT) responses \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This issue arises during fine-tuning on COT data, leading to potential limitations in reasoning performance. To resolve this, AFT introduces a three-step process: first, fine-tuning LLMs with COT training data; second, generating multiple COT responses and categorizing them into positive and negative based on correctness; and third, calibrating scores using a constraint alignment loss that ensures positive responses outperform negative ones while keeping negative scores within a reasonable range \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This loss function includes two objectives: alignment, which encourages high-quality COTs, and constraint, which prevents model degradation by limiting negative scores \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Furthermore, AFT is adaptable to ranking scenarios when ranking feedback is available, and it builds upon recent ranking-based alignment methods like DPO, RRHF, and PRO by emphasizing the importance of constraint, which these approaches have largely overlooked \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Extensive experiments across four reasoning benchmarks demonstrate AFT's effectiveness in enhancing reasoning performance with both binary and ranking feedback \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Additionally, related work explores alignment techniques such as prompting, imitation learning, and ranked preference modeling, showing that ranked preference modeling scales better with model size than imitation learning \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)\]. These findings highlight the importance of aligning model behavior with human values while maintaining performance and robustness \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)\].

### <a id='category-in-context-learning-capabilities'></a>In-Context Learning Capabilities: 5 papers found.
In-Context Learning Capabilities have been extensively explored in recent research, highlighting the ability of large language models (LLMs) to perform reasoning tasks with minimal or no examples. For instance, the Zero-shot-CoT approach demonstrates that LLMs can achieve significant improvements in arithmetic and symbolic reasoning by simply adding a "Let's think step by step" prompt, without requiring any hand-crafted few-shot examples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. Similarly, Toolformer enables LLMs to use external tools via APIs, achieving competitive performance across various downstream tasks through self-supervised learning \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04761)\]. These findings suggest that LLMs possess strong zero-shot reasoning capabilities that can be harnessed through prompt engineering and integration with external systems. Furthermore, the development of specialized training methods, such as Reinforcement Learning from Evol-Instruct Feedback (RLEIF), has further enhanced mathematical reasoning abilities of LLMs, demonstrating their potential to excel in complex reasoning tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09583)\]. Overall, these studies underscore the versatility and effectiveness of in-context learning strategies in unlocking the broader cognitive capabilities of LLMs.

### <a id='category-evaluation-bias-and-fairness-studies'></a>Evaluation Bias and Fairness Studies: 4 papers found.
The category "Evaluation Bias and Fairness Studies" explores critical issues in the assessment of large language models (LLMs), focusing on biases and fairness in evaluation methodologies. Existing research highlights that traditional benchmarks, such as GLUE, often favor models that can generalize across tasks and domains, yet they may not fully capture the nuanced understanding required for real-world applications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.07461)\]. Additionally, studies have uncovered systematic biases in evaluation paradigms where LLMs are used as evaluators, revealing that the order of context can manipulate evaluation outcomes, leading to skewed results \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. For instance, altering the order of responses can make one model appear superior, even when the actual quality is comparable. To address these issues, researchers propose calibration frameworks that incorporate multiple evidence generation, balanced position aggregation, and human-in-the-loop mechanisms to ensure more reliable and fair assessments \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. Furthermore, there is growing interest in evaluating LLMs at a more granular level, such as character-level understanding, which reveals that many models struggle with basic linguistic tasks that humans handle effortlessly \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11357)\]. These findings underscore the need for more robust and equitable evaluation methods that align with human judgment and account for potential biases in both data and model behavior.
