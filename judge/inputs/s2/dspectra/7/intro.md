## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to perform complex reasoning tasks, often enhanced through techniques like chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916)\]. However, identifying specific behaviors such as refusal within LLMs remains a significant challenge, as existing methods often rely on predefined templates or manual analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This limitation motivates the development of automated frameworks for steering LLMs toward desired behaviors, such as safer or more aligned outputs.

Recent work has explored the use of alignment techniques to improve the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. For instance, Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) introduces a constraint alignment loss that ensures positive responses outperform negative ones while keeping negative scores within a reasonable range \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where models are expected to provide reliable reasoning, such as in mathematical reasoning tasks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09583)\]. Similarly, the ReAct framework synergizes reasoning and acting by allowing models to interact with external sources, such as knowledge bases, to gather additional information \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)\]. This interaction helps mitigate issues like hallucination and error propagation, which are common in purely language-based reasoning approaches.

The importance of in-context learning has also been emphasized, as LLMs can utilize extensive background knowledge and task information without requiring explicit training data \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.18223)\]. This capability is further extended in multimodal settings, where models like MMICL enhance vision-language understanding by efficiently handling complex multi-modal inputs \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07915)\]. These models leverage in-context learning to adapt to diverse tasks, demonstrating the versatility of LLMs in cross-modal reasoning.

Moreover, the evaluation of LLMs has raised concerns about fairness and bias. Studies have shown that the evaluation of LLMs as referees can be systematically biased, with results influenced by the order of responses in the context \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\]. To address this, calibration frameworks have been proposed, including Multiple Evidence Calibration and Balanced Position Calibration, which aim to reduce evaluation bias and improve alignment with human judgments \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)\].

In addition, the role of pre-training and fine-tuning in enhancing LLM capabilities has been widely explored. For example, the LXMERT framework pre-trains on image-and-sentence pairs to learn cross-modal relationships, achieving state-of-the-art results on visual question answering tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.07490)\]. Similarly, ViLBERT extends BERT to a multi-modal model, enabling joint representations of image content and natural language \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.02265)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of pre-training in developing robust and transferable representations across modalities.
