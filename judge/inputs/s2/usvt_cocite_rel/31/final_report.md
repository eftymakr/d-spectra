## Related Works

Unsupervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning general-purpose representations across various domains, including natural language processing and computer vision \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. However, its application to DNA pre-training has been limited, primarily due to the lack of sufficient information in pure DNA sequences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. This limitation is attributed to the fact that DNA functions are regulated by genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility, which are not captured by sequence alone \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. As a result, researchers have explored alternative approaches to improve DNA representation learning.

Several studies have investigated unsupervised learning methods for visual and textual representations, such as Contrastive Predictive Coding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\], which leverages autoregressive models to predict future samples in latent space \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Similarly, unsupervised visual representation learning has been explored through context prediction tasks, where models learn to recognize objects and their parts by predicting spatial relationships between image patches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of unsupervised learning to capture meaningful features from unlabeled data.

In the context of language modeling, pre-training methods like BERT \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] and RoBERTa \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\] have achieved significant performance gains by leveraging large-scale corpora. These models use masked language modeling to learn contextual representations, which are then fine-tuned for downstream tasks. However, these methods primarily focus on language understanding and do not directly address the challenges of DNA representation learning.

Recent work on unsupervised feature learning has also explored instance discrimination and non-parametric methods to learn representations that capture apparent similarity among instances \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\]. These methods have shown promise in improving performance on tasks such as image classification and object detection \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01978)\]. Additionally, contrastive learning has been applied to visual representations, with frameworks like SimCLR \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\] achieving state-of-the-art results by maximizing the similarity between augmented views of the same image \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\].

For genomic profile prediction, supervised training has been proposed as an alternative to pure sequence pre-training. This approach leverages the relationships between DNA sequences and genomic profiles to learn more effective representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. By capturing the complex relationships between DNA sequences and genomic profiles, SPACE achieves state-of-the-art performance on various tasks.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-and-context-prediction-methods'></a>Contrastive Learning and Context Prediction Methods: 6 papers found.
Contrastive learning and context prediction methods have been widely explored for unsupervised representation learning, with various approaches aiming to leverage unlabeled data effectively. Early work focused on using spatial context within images to train rich visual representations, such as predicting the relative position of image patches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. This approach demonstrated the ability to capture visual similarity across images and improve object recognition \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05192)\]. Similarly, Contrastive Predictive Coding (CPC) introduced a probabilistic contrastive loss to learn latent representations that are useful for predicting future samples, showing strong performance across multiple domains like speech, images, and reinforcement learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Later advancements, such as SimCLR, simplified contrastive learning by using data augmentations and learnable transformations, achieving state-of-the-art results on ImageNet with self-supervised representations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. Prototypical Contrastive Learning (PCL) further improved representation learning by introducing prototypes to encode semantic structures, leading to better performance in low-resource transfer tasks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04966)\]. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) addressed the challenge of maintaining a large and consistent dictionary for contrastive learning, enabling effective transfer to downstream tasks like object detection \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722)\]. These methods collectively highlight the importance of leveraging context and contrastive mechanisms to learn robust and transferable representations, setting the foundation for more advanced unsupervised and semi-supervised learning strategies.

For instance, BERT was found to be significantly undertrained, and with proper training, it could match or exceed the performance of later models \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. Similarly, methods like SpanBERT improve upon BERT by masking contiguous spans of text and training the model to predict the entire content of masked spans \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10529)\]. Other approaches, such as the context-free network (CFN), use jigsaw puzzles to pre-train features for object detection and classification, achieving strong results on benchmarks like Pascal VOC \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09246)\]. These paradigms highlight the importance of thoughtful design choices and hyperparameter tuning in achieving effective pre-training, as demonstrated in replication studies of BERT \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. The success of these methods has also inspired applications in other domains, such as DNA pre-training, where supervised training for genomic profile prediction has been shown to yield better results than pure sequence pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)\]. Overall, these works emphasize the potential of unsupervised pre-training to enhance performance across a variety of tasks, while also highlighting the challenges and considerations involved in their implementation.
