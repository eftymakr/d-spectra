## Related Works

Calibration of machine learning models has been extensively studied, particularly in the context of deep neural networks. A key challenge is ensuring that predicted probabilities accurately reflect the true likelihood of correct predictions. Early work on calibration focused on simple models, but modern neural networks often exhibit poor calibration \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue is exacerbated by factors such as depth, width, and Batch Normalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. To address this, temperature scaling—a single-parameter variant of Platt Scaling—has been shown to be surprisingly effective in calibrating predictions across various architectures and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\].

Recent efforts have sought to improve calibration by introducing more flexible methods. For instance, Bayesian methods, such as Bayesian Neural Networks, provide a principled way to estimate uncertainty but are computationally expensive \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142)\]. In contrast, non-Bayesian approaches like Deep Ensembles have been proposed as a simpler alternative that is both scalable and effective \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. These methods produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates that are competitive with Bayesian approaches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. However, they often lack the flexibility to capture complex calibration behaviors, especially in multiclass settings \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\].

The need for more expressive calibration methods has led to the development of techniques such as Dirichlet calibration, which generalizes beta calibration to multiclass problems \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\]. This method is particularly useful for neural networks, as it can be implemented with a simple transformation of the output probabilities \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\]. Similarly, histogram binning has been used to evaluate calibration, although it is sample inefficient compared to scaling methods \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\]. To address this, the scaling-binning calibrator was introduced, combining the strengths of both approaches \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10155)\].

Another line of research has focused on the limitations of post-hoc calibration methods. For example, existing methods often fail to account for field-level calibration errors, which measure bias in predictions over specific subsets of data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10713)\]. This has motivated the development of Neural Calibration, a method that leverages field-aware information to improve calibration accuracy \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10713)\]. Furthermore, the use of mixup training has been shown to improve calibration by introducing label smoothing, which reduces overconfidence in predictions \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\].

In addition to post-hoc methods, some work has explored integrating calibration during training. For instance, the use of focal loss has been shown to produce well-calibrated models without compromising accuracy \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\].
