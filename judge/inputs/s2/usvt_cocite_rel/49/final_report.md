## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the domain of Web search. These models leverage dense representations to efficiently retrieve relevant documents, often outperforming traditional sparse retrieval methods \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. However, training these models typically requires substantial amounts of labeled query-document pairs, which are expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. To address this, synthetic queries generated by large language models have emerged as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Despite this, the quality of synthetic queries can vary, leading to suboptimal retrieval performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. 

Existing approaches often rely on external re-rankers to filter out noisy query-document pairs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, our work proposes a framework that integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715)\]. This approach aims to optimize the model to generate high-quality queries that maximize downstream retrieval effectiveness. DPO has been shown to improve alignment with human preferences and outperform traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) methods in several aspects \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715)\]. 

The use of DPO in retrieval tasks is not new. It has been applied to fine-tune language models to align with human preferences, demonstrating stability and simplicity compared to methods like PPO \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715)\]. Additionally, DPO has been used to improve the sentiment control of generated text and enhance response quality in summarization and dialogue tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. These results suggest that DPO can be effectively applied to retrieval tasks by integrating ranking signals into the query generation process.

However, PPO is sensitive to hyperparameters and requires multiple models, making it complex to implement and scale \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05302)\]. ### <a id='category-reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback-(rlhf)-methods'></a>Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) Methods: 7 papers found.
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a critical approach for aligning large language models with human preferences, enhancing their performance in tasks such as summarization, dialogue, and content generation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. This method typically involves training a reward model based on human preferences and using it to fine-tune a policy via reinforcement learning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. However, RLHF relies on two key approximations: substituting pairwise preferences with pointwise rewards and assuming reward models generalize to out-of-distribution data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. To address these limitations, Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) was introduced, which bypasses the reward modeling stage by directly learning a policy from pairwise preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. Despite this improvement, DPO still depends on the first approximation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. Recent work has proposed $\Psi$PO, a new general objective that bypasses both approximations by using pairwise preferences directly \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. Additionally, methods like RRHF have been developed to simplify the alignment process by using ranking loss and reducing the number of required models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. These approaches highlight ongoing efforts to improve the efficiency, robustness, and alignment of language models with human preferences through RLHF \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\].
