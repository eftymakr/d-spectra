## Related Works

Neural retrieval methods using transformer-based pre-trained language models have significantly advanced multilingual and cross-lingual retrieval \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. However, their effectiveness for low-resource, morphologically rich languages such as Amharic remains underexplored due to data scarcity and suboptimal tokenization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12832)\]. This challenge is not unique to Amharic; similar issues have been observed in other low-resource languages, where multilingual models like mBERT often underperform compared to monolingual models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09093)\]. For instance, mBERT has been shown to perform poorly on low-resource languages such as Swahili and Urdu, where it only achieves a 15.7% improvement over previous XLM models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09093)\]. These findings highlight the need for language-specific adaptations to improve retrieval performance in such settings.

Several works have explored the use of multilingual pre-trained models for cross-lingual retrieval. XLM-R, a multilingual masked language model trained on one hundred languages, has shown significant improvements over mBERT on cross-lingual benchmarks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. However, the performance of these models is still limited in low-resource languages due to the lack of sufficient training data and the challenges of cross-lingual alignment \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02116)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed various approaches, such as ColBERT-X, which uses the XLM-R encoder to support cross-language information retrieval \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\]. This model can be trained in two ways: zero-shot training using the English MS MARCO collection or translate-train using machine translations of the associated passages \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08471)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of multilingual models for cross-lingual retrieval but also highlight the limitations of relying solely on multilingual models without language-specific fine-tuning.

In addition to multilingual models, there has been significant progress in dense retrieval methods. Dense retrieval models, such as those based on BERT, have shown superior performance over traditional sparse retrieval methods like BM25 \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. However, dense retrieval models often require large amounts of training data and are not well-suited for low-resource settings \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.04906)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed methods like ANCE, which uses approximate nearest neighbor negative contrastive learning to improve the training of dense retrievers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. This approach has been shown to outperform both dense and sparse retrieval baselines, achieving nearly the same accuracy as sparse-retrieval-and-BERT-reranking \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of dense retrieval in low-resource settings but also highlight the need for more efficient training strategies.
