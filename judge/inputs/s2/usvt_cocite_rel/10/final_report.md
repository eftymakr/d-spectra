## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) in the training of language models. While NTP, which predicts one token at a time, remains the standard approach, recent research has shown that predicting multiple tokens simultaneously can lead to improved efficiency and performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach involves using multiple independent output heads to predict $k$ future tokens at each step, with the model trunk shared across all heads \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The benefits of MTP have been observed in both training and inference, particularly for large models, where it leads to higher sample efficiency and faster inference times \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) face challenges in adapting to the MTP objective, as they may struggle with the increased complexity of predicting multiple tokens at once \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

To address these challenges, several studies have explored curriculum learning strategies for MTP training. One approach involves a forward curriculum, which gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This strategy allows SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pre-training, leading to improved downstream NTP performance and generative output quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Another approach is a reverse curriculum, which starts with MTP and gradually reduces the number of tokens predicted, aiming to achieve stronger NTP performance and output quality \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, the reverse curriculum fails to provide the self-speculative decoding benefits observed in the forward curriculum \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

The limitations of NTP have also been extensively studied. For instance, teacher-forcing, a common training technique in NTP, can lead to shortcut learning, where the model relies on the revealed prefix to spuriously fit future tokens \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11143)\]. This can compromise the accuracy of the next-token predictor \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.11143)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed alternative training objectives, such as future n-gram prediction, which encourages the model to plan for future tokens and prevent overfitting on local correlations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\]. This approach has been successfully implemented in models like ProphetNet, which outperforms traditional sequence-to-sequence models on summarization and question generation tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\].

In addition to training objectives, inference efficiency has also been a focus of recent research. Techniques such as speculative decoding and Medusa, which introduce multiple decoding heads to predict multiple tokens in parallel, have shown significant speedups without compromising generation quality \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. These methods leverage parallel processing to reduce the number of decoding steps required, making them particularly useful for large models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. ### <a id='category-next-token-prediction-(ntp)-limitations'></a>Next-token Prediction (NTP) Limitations: 4 papers found.
The category "Next-token Prediction (NTP) Limitations" highlights critical challenges in using NTP as a training objective for language models. Research indicates that NTP, while foundational, can lead to significant issues during training and inference. For instance, teacher-forcing—a common training technique—can fail to learn accurate next-token predictors in certain tasks, even when the task is straightforward to learn \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. This failure can result in error compounding during autoregressive inference, undermining model reliability \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. Additionally, studies show that even advanced models like Transformers and Mamba struggle with specific planning tasks that require multi-token prediction, suggesting limitations in their ability to handle complex reasoning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. Furthermore, the reliance on outcome supervision, which provides feedback only for final results, is less effective than process supervision, which offers feedback for intermediate steps, in training models to solve complex problems \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050)\]. These findings underscore the need for alternative training strategies, such as teacherless training, which uses dummy tokens to predict multiple tokens in advance, potentially resolving some of the limitations of traditional NTP approaches \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. The exploration of transfer learning and multimodal NTP also reveals that while NTP has been successfully extended to various modalities, there remain challenges in tokenization, model architecture, and evaluation \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. Overall, these works collectively emphasize the need for more robust training methodologies and a deeper understanding of how NTP can be adapted to improve model performance across diverse tasks.

### <a id='category-inference-speed-optimization-methods'></a>Inference Speed Optimization Methods: 2 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Large Language Models (LLMs) employ auto-regressive decoding that requires sequential computation, with each step reliant on the previous one's output. This creates a bottleneck as each step necessitates moving the full model parameters from High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) to the accelerator's cache. While methods such as speculative decoding have been suggested to address this issue, their implementation is impeded by the challenges associated with acquiring and maintaining a separate draft model. In this paper, we present Medusa, an efficient method that augments LLM inference by adding extra decoding heads to predict multiple subsequent tokens in parallel. Using a tree-based attention mechanism, Medusa constructs multiple candidate continuations and verifies them simultaneously in each decoding step. By leveraging parallel processing, Medusa substantially reduces the number of decoding steps required. We present two levels of fine-tuning procedures for Medusa to meet the needs of different use cases: Medusa-1: Medusa is directly fine-tuned on top of a frozen backbone LLM, enabling lossless inference acceleration. Medusa-2: Medusa is fine-tuned together with the backbone LLM, enabling better prediction accuracy of Medusa heads and higher speedup but needing a special training recipe that preserves the backbone model's capabilities. Moreover, we propose several extensions that improve or expand the utility of Medusa, including a self-distillation to handle situations where no training data is available and a typical acceptance scheme to boost the acceptance rate while maintaining generation quality. We evaluate Medusa on models of various sizes and training procedures. Our experiments demonstrate that Medusa-1 can achieve over 2.2x speedup without compromising generation quality, while Medusa-2 further improves the speedup to 2.3-3.6x.»
