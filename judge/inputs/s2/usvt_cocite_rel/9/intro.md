## Related Works

The detection and segmentation of paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs) in multiple sclerosis (MS) have been the focus of significant research, particularly due to their association with disease progression and disability \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Traditional manual segmentation methods are time-consuming and subject to inter- and intra-observer variability \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/71b5e8d9970f3b725127eb7da3d5183b921ad017)\]. Automated methods have been proposed to address these limitations, leveraging machine learning and deep learning techniques \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]\[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]\[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/691751bfa44017d8041e17409411f9e9ffc3dec6)\]. 

Several studies have focused on the identification of PRLs using susceptibility-based MRI, such as quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), which provides high contrast between rim and non-rim lesions \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/455e181fe505a36406af032700fea2ca87a2e9b9)\]. The presence of PRLs has been shown to correlate with increased disability and more aggressive disease progression \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb5b80dc4165d0ac3e28d1882b898633e84779c2)\]. Automated detection methods have been developed to classify lesions based on radiomic features extracted from T2*-phase images \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. These methods often face challenges due to the heterogeneity of lesion appearance and the imbalance between rim and non-rim lesions \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. 

To address class imbalance, some studies have employed synthetic data generation techniques. For instance, QSMRim-Net uses a synthetic minority oversampling technique to improve classification performance \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/45bf918158d3f779cb41b982fa9237682c11dd96)\]. Similarly, the paper under review introduces a generative adversarial network (GAN) to create synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps of PRLs, which are then used to improve classifier performance \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. This approach allows for the generation of synthetic data that approximates the unseen distribution of PRLs, thereby enhancing detection accuracy \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/09b2a6040c15e8672169531fc76bba0b5a04296b)\]. 

In addition to synthetic data, methods have been proposed to improve segmentation accuracy by incorporating multi-channel information. For example, the use of multi-contrast MRI data has been shown to enhance lesion detection and segmentation \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05135)\]. The integration of anatomical information into the segmentation process has also been explored, with methods such as ALL-Net demonstrating improved performance by incorporating anatomical coordinate information \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ebff27a8db308b40ebb43fe974403369d56685ef)\]. 

Deep learning models have been extensively applied for MS lesion segmentation, with architectures such as U-Net and its variants being widely used \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2f966a1741075c2550f14406c88fc69b0a0dfe22)\]. These models have been trained on multi-modal MRI data, including T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR sequences, to improve segmentation accuracy \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10029)\]. The use of 3D convolutional neural networks (CNNs) has also been explored for longitudinal lesion segmentation, enabling the simultaneous processing of baseline and follow-up scans \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/dbfd59111d676f28fbba4131cc34b13084455e13)\].
