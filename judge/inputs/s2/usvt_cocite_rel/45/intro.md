## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has emerged as a critical area in sentiment analysis, particularly in the context of user-generated content such as app reviews. While traditional sentiment analysis often focuses on polarity (positive, negative, neutral) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\], recent work has emphasized the need for more nuanced emotion detection to better capture user sentiment. This has led to the development of large-scale annotated datasets and advanced machine learning models for emotion recognition \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\].

The GoEmotions dataset, for instance, represents a significant advancement in this field, offering 58,000 manually annotated Reddit comments labeled for 27 emotion categories \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. This dataset has been used to evaluate the performance of BERT-based models, achieving an average F1-score of 0.46 across the emotion taxonomy \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\]. However, the complexity of fine-grained emotion detection remains a challenge, as demonstrated by the limited performance of models on more detailed emotion categories \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6a6f1b8ef3eefaf8027a76134859b44886a5f795)\].

Similarly, the SemEval-2018 Task 1: Affect in Tweets \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66c8ab0b3b7698cf5333c3f7350a6eaa5302294b)\] highlights the importance of emotion classification in social media text, with subtasks including emotion intensity regression and valence classification. This task involved multiple languages and demonstrated the variability in how affect is expressed across different platforms and cultures. The dataset and results from this task have contributed to the broader understanding of affective text analysis \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/66c8ab0b3b7698cf5333c3f7350a6eaa5302294b)\].

The development of emotion lexicons has also played a crucial role in improving emotion detection. The NRC VAD Lexicon, for example, provides human-rated valence, arousal, and dominance scores for over 20,000 English words \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/502c1e8bba1578241c354a54926ca913d5fa9c8a)\]. This resource has been used to enhance the understanding of emotional content in text and has influenced the design of emotion classification models. However, the reliance on subjective annotations has led to variability in how emotions are interpreted \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/502c1e8bba1578241c354a54926ca913d5fa9c8a)\].

Crowdsourcing has been proposed as a method to generate large, high-quality emotion lexicons \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. This approach has been applied to create word–emotion and word–polarity association lexicons, with experiments showing that asking if a term is associated with an emotion leads to higher interannotator agreement than asking if it evokes an emotion \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\]. Despite these efforts, challenges remain in ensuring consistency and accuracy in emotion annotation, particularly when dealing with complex or mixed emotional responses \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\].

Recent work on emotion detection in poetry \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07723)\] has further expanded the scope of emotion analysis, focusing on aesthetic emotions elicited in the reader rather than what is expressed in the text
