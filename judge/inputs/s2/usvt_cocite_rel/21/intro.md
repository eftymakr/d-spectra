## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images, leveraging implicit representations to capture both geometry and appearance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]. These methods typically take multiple views as input and optimize the scene representation by minimizing the uncertainty across views \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often limited by the number of input views, especially in complex or dynamic scenes where certain angles are not captured \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1603.06041)\]. To address this limitation, researchers have explored data augmentation techniques, such as video frame interpolation, to generate additional views and improve the robustness of the reconstruction process \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\].

Video frame interpolation has been widely studied, with early methods relying on motion estimation and pixel synthesis as separate steps \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. More recent approaches have integrated these steps into a single process, using deep neural networks to estimate spatially adaptive convolution kernels \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. For example, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\] proposed a method that combines motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a single convolutional process, allowing for high-quality video frame interpolation without requiring ground-truth optical flow data. This approach has been further refined with the use of separable convolutions, reducing the number of parameters needed to estimate motion and re-sampling \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.06825)\]. These methods have demonstrated effectiveness in handling occlusion, blur, and abrupt brightness changes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12052)\].

In the context of neural rendering, several works have explored the integration of video frame interpolation to augment training data. For instance, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\] proposed a method that uses deep convolutional neural networks to predict multiple intermediate frames, jointly modeling motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning. This approach has been shown to improve the quality of reconstructed scenes by providing additional views during training \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01618)\]. Similarly, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04532)\] introduced a method that uses deep voxel flow to synthesize new video frames by flowing pixel values from existing ones, without requiring human supervision \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02836)\].

Recent advances in implicit surface reconstruction have also contributed to the field of neural rendering. Methods such as NeuS \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.11215)\] and UNISURF \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02463)\] have shown promise in reconstructing high-quality surfaces from multi-view inputs, often without requiring explicit object masks \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00665)\]. These methods leverage implicit representations, such as signed distance functions (SDFs), to capture detailed geometry and appearance \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13597)\]. Additionally, \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08768)\] proposed a method that integrates normal priors into the neural rendering pipeline, improving the reconstruction of texture-less indoor scenes \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.13415)\].

In summary, the integration of video frame interpolation into neural rendering has shown significant potential for improving reconstruction quality
