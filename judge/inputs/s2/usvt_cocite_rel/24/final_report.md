## Related Works

Diffusion models have become a cornerstone in generative modeling, particularly in image and video generation, due to their ability to produce high-quality, diverse outputs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. However, the computational and memory demands of these models, especially diffusion transformers (DiTs), pose significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. Quantization has emerged as a promising solution to reduce storage and computational costs by lowering the bit-width of model parameters \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. Despite its potential, existing quantization methods for image generation models often fail to generalize effectively to video generation tasks, which introduce additional complexity due to temporal dependencies \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\].

Several works have explored post-training quantization (PTQ) for diffusion models. Q-Diffusion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\] introduced a novel PTQ method tailored to the multi-timestep pipeline of diffusion models, achieving 4-bit quantization with minimal performance degradation. Similarly, PTQ4DM \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\] focused on accelerating diffusion models by compressing the noise estimation network, achieving 8-bit quantization while maintaining performance. These methods, however, primarily target image generation and do not address the unique challenges of video generation, such as temporal consistency and spatiotemporal correlations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\].

For video generation, the problem is further compounded by the need to maintain temporal coherence across frames, which is not typically addressed in image-based quantization techniques \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Temporal Dynamic Quantization \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\] introduced a method that dynamically adjusts quantization intervals based on time step information, improving output quality without increasing inference overhead. This approach is compatible with both PTQ and quantization-aware training (QAT), but it does not specifically address the challenges of video DiT models.

Recent works have also explored quantization for diffusion transformers, such as DiT-XL/2, which are used in video generation \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. Q-DiT \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03458)\] proposed a method that handles the spatial and temporal variability in DiT models by introducing automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization. This method achieved significant improvements in FID scores when quantizing DiT-XL/2 to W6A8 and W4A8. Similarly, ViDiT-Q \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\] was designed for DiT models, achieving W8A8 and W4A8 quantization with minimal degradation in visual quality and metrics.

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-generative-modeling'></a>Diffusion Models in Generative Modeling: 7 papers found.
Diffusion models have seen significant advancements in generative modeling, particularly in image and video generation, with recent work focusing on improving efficiency and performance. Latent diffusion models (LDMs) have been introduced to reduce computational costs by operating in the latent space of pretrained autoencoders, enabling high-resolution synthesis with lower resource requirements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. This approach has been further enhanced by incorporating cross-attention layers, allowing for flexible conditioning on inputs such as text or bounding boxes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Additionally, diffusion transformers (DiTs), which replace the U-Net backbone with a transformer architecture, have demonstrated superior scalability and performance on image generation tasks, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmarks like ImageNet \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. For video generation, Latte and MagicVideo have extended these ideas, using transformer-based architectures to model spatio-temporal patterns in the latent space, achieving high-quality video synthesis with reduced computational overhead \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]. However, challenges remain in quantizing video diffusion models due to the loss of information and misalignment of optimization objectives \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. To address these, Q-VDiT introduces token-aware quantization and temporal maintenance distillation, improving scene consistency and outperforming existing methods \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance the efficiency and applicability of diffusion models across various modalities.

### <a id='category-quantization-techniques-for-diffusion-transformers'></a>Quantization Techniques for Diffusion Transformers: 3 papers found.
Recent advancements in diffusion models, particularly the shift from UNet-based architectures to Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), have significantly enhanced the quality and scalability of image and video generation \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. However, the computational costs associated with these large-scale models remain a major barrier to their deployment on edge devices \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising solution to reduce memory usage and computational complexity by lowering the bit-width of model parameters \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Despite its potential, existing PTQ frameworks, primarily designed for traditional diffusion models, suffer from biased quantization, leading to performance degradation when applied to DiTs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. This issue is further compounded by the unique characteristics of DiTs, such as significant spatial and temporal variance in weights and activations \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed specialized quantization techniques, such as Q-DiT, which integrates automatic quantization granularity allocation and sample-wise dynamic activation quantization to handle the inherent variability of DiTs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. Similarly, ViDiT-Q was developed to tackle the specific challenges of quantizing DiTs for text-to-image and video generation tasks, achieving W8A8 and W4A8 quantization with minimal visual quality degradation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]. Another approach, PTQ4DiT, introduces methods like Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) to mitigate quantization errors caused by extreme channel magnitudes and temporal activation variations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. These efforts highlight the growing interest in developing tailored quantization strategies for DiTs, aiming to balance efficiency with the high fidelity required for real-world applications \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02540)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\].

Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «While neural networks have advanced the frontiers in many applications, they often come at a high computational cost. Reducing the power and latency of neural network inference is key if we want to integrate modern networks into edge devices with strict power and compute requirements. Neural network quantization is one of the most effective ways of achieving these savings but the additional noise it induces can lead to accuracy degradation. In this white paper, we introduce state-of-the-art algorithms for mitigating the impact of quantization noise on the network's performance while maintaining low-bit weights and activations. We start with a hardware motivated introduction to quantization and then consider two main classes of algorithms: Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) and Quantization-Aware-Training (QAT). PTQ requires no re-training or labelled data and is thus a lightweight push-button approach to quantization. In most cases, PTQ is sufficient for achieving 8-bit quantization with close to floating-point accuracy. QAT requires fine-tuning and access to labeled training data but enables lower bit quantization with competitive results. For both solutions, we provide tested pipelines based on existing literature and extensive experimentation that lead to state-of-the-art performance for common deep learning models and tasks.»
