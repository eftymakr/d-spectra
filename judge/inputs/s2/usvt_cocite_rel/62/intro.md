## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural network models that directly generate document identifiers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach leverages large language models to perform retrieval by generating identifiers, offering potential improvements in efficiency and integration with generative models \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Earlier works, such as the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), demonstrated that a single Transformer model can encode all corpus information, enabling direct query-to-docid mapping \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This idea was further extended with models like SEAL, which uses autoregressive generation of ngrams as document identifiers, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the KILT benchmark \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, CorpusBrain introduced a pre-trained generative retrieval model that eliminates the need for external indexes, encoding all corpus information within the model's parameters \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\].

The effectiveness of generative retrieval has also been explored in the context of large-scale corpora. For example, the RIPOR framework was designed to optimize generative retrieval for large-scale benchmarks, achieving significant improvements in MRR on the MS MARCO dataset \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. This work emphasized the importance of ranking optimization during sequential document ID generation. Additionally, the GenRet method proposed a document tokenization learning approach to define semantic docids, improving retrieval performance on datasets like NQ320K \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These studies highlight the growing interest in generative retrieval as a viable alternative to traditional retrieval pipelines.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain, particularly in scaling generative retrieval to large corpora. The paper "How Does Generative Retrieval Scale to Millions of Passages?" explored this issue, revealing that existing methods struggle with performance when applied to corpora with millions of documents \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. The study also found that synthetic queries can be useful during indexing, but many architectural modifications fail to account for computational costs. Furthermore, the paper "Continual Learning for Generative Retrieval over Dynamic Corpora" addressed the need for continual learning in dynamic environments, proposing a model that can incrementally index new documents while preserving retrieval accuracy \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the learning-to-rank aspect of generative retrieval. The LTRGR framework introduced a learning-to-rank approach that optimizes autoregressive models toward the final passage ranking target, achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. Similarly, the PAG model introduced simultaneous decoding to guide autoregressive generation, achieving significant improvements in MRR on the MS MARCO dataset \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\].
