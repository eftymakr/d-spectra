## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising paradigm in information retrieval, shifting from traditional index-based methods to end-to-end neural network models that directly generate document identifiers \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This approach leverages large language models to perform retrieval by generating identifiers, offering potential improvements in efficiency and integration with generative models \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. Earlier works, such as the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), demonstrated that a single Transformer model can encode all corpus information, enabling direct query-to-docid mapping \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. This idea was further extended with models like SEAL, which uses autoregressive generation of ngrams as document identifiers, achieving state-of-the-art performance on the KILT benchmark \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, CorpusBrain introduced a pre-trained generative retrieval model that eliminates the need for external indexes, encoding all corpus information within the model's parameters \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\].

The effectiveness of generative retrieval has also been explored in the context of large-scale corpora. For example, the RIPOR framework was designed to optimize generative retrieval for large-scale benchmarks, achieving significant improvements in MRR on the MS MARCO dataset \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. This work emphasized the importance of ranking optimization during sequential document ID generation. Additionally, the GenRet method proposed a document tokenization learning approach to define semantic docids, improving retrieval performance on datasets like NQ320K \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These studies highlight the growing interest in generative retrieval as a viable alternative to traditional retrieval pipelines.

Despite these advancements, challenges remain, particularly in scaling generative retrieval to large corpora. The paper "How Does Generative Retrieval Scale to Millions of Passages?" explored this issue, revealing that existing methods struggle with performance when applied to corpora with millions of documents \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. The study also found that synthetic queries can be useful during indexing, but many architectural modifications fail to account for computational costs. Furthermore, the paper "Continual Learning for Generative Retrieval over Dynamic Corpora" addressed the need for continual learning in dynamic environments, proposing a model that can incrementally index new documents while preserving retrieval accuracy \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14968)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the learning-to-rank aspect of generative retrieval. The LTRGR framework introduced a learning-to-rank approach that optimizes autoregressive models toward the final passage ranking target, achieving state-of-the-art performance on multiple benchmarks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. Similarly, the PAG model introduced simultaneous decoding to guide autoregressive generation, achieving significant improvements in MRR on the MS MARCO dataset \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\]. ### <a id='category-efficiency-and-integration-with-llms'></a>Efficiency and Integration with LLMs: 6 papers found.
Efficiency and Integration with LLMs is a critical area in generative retrieval research, focusing on how to effectively leverage large language models (LLMs) while maintaining computational efficiency. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges. For instance, SEAL utilizes autoregressive models to generate and score distinctive ngrams from passages, mapping them to full documents through an efficient data structure, achieving significant improvements over traditional retrieval systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10628)\]. Similarly, RIPOR introduces a novel prefix-oriented ranking optimization algorithm and constructs document IDs based on relevance associations, leading to substantial performance gains on large-scale benchmarks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. PAG further enhances efficiency by employing simultaneous decoding to guide autoregressive generation of document identifiers, achieving faster query latency and better performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14600)\]. The Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) proposes an end-to-end sequence-to-sequence network that generates document identifiers directly for queries, incorporating a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder and semantic document identifiers to improve recall \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.02743)\]. Additionally, Ultron aims to unify indexing and retrieval stages by encoding document knowledge into the model, using semantically rich docids and a three-stage training workflow to enhance retrieval accuracy \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09257)\]. These methods collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to integrate generative retrieval with LLMs while optimizing for efficiency and performance.

### <a id='category-generative-retrieval-paradigm-overview'></a>Generative Retrieval Paradigm Overview: 3 papers found.
The generative retrieval paradigm represents a significant shift from traditional search methodologies by leveraging large-scale pre-trained generative models to directly generate document identifiers during retrieval \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. This approach eliminates the need for explicit indexing and ranking stages, enabling end-to-end optimization and improved efficiency \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. Unlike conventional methods that rely on index-retrieve-then-rank pipelines, generative retrieval models, such as CorpusBrain, encode all corpus information within their parameters, allowing for seamless integration with generative language models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. This paradigm also addresses limitations in traditional retrieval by enabling more flexible and context-aware document retrieval \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. However, challenges remain, such as the learning gap between autoregressive models and passage ranking objectives, which has led to the development of frameworks like LTRGR that incorporate learning-to-rank strategies to improve performance \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. Additionally, generative retrieval has shown promise in entity retrieval tasks, where models like GENRE generate entity names token-by-token, enabling more accurate and memory-efficient entity disambiguation and linking \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09134)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in generative retrieval for knowledge-intensive tasks, particularly in scenarios requiring zero- or low-resource settings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\].

### <a id='category-limitations-and-future-directions'></a>Limitations and Future Directions: 2 papers found.
The category "Limitations and Future Directions" highlights critical challenges and potential improvements in generative retrieval systems. Existing studies show that while generative retrieval offers promising alternatives to traditional indexing methods by integrating retrieval and generation into a single model \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\], scaling these systems to large corpora remains a significant challenge. For instance, prior work has demonstrated that generative retrieval techniques, when applied to corpora of up to 100k documents, can be competitive with state-of-the-art dual encoders \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. However, when scaling to millions of passages, such as in the MS MARCO passage ranking task with 8.8M passages, performance degrades, and existing architectural modifications fail to account for computational constraints \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Additionally, the mismatch between the long documents indexed and the short queries used during retrieval exacerbates performance issues, particularly in cross-lingual settings \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To address this, recent approaches like DSI-QG propose using synthetic queries generated by a query generation model during indexing, which allows the model to better align with retrieval queries \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. These findings underscore the need for more robust methods that can effectively handle large-scale retrieval while maintaining performance and efficiency. Future research should focus on improving the generalization capabilities of generative retrieval models, particularly in out-of-distribution scenarios, and explore novel ways to bridge the gap between indexing and retrieval processes \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\].

### <a id='category-generalization-on-out-of-distribution-data'></a>Generalization on Out-of-Distribution Data: 2 papers found.
Generalization on out-of-distribution data has been a focal point in generative retrieval research, with recent works exploring how models can maintain performance when faced with unseen data. For instance, GenRet introduces a discrete auto-encoding framework to learn semantic document identifiers (docids) that enable end-to-end retrieval, demonstrating strong generalization on datasets like NQ320K \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. Similarly, the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) approach leverages a single Transformer model to encode all corpus information into its parameters, allowing direct query-to-docid mapping without reliance on traditional indexes \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.06991)\]. These methods highlight the importance of designing retrieval systems that can adapt to new data distributions. Both GenRet and DSI emphasize the need for flexible and robust representations, as fixed rule-based docids or simplistic retrieval pipelines often fail to capture the full semantic context of documents \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. The ability to generalize across unseen data is further supported by the progressive training schemes and autoregressive modeling strategies employed in these approaches, which aim to stabilize training and improve the model's capacity to handle diverse query scenarios \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04171)\]. These findings underscore the ongoing efforts to enhance the generalization capabilities of generative retrieval systems, particularly in real-world settings where data distributions may shift over time.
