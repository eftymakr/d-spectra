## Related Works

Fairness in machine learning has been extensively studied, with a focus on mitigating biases in model outputs. Early work introduced statistical fairness definitions, such as parity of false positive rates across groups \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. These definitions, however, are vulnerable to "fairness gerrymandering," where a classifier appears fair on individual groups but fails on structured subgroups \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. To address this, researchers proposed subgroup fairness, requiring statistical parity across exponentially many subgroups defined by structured functions of protected attributes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\]. This approach, while more robust, introduces computational challenges, as auditing subgroup fairness is equivalent to weak agnostic learning, which is computationally hard \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05144)\].

Recent efforts have focused on benchmarking fairness in large language models (LLMs), which have become central to many NLP applications. The Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM) initiative introduced a comprehensive framework to evaluate LMs across 16 core scenarios and 7 metrics, improving transparency and standardization \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ce913026f693101e54d3ab9152e107034d81fce1)\]. However, existing benchmarks often lack clarity in defining what is being measured, and they may fail to capture nuanced forms of stereotyping \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3e65f572322e192fe36ae52a8a7f025b0685dfc6)\]. For instance, the CrowS-Pairs dataset evaluates social biases in masked language models by presenting pairs of stereotypical and non-stereotypical sentences, revealing that models consistently favor stereotypical content \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00133)\]. Similarly, the BBQ benchmark highlights how biases manifest in question-answering tasks, showing that models often rely on stereotypes when context is under-informative \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08193)\].

Intrinsic and extrinsic fairness metrics have also been explored. Intrinsic metrics evaluate bias in language representations, while extrinsic metrics assess fairness in downstream tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13928)\]. However, these metrics do not reliably correlate, even after accounting for confounding factors \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15859)\]. This suggests that intrinsic metrics may not be sufficient for guiding debiasing efforts, and extrinsic measures should be prioritized \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15859)\]. The HONEST benchmark further demonstrates how language models can reproduce harmful stereotypes, such as associating female targets with sexual promiscuity or male targets with homosexuality \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4f76d26369ee573cb03db4b9f6e4ab5d61806095)\]. These findings underscore the need for more nuanced fairness evaluations that go beyond simple parity metrics.

The concept of counterfactual fairness has also been introduced, using causal inference to ensure that decisions remain consistent across different demographic groups \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.06856)\]. This framework aims to prevent models from making decisions that would change if an individual belonged to a different group. 	Document 2: [Snippet]: «Natural Language Processing (NLP) systems learn harmful societal biases that cause them to amplify inequality as they are deployed in more and more situations. To guide efforts at debiasing these systems, the NLP community relies on a variety of metrics that quantify bias in models. Some of these metrics are intrinsic, measuring bias in word embedding spaces, and some are extrinsic, measuring bias in downstream tasks that the word embeddings enable. Do these intrinsic and extrinsic metrics correlate with each other? We compare intrinsic and extrinsic metrics across hundreds of trained models covering different tasks and experimental conditions. Our results show no reliable correlation between these metrics that holds in all scenarios across tasks and languages. We urge researchers working on debiasing to focus on extrinsic measures of bias, and to make using these measures more feasible via creation of new challenge sets and annotated test data.
