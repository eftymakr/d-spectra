## Related Works

The field of large language model (LLM) training has seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the areas of instruction tuning, fine-tuning, and performance modeling. Instruction tuning has emerged as a critical technique for improving the zero-shot and few-shot learning capabilities of LLMs. For instance, instruction tuning involves training models on a collection of tasks described via natural language instructions, which significantly improves their performance on unseen tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. This approach has been successfully applied to a 137B parameter model, which outperformed zero-shot GPT-3 on 20 of 25 tasks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. Similarly, the WizardLM model was developed by evolving complex instructions using an LLM, resulting in superior instruction data compared to human-created ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. This highlights the potential of AI-evolved instruction data in enhancing LLMs.

Fine-tuning remains a cornerstone of improving LLM performance on specific tasks. Techniques such as QLoRA have enabled efficient fine-tuning of large models by using low-rank adapters and quantization, reducing memory usage and enabling training on consumer-grade hardware \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)\]. This approach has been used to finetune over 1,000 models, demonstrating the feasibility of efficient fine-tuning for a wide range of tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314)\]. Additionally, LLaMA and its variants have been trained on large-scale datasets, achieving competitive performance with proprietary models like GPT-3 and PaLM \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971)\]. The success of these models underscores the importance of large-scale training data and efficient training strategies.

Performance modeling and estimation are also critical for optimizing LLM training in distributed environments. Recent work has focused on developing accurate performance models that can predict execution behaviors and guide optimization efforts. The Lumos toolkit, introduced in this paper, is designed to capture and predict the execution behaviors of modern LLMs by replaying execution traces \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. This approach has been validated on a production ML cluster with up to 512 NVIDIA H100 GPUs, demonstrating its ability to accurately estimate performance across different models and configurations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. The success of instruction tuning is attributed to factors such as the number of finetuning datasets, model scale, and the use of natural language instructions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652)\]. These findings highlight the importance of instruction tuning in developing versatile and high-performing language models across various domains.

### <a id='category-model-scaling-and-sample-efficiency'></a>Model Scaling and Sample Efficiency: 5 papers found.
Model scaling and sample efficiency are critical areas of research in large language model (LLM) development, with multiple studies demonstrating the benefits of increasing model size and the challenges of achieving efficient few-shot learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. For instance, GPT-3, a 175 billion parameter model, shows significant improvements in task-agnostic, few-shot performance, often rivaling fine-tuning approaches \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12244)\]. However, even with such large models, there are limitations, particularly in tasks requiring multi-step mathematical reasoning, where current models struggle despite the simplicity of the problem distribution \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)\]. Similarly, the MATH dataset highlights that while large models can be trained on extensive mathematical problems, accuracy remains relatively low, and scaling alone may not be sufficient to achieve strong mathematical reasoning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05653)\]. Additionally, empirical scaling laws reveal that loss scales as a power-law with model size, dataset size, and compute used, with larger models being more sample-efficient \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165)\]. These findings underscore the importance of not only increasing model parameters but also exploring new algorithmic advancements to improve performance on complex tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05653)\]. Furthermore, the development of open-access models like BLOOM demonstrates the potential for democratizing access to powerful LLMs, although challenges remain in achieving consistent performance across diverse tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288)\]. Overall, these studies collectively highlight the ongoing challenges and opportunities in scaling LLMs for efficient and effective performance across a wide range of tasks.

The study also highlights the effectiveness of repeated sampling in generating correct code and discusses the limitations of the model in handling complex docstrings and variable bindings \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374)\]. Such models are essential for understanding and improving the efficiency of LLMs in real-world deployment scenarios.
