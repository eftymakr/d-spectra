## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced speech naturalness, but challenges remain in achieving precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00749790f8973169af3f794bee77b15364b61832)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00749790f8973169af3f794bee77b15364b61832)\]. Similarly, conventional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are impractical in low-resource settings \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00749790f8973169af3f794bee77b15364b61832)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges, focusing on methods that improve prosody modeling, control, and correction in TTS systems.

Several studies have explored the use of pre-trained language models to enhance TTS systems. For instance, \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\] proposes using BERT embeddings to encode input phrases, which are then used as additional inputs to a Tacotron2-based TTS model. This approach improves prosody and pronunciation by leveraging semantic information from the text. Similarly, \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00749790f8973169af3f794bee77b15364b61832)\] integrates a BERT model into an RNN-based TTS system, demonstrating that fine-tuning the BERT component leads to better prosody and pronunciation. These methods highlight the potential of pre-trained language models in improving TTS quality \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fbf2a6a887ea92311cf207d522c535daf867a6ba)\]\[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/00749790f8973169af3f794bee77b15364b61832)\].

Prosody modeling is a critical component of TTS systems, and several approaches have been proposed to improve it. \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05161)\] investigates the use of cross-utterance context vectors derived from BERT embeddings to enhance prosody generation in end-to-end TTS systems. The results show that incorporating information from neighboring sentences improves the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06484)\] extends this idea by proposing a model that explicitly captures cross-sentence contextual information, leading to better prediction of prosodic variations between consecutive sentences. These approaches demonstrate that incorporating contextual information can significantly improve prosody modeling in multi-sentence TTS \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05161)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06484)\].

Controllability of prosody is another key challenge in TTS. \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\] introduces a method for prosody transfer by conditioning Tacotron on a learned embedding space derived from a reference acoustic representation. This allows synthesized speech to match the prosody of the reference signal, even when the reference and synthesis speakers differ. \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] proposes a hierarchical prosody modeling approach for non-autoregressive TTS, where phoneme-level prosody features are conditioned on word-level prosody features. This method improves the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09047)\]\[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\].

### <a id='category-non-autoregressive-tts-architectures'></a>Non-autoregressive TTS Architectures: 8 papers found.
Non-autoregressive TTS architectures have been widely explored to improve training and inference efficiency, addressing limitations of autoregressive models. Models like FastSpeech \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\] and Tacotron \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\] have introduced parallel processing capabilities, enabling faster synthesis by avoiding sequential dependencies. FastSpeech, for instance, uses a teacher-student distillation pipeline to enhance duration prediction and mel-spectrogram generation, though it faces challenges with accuracy and information loss \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. To mitigate these issues, FastSpeech 2 was proposed, directly using ground-truth targets and incorporating additional speech features such as pitch and energy for better prosody control \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Similarly, Tacotron generates speech at the frame level, achieving faster inference compared to sample-level autoregressive methods \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\]. Other works, such as the hierarchical conditioning approach \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\], focus on learning latent prosody spaces to enable more versatile and controllable speech synthesis. Additionally, models like Glow-TTS \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\] eliminate the need for external aligners by integrating flow-based generative models, enabling robust and fast speech synthesis. These advancements highlight the growing emphasis on parallel, efficient, and controllable TTS systems, particularly in scenarios requiring post-hoc adjustments or multi-speaker support \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04664)\]\[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\].

The system comprises five major building blocks: a segmentation model for locating phoneme boundaries, a grapheme-to-phoneme conversion model, a phoneme duration prediction model, a fundamental frequency prediction model, and an audio synthesis model. For the segmentation model, we propose a novel way of performing phoneme boundary detection with deep neural networks using connectionist temporal classification (CTC) loss. For the audio synthesis model, we implement a variant of WaveNet that requires fewer parameters and trains faster than the original. By using a neural network for each component, our system is simpler and more flexible than traditional text-to-speech systems, where each component requires laborious feature engineering and extensive domain expertise. Finally, we show that inference with our system can be performed faster than real time and describe optimized WaveNet inference kernels on both CPU and GPU that achieve up to 400x speedups over existing implementations.»
