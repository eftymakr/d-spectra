## Related Works

Long-tailed recognition remains a challenging problem in computer vision due to the skewed class distribution in real-world datasets. Several approaches have been proposed to address this issue, primarily focusing on class-balancing strategies, contrastive learning, and representation learning. Early methods often relied on class-rebalancing techniques such as re-weighting or re-sampling to mitigate the imbalance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. However, these approaches typically involve joint learning of representations and classifiers, which can lead to suboptimal performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09217)\]. 

Recent work has explored the potential of contrastive learning for long-tailed recognition. Supervised contrastive learning (SCL) has shown promise in balanced settings by pulling together samples from the same class and pushing apart those from different classes \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.11362)\]. However, in long-tailed scenarios, SCL struggles to form a regular simplex, an ideal geometric configuration for representation learning \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. This issue is exacerbated by the imbalance in data distribution, leading to poor separability of minority class samples in the feature space \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13998)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed various improvements to contrastive learning. For instance, Balanced Contrastive Learning (BCL) introduces class-averaging and class-complement strategies to balance gradient contributions and ensure uniform distribution of features \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.09052)\]. Similarly, Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) introduces class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process and improve feature learning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12028)\]. 

In addition to contrastive learning, other methods have focused on improving the classifier learning process. The BBN framework decouples representation and classifier learning, enabling more effective handling of class imbalance \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02413)\]. This approach has been further refined with cumulative learning strategies that prioritize learning from tail classes \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02413)\]. Similarly, the RIDE framework employs a distribution-aware diversity loss and an expert routing module to improve the performance of minority classes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01809)\]. These methods highlight the importance of addressing both representation and classifier learning in long-tailed recognition.

The effectiveness of contrastive learning has also been explored in the context of self-supervised learning. SimCLR, a simple framework for contrastive learning, demonstrates that data augmentation and normalized embeddings play a critical role in learning useful representations \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709)\]. This has inspired further research into contrastive learning for long-tailed recognition, such as the Balanced Meta-Softmax approach, which addresses label distribution shifts by introducing an unbiased extension of the Softmax function \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10740)\].
