## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has become the de facto standard for ensuring privacy in data analysis \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4ce10063cd25447dcde75c2d9ce327446ced952)\]. However, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, particularly in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. For instance, servers may sample noise from incorrect distributions or generate correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. This issue has been studied in various contexts, highlighting the need for additional verification mechanisms to ensure the correctness of DP mechanisms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\].

Several works have explored the limitations of local differential privacy (LDP) protocols. For example, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\] shows that LDP protocols are highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can obscure the distribution of honest users' input. Similarly, \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\] demonstrates that attackers can inject fake users to manipulate the output of LDP protocols, leading to data poisoning attacks. These findings underscore the importance of robustness in LDP protocols \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]\[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\].

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed verifiable differentially private mechanisms. For instance, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\] introduces a verifiable randomization mechanism that allows the data collector to verify the completeness of the randomization process, preventing output manipulation. This approach provides strong protection against malicious data providers \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Similarly, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\] introduces interactive proofs for differential privacy, requiring the publishing entity to output a zero-knowledge proof that convinces a verifier the output is both differentially private and reliable. This ensures that the randomness used in DP is faithfully generated without revealing it to the verifier \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\].

Furthermore, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\] shows that the shuffled model can achieve significantly better sample complexity than the local model for histogram estimation, highlighting its potential for practical applications \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\].

### <a id='category-shuffled-model-of-differential-privacy'></a>Shuffled Model of Differential Privacy: 4 papers found.
The shuffled model of differential privacy offers a middle ground between the local and central models, enabling accurate computation of statistics while maintaining user privacy without requiring full trust in a central server \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.11839)\]. This model introduces an anonymous channel that randomly permutes user messages, allowing for improved accuracy compared to the local model while avoiding the complexities of cryptographic secure function evaluation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]. Recent work has demonstrated that shuffled protocols can achieve the accuracy of the central model while preserving the privacy benefits of the local model \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. Moreover, the shuffled model has been shown to provide better performance than the local model for certain tasks, such as estimating histograms with error independent of domain size \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. However, the model is not without limitations; for example, under pure differential privacy and single-message randomizers, the shuffled and local models are equivalent \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. These findings highlight the potential of the shuffled model as a promising direction for future research in distributed privacy-preserving computation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.11839)\].

### <a id='category-data-poisoning-and-adversarial-manipulation'></a>Data Poisoning and Adversarial Manipulation: 2 papers found.
Local differential privacy (LDP) protocols, which allow users to perturb their data before sending it to an untrusted collector, have been extensively studied for their utility in privacy-preserving data analytics \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. However, these protocols are vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can significantly distort the results by injecting fake data or altering their inputs \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. This vulnerability is particularly pronounced when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large, as the perturbations introduced by honest users become less distinguishable from malicious ones \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. Researchers have shown that attackers can launch data poisoning attacks by injecting fake users who send carefully crafted data, leading to inaccurate frequency estimates or false heavy hitter identifications \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. While some countermeasures have been proposed, such as modifying the protocol to detect and mitigate such attacks, their effectiveness varies depending on the scenario \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\]. These findings highlight the need for more robust mechanisms that can withstand adversarial manipulation while maintaining the privacy guarantees of LDP. The work presented in this paper addresses these challenges by introducing Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP), which incorporates verification mechanisms to ensure the correctness of the execution of LDP protocols \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. This approach builds on prior research that has emphasized the importance of verifying the integrity of privacy-preserving mechanisms in distributed settings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\].

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-foundations'></a>Differential Privacy Foundations: 9 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy Foundations" encompasses foundational research on differential privacy (DP), including its theoretical underpinnings, practical implementations, and extensions to handle specific challenges. Early work established DP as a rigorous framework for privacy, ensuring that the output of a mechanism does not reveal information about any individual data point \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e4ce10063cd25447dcde75c2d9ce327446ced952)\]. This was further refined with the introduction of the Rényi divergence-based relaxation, which allows for more flexible and tighter analysis of privacy guarantees, especially in composite mechanisms \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.07476)\]. The local model of DP, which does not require a trusted data collector, has been widely adopted in commercial settings due to its privacy benefits, though it often sacrifices utility and algorithmic expressibility compared to the central model \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. To address these limitations, systems like Cryptε have been proposed, enabling the benefits of the central model without a trusted collector by using encrypted data and two untrusted servers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Additionally, research has focused on improving the efficiency and accuracy of DP mechanisms, such as for repeated telemetry data collection, where existing LDP algorithms struggle with long-term privacy guarantees \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to balance privacy, utility, and practicality in DP implementations.
