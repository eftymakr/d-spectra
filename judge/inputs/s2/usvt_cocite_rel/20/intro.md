## Related Works

Deep learning has significantly impacted the field of inverse problems in imaging, with two prominent approaches being deep-unrolling and plug-and-play (PnP) methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. PnP approaches, which integrate off-the-shelf deep denoisers into iterative algorithms, offer flexibility in handling varying compression ratios (CRs) but often suffer from limitations in reconstruction accuracy and speed \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03488)\]. These methods rely on the idea that advanced denoisers can act as implicit regularizers, replacing traditional proximal operators \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.06756)\]. However, the performance of PnP methods can be suboptimal when the denoiser is not well-suited to the specific problem at hand \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10557)\].

On the other hand, unrolling approaches transform iterative optimization algorithms into deep neural networks by truncating the process and making it end-to-end trainable \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.05399)\]. These networks typically achieve higher accuracy and faster inference speeds compared to PnP methods. However, they require retraining or fine-tuning when the compression ratio changes, which limits their adaptability \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b954a94af132294a4a4e2efce15027758a535742)\]. This limitation highlights the need for a more flexible and generalizable approach that can handle varying CRs without retraining \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03264)\].

Recent works have explored the integration of both PnP and unrolling approaches to leverage their respective strengths \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.04693)\]. For instance, the use of algorithm unrolling has been shown to provide interpretable and efficient deep learning solutions for signal and image processing \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03679)\]. This technique connects iterative algorithms with deep neural networks, enabling the development of networks that can be trained on reasonably sized datasets \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02862)\]. Similarly, the concept of deep unfolding has been used to design networks that inherit the flexibility of model-based methods while maintaining the performance of learning-based approaches \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.12529)\].

The idea of using deep networks to approximate proximal operators has also gained traction \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10428)\]. This approach allows for the integration of learned denoisers into optimization frameworks, leading to improved performance in tasks such as image denoising and deblurring \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03981)\]. Additionally, the use of residual learning and batch normalization in deep convolutional neural networks has enabled the development of models that can handle a wide range of image restoration tasks \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08983)\].

In the context of image restoration, the use of trainable nonlinear reaction diffusion models has shown promise in achieving fast and effective results \[[14](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3013361190851e886398543caa68534578593fb3)\]. These models incorporate domain-specific knowledge and can be trained to exploit the structural properties of images \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08616)\].
