Early assemblers, such as SOAPdenovo and ABySS, relied on de Bruijn graphs and overlap-layout-consensus (OLC) strategies to reconstruct genomes from fragmented data \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7279acd9c61a8de019ea2d55d23124dfbb6b5269)\]. These methods, while effective for small genomes, struggled with scalability and accuracy for large, complex genomes due to memory constraints and computational inefficiencies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.01801)\]. The introduction of long-read sequencing technologies, such as PacBio and Oxford Nanopore, has significantly improved assembly continuity, but these technologies also introduce high error rates that require sophisticated error correction and assembly strategies \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b59fd18ada769ef76fb7fcc7382f063791abfab)\].

Several studies have explored the parametric complexity of the assembly problem, highlighting the interplay between repeat complexity, read lengths, and coverage in determining the difficulty of assembly \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/618d82a202bb0408e388eba250c66c8a90700c81)\]. This has led to the development of more advanced assemblers that leverage distributed computing and parallel processing to handle large datasets. Efforts to reduce memory usage and improve computational efficiency have also been a key focus.
