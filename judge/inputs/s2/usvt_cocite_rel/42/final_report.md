## Related Works

The growing adoption of cloud-native applications has led to increased reliance on containerization technologies and managed cloud services to achieve rapid deployment and scalability. Kubernetes, in particular, has emerged as a de facto standard for orchestrating containerized applications \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d11e5c5c08af88ee8abb9ebaa166b1d6fd198501)\]. Prior research has focused on optimizing resource efficiency and cost at the system level, but network costs in Kubernetes clusters have remained underexplored \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d11e5c5c08af88ee8abb9ebaa166b1d6fd198501)\]. This paper contributes to the emerging field of FinOps, which integrates financial management with cloud operations to optimize costs in cloud-based applications \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d11e5c5c08af88ee8abb9ebaa166b1d6fd198501)\]. 

Several studies have examined the performance and resource management of containerized applications in cloud environments. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10350)\] investigates the performance of computational-intensive applications such as big data and deep learning in cloud-native environments, highlighting the impact of resource management schemes on completion time and resource usage. The paper demonstrates that changing default settings can reduce completion time by up to 79.4%, while different resource management schemes can increase it by up to 96.7%. This underscores the importance of fine-tuning resource allocation strategies to achieve optimal performance and cost efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10350)\].

In addition, \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05998)\] addresses the scheduling problem in distributed computing frameworks, focusing on jobs with diverse resource requirements. The study proposes oblivious scheduling algorithms that can achieve at least 1/2 and 2/3 of the maximum throughput without requiring knowledge of traffic or resource requirement distributions. These algorithms are designed to handle high-dimensional settings where traditional scheduling approaches are less effective \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05998)\]. This work is relevant to the broader context of optimizing resource allocation and cost management in Kubernetes clusters.

The integration of container orchestration platforms like Kubernetes with cloud-native workflows has also been explored. For example, \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d32b4e8ef3fbde009efa47249081feaa4aac8b27)\] presents a cloud provider-agnostic and scalable container orchestration setup for the Galaxy workflow environment, enabling it to run on and offload jobs to various cloud providers through Kubernetes. This approach enhances the scalability and reproducibility of bioinformatics workflows \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d32b4e8ef3fbde009efa47249081feaa4aac8b27)\]. Similarly, \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d1b8c407ddcf31dba806e580de02cb8c873ea2a6)\] describes the PhenoMeNal e-infrastructure, which provides a complete, workflow-oriented solution for metabolomics data analysis using Docker containers and Kubernetes orchestration. Jobs with diverse resource requirements (e.g. memory requirements) arrive over time and must be served by a cluster of servers, each with a finite resource capacity. To improve throughput and delay, the scheduler can pack as many jobs as possible in the servers subject to their capacity constraints. Motivated by the ever-increasing complexity of workloads in shared clusters, we consider a setting where the jobs’ resource requirements belong to a very large number of diverse types or, in the extreme, even infinitely many types, e.g. when resource requirements are drawn from an unknown distribution over a continuous support. The application of classical scheduling approaches that crucially rely on a predefined finite set of types is discouraging in this high (or infinite) dimensional setting. We first characterize a fundamental limit on the maximum throughput in such setting, and then develop oblivious scheduling algorithms that have tow complexity and can achieve at least 1/2 and 2/3 of the maximum throughput, without the knowledge of traffic or resource requirement distribution. These technologies are particularly beneficial for microservices architectures, where independent, loosely coupled modules can be developed and deployed separately, enhancing maintainability and scalability \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18a720c86ed6e166c26f7d39490bfd11b93ea3ec)\]. However, while Kubernetes offers robust mechanisms for deployment and scaling, studies have shown that under default configurations, it may not always ensure optimal availability, with experiments revealing higher-than-expected service outages \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/18a720c86ed6e166c26f7d39490bfd11b93ea3ec)\]. As organizations increasingly rely on cloud-native applications and managed services like Kubernetes, the need for cost-effective and efficient resource management becomes critical, especially in the context of emerging disciplines such as FinOps, which integrates financial management with cloud operations to optimize costs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10350)\].

Workflow management in scientific computing has seen significant advancements with the integration of containerization and orchestration tools to address reproducibility and scalability challenges \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/51e2cba5a013cbd11f2ab760a4e3906cfff44780)\]. Pachyderm, an open-source workflow system, provides a data pipelining and versioning layer on top of container ecosystems, leveraging Kubernetes for orchestration \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/51e2cba5a013cbd11f2ab760a4e3906cfff44780)\]. This approach ensures that computational workflows can be reliably executed across different environments while maintaining data integrity throughout the processing pipeline \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/51e2cba5a013cbd11f2ab760a4e3906cfff44780)\]. Similarly, the Pegasus Workflow Management System supports container execution in distributed workflows, highlighting the importance of containerization in ensuring long-term reproducibility \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08204)\]. Additionally, Popper, a container-native workflow engine, enables seamless execution of workflows without assuming the presence of a Kubernetes cluster, making it suitable for diverse environments such as local machines and HPC clusters \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4163bdd019beaec6481042813eb4193681e5bf6b)\]. These systems collectively emphasize the need for robust, flexible, and reproducible workflow management solutions in scientific research, particularly in fields like bioinformatics where large-scale data processing is common \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/51e2cba5a013cbd11f2ab760a4e3906cfff44780)\].
