## Related Works

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have emerged as a critical research direction, aiming to integrate textual and visual information for more comprehensive understanding and generation. Early works such as BERT \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\] focused primarily on language understanding, but recent advancements have emphasized the integration of vision and language. For instance, Flamingo \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198)\] introduced a framework that bridges vision-only and language-only models, enabling few-shot learning on multimodal tasks. Similarly, PaLI \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.06794)\] extended this approach to multilingual settings, demonstrating the importance of joint scaling of vision and language components.

The ability to understand and generate nonverbal cues, such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language, remains a significant challenge in MLLMs. Prior works like Visual Genome \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07332)\] focused on dense image annotations to model object relationships, which is a foundational step toward more complex multimodal understanding. However, these datasets primarily support perception tasks rather than conversational ones. More recently, Video-LLaMA \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02858)\] and Video-ChatGPT \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05424)\] have made strides in video understanding by integrating visual and auditory information, enabling detailed conversations about video content. These models use cross-modal training to align visual and audio features with language representations, which is a crucial step toward more natural interaction.

The development of datasets that include annotated videos with time-aligned text, facial expressions, and body language has been essential for training models like MARS. The VENUS dataset \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14824)\] is a notable example, as it provides a large-scale, annotated resource for studying nonverbal communication. This dataset enables models to learn the intricate relationships between text and nonverbal cues, which is critical for generating immersive conversational experiences. Other works, such as Shikra \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15195)\], have focused on referential dialogue, demonstrating the ability of MLLMs to handle spatial coordinates and natural language inputs, which is a key component of nonverbal communication in dialogue.

Instruction tuning has also played a vital role in improving the performance of MLLMs on multimodal tasks. For example, LLaVA \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08485)\] leverages instruction-tuned data to enhance zero-shot capabilities on new tasks, while Otter \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.03726)\] extends this approach by incorporating both textual and visual in-context examples for instruction tuning. These methods have shown that proper alignment of visual features with language models can significantly enhance multimodal understanding and generation capabilities.

In addition to these advancements, there has been growing interest in models that can handle a wide range of modalities and tasks
