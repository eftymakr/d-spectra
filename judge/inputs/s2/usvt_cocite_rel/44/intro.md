## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching. Early approaches primarily relied on matrix factorization \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.2944)\], which decomposes user-item interaction matrices into latent user and item embeddings. However, these methods struggle with scalability and sparsity, motivating the exploration of deep learning-based techniques. Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] was one of the first to apply deep neural networks to collaborative filtering, using a multi-layer perceptron to model user-item interactions. This approach significantly improved recommendation performance by capturing non-linear patterns in user preferences.

Recent advances in graph neural networks have further enhanced the modeling of complex interactions. GraphSAGE \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\] introduced an inductive framework for generating node embeddings by aggregating features from a node's local neighborhood, making it suitable for large-scale and evolving graphs. Similarly, GCN-based methods, such as the one proposed in \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\], have been applied to web-scale recommendation tasks, leveraging graph convolutional networks to incorporate both graph structure and node features. These models have demonstrated superior performance on large datasets, particularly when combined with efficient training strategies \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\].

Another line of research focuses on integrating side information, such as item attributes or user behavior, into the recommendation process. Variational Autoencoders for Collaborative Filtering \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\] extended traditional factorization models by introducing a probabilistic framework that allows for more flexible modeling of user-item interactions. This approach also incorporates Bayesian inference, which has been shown to improve model robustness and generalization. Additionally, the use of knowledge graphs has gained traction in recommendation systems, as demonstrated by KGAT \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\], which explicitly models high-order relations in knowledge graphs to improve recommendation accuracy.

The importance of preserving information during the embedding process has also been emphasized. For instance, the work on graph convolutional neural networks \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\] highlights the need for localized spectral filtering to capture meaningful patterns in graph-structured data. This approach enables the learning of stationary and compositional features, which are crucial for accurate recommendations. Similarly, the study on information loss during binarization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\] underscores the necessity of mitigating information loss at various stages of the embedding process, a key insight that informs the design of BiGeaR++.
