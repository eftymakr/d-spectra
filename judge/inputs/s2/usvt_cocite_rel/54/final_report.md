## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods such as matrix factorization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\] and deep learning-based approaches \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] primarily relied on user and item embeddings derived from pre-existing features, often neglecting the collaborative signal inherent in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more sophisticated models that explicitly incorporate graph structures into the embedding process.

Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] was one of the first to propose a framework that integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure. This approach enables the modeling of high-order connectivity, effectively capturing collaborative filtering effects \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, subsequent work has highlighted challenges in scalability and training efficiency, particularly for large-scale recommendation tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

LightGCN \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] simplified the design of GCNs by removing non-linear activation functions and feature transformations, which were found to contribute little to performance and hinder training. This linear approach, based solely on neighborhood aggregation, demonstrated substantial improvements over NGCF and other GCN-based models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. The success of LightGCN underscores the importance of simplicity and efficiency in large-scale recommendation systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

Another line of research has focused on improving the modeling of user-item interactions through graph attention mechanisms. Graph Attention Networks (GATs) \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\] introduced masked self-attentional layers to enable nodes to assign different weights to their neighbors without requiring costly matrix operations. This approach has been shown to achieve or match state-of-the-art results across multiple benchmarks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]. Similarly, RippleNet \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] leverages knowledge graphs to propagate user preferences, enhancing recommendation accuracy by incorporating side information \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\].

The integration of knowledge graphs into recommendation systems has also been explored, with models like Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\] explicitly modeling high-order relations in the graph. KGAT uses an attention mechanism to discriminate the importance of neighbors during embedding propagation, leading to improved performance on benchmark datasets \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\].

In addition to GCNs, other approaches have focused on improving the robustness and generalization of recommendation models. ### <a id='category-graph-convolutional-networks-in-recommendations'></a>Graph Convolutional Networks in Recommendations: 10 papers found.
Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have emerged as a powerful approach for collaborative filtering in recommender systems, leveraging the user-item interaction graph to capture higher-order connectivity and collaborative signals \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods, such as matrix factorization, relied on pre-defined features to generate embeddings, but these approaches often failed to explicitly encode collaborative information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. To address this, NGCF was introduced, integrating user-item graph structures into the embedding process through embedding propagation, enabling more effective modeling of collaborative filtering effects \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, training GCNs on large-scale graphs remains challenging due to non-linear activation functions and over-smoothing issues \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. LightGCN was proposed as a simpler alternative, focusing on neighborhood aggregation without non-linearities, leading to improved performance and easier training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, DGCF further advanced the field by disentangling user intents in interactions, enhancing the interpretability of learned representations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. Additionally, methods like SGL and LightGCL have explored self-supervised learning and contrastive learning to improve robustness and accuracy, especially for long-tail items and sparse data \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10783)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.08191)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to refine GCN-based models for more effective and scalable recommendation systems.

### <a id='category-matrix-factorization-and-deep-learning-approaches'></a>Matrix Factorization and Deep Learning Approaches: 6 papers found.
In the realm of collaborative filtering (CF) for recommender systems, recent advancements have focused on leveraging deep learning techniques to enhance traditional matrix factorization approaches. While matrix factorization remains a cornerstone for modeling user-item interactions, its linear assumptions are often insufficient to capture complex patterns in user behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. To address this, neural collaborative filtering (NCF) was introduced, replacing the inner product used in matrix factorization with a multi-layer perceptron to learn non-linear interactions between users and items \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. This framework is capable of generalizing matrix factorization and has shown significant improvements in recommendation performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. Additionally, variational autoencoders (VAEs) have been extended to CF for implicit feedback, offering non-linear probabilistic modeling that surpasses linear factor models in representation capacity \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. Furthermore, the Wide & Deep learning approach combines memorization through wide linear models with generalization via deep neural networks, effectively balancing the trade-off between capturing known patterns and learning new ones \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07792)\]. These developments highlight a growing trend toward integrating deep learning with CF to improve recommendation accuracy and adaptability to sparse and high-dimensional data.

### <a id='category-scalability-and-training-efficiency'></a>Scalability and Training Efficiency: 3 papers found.
In the context of scalability and training efficiency, recent works have focused on improving the performance of graph-based models while reducing computational overhead. For instance, a scalable approach for semi-supervised learning on graph-structured data has been proposed, which uses an efficient variant of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that operate directly on graphs \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. This model scales linearly with the number of graph edges and learns representations that encode both local graph structure and node features, demonstrating significant improvements on citation networks and knowledge graph datasets. Similarly, another study has introduced a simplified version of Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) by removing nonlinearities and collapsing weight matrices between layers, resulting in a linear model that corresponds to a fixed low-pass filter followed by a linear classifier \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\]. This approach not only improves scalability but also achieves up to two orders of magnitude speedup over FastGCN without compromising accuracy. Additionally, a contrastive learning-based method for recommendation systems has been developed, which eliminates graph augmentations and instead uses noise-based embedding augmentation to generate views for contrastive learning \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.02544)\]. This method, termed XSimGCL, demonstrates that simpler approaches can outperform complex graph augmentation-based methods in terms of both recommendation accuracy and training efficiency. These studies collectively highlight the importance of simplifying graph models while maintaining their effectiveness, emphasizing the trade-off between model complexity and computational efficiency.

The category "Spectral Graph Theory and Graph Filtering" encompasses research that extends traditional signal processing and machine learning techniques to graph-structured data, leveraging spectral methods to analyze and model complex relationships. This body of work focuses on generalizing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to high-dimensional irregular domains such as social networks, brain connectomes, and word embeddings, by formulating CNNs within the framework of spectral graph theory \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. This approach enables the design of localized, fast convolutional filters that operate on graphs while maintaining linear computational complexity, similar to classical CNNs \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]. Furthermore, recent work has introduced novel models such as Spectral Collaborative Filtering (SpectralCF), which directly operates in the spectral domain of user-item bipartite graphs to alleviate the cold-start problem in recommendation systems by leveraging connectivity information \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. These advancements highlight the growing importance of spectral graph theory in enhancing the performance and adaptability of models for real-world applications involving graph-structured data \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\]\[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1211.0053)\].
