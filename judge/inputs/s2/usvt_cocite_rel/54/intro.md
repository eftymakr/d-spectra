## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early methods such as matrix factorization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\] and deep learning-based approaches \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\] primarily relied on user and item embeddings derived from pre-existing features, often neglecting the collaborative signal inherent in user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This limitation has motivated the development of more sophisticated models that explicitly incorporate graph structures into the embedding process.

Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] was one of the first to propose a framework that integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure. This approach enables the modeling of high-order connectivity, effectively capturing collaborative filtering effects \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. However, subsequent work has highlighted challenges in scalability and training efficiency, particularly for large-scale recommendation tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

LightGCN \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\] simplified the design of GCNs by removing non-linear activation functions and feature transformations, which were found to contribute little to performance and hinder training. This linear approach, based solely on neighborhood aggregation, demonstrated substantial improvements over NGCF and other GCN-based models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. The success of LightGCN underscores the importance of simplicity and efficiency in large-scale recommendation systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\].

Another line of research has focused on improving the modeling of user-item interactions through graph attention mechanisms. Graph Attention Networks (GATs) \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\] introduced masked self-attentional layers to enable nodes to assign different weights to their neighbors without requiring costly matrix operations. This approach has been shown to achieve or match state-of-the-art results across multiple benchmarks \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\]. Similarly, RippleNet \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\] leverages knowledge graphs to propagate user preferences, enhancing recommendation accuracy by incorporating side information \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03467)\].

The integration of knowledge graphs into recommendation systems has also been explored, with models like Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\] explicitly modeling high-order relations in the graph. KGAT uses an attention mechanism to discriminate the importance of neighbors during embedding propagation, leading to improved performance on benchmark datasets \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\].

In addition to GCNs, other approaches have focused on improving the robustness and generalization of recommendation models. Variational Autoencoders for Collaborative Filtering \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\] introduced a non-linear probabilistic model that enables better modeling of user-item interactions compared to linear factor models. This approach has shown significant improvements over state-of-the-art baselines \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\].
