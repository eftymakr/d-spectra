## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to leverage sequential user behavior across multiple domains for improved recommendations. Early approaches primarily focused on modeling sequential patterns using traditional methods such as Markov Chains (MCs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, these methods often struggle with capturing long-term dependencies and complex sequential patterns \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these limitations, more advanced models like SASRec and Caser have been proposed, utilizing self-attention and convolutional filters respectively to better model sequential dynamics \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]\[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. Despite these advancements, existing CDSR methods still face challenges in effectively transferring knowledge between domains and modeling mixed behavior sequences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\].

Recent studies have explored the use of graph-based models to capture cross-domain interactions. For example, DA-GCN introduces a domain-aware graph convolution network to learn user-specific representations by considering the cross-domain graph structure \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Similarly, the mixed information flow network (MIFN) proposes a dual approach to model both behavioral and knowledge flow between domains \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. These models aim to improve recommendation performance by effectively transferring information across domains. However, they often rely on explicit modeling of item IDs, which limits their transferability to new domains \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\].

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into recommendation systems has shown promise in addressing some of these limitations. LLMs are capable of capturing semantic information and reasoning about user preferences, making them suitable for cross-domain sequential recommendation tasks \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09261)\]. For instance, BERT4Rec uses bidirectional self-attention to model user behavior sequences, allowing for more accurate predictions by considering both left and right contexts \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Additionally, the use of LLMs in recommendation systems has been explored in the context of cross-domain settings, such as in the Chat-Rec framework, which leverages LLMs for interactive and explainable recommendations \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of LLMs in capturing semantic relationships and improving recommendation performance.

Several studies have also focused on improving the transferability of recommendation models by leveraging pre-trained language models. For example, VQ-Rec proposes a vector-quantized item representation scheme to enhance transferability across domains \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12316)\]. Similarly, the UniSRec framework utilizes universal sequence representation learning to enable efficient transfer across different recommendation scenarios \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]. These methods aim to reduce the dependency on domain-specific data by leveraging pre-trained models and transfer learning techniques.

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into recommendation systems has gained significant traction, particularly in addressing challenges related to cross-domain sequential recommendations. Existing methods often struggle with the overlap dilemma, where they rely heavily on users with interactions across multiple domains, and the transition complexity, which involves capturing intricate patterns from mixed behavior sequences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. LLMs, with their strong semantic reasoning and representation capabilities, offer a promising solution by enabling the modeling of item relationships and user preferences from a semantic perspective \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]. These approaches highlight the potential of LLMs in improving recommendation quality through semantic understanding and adaptive modeling \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09261)\]. ### <a id='category-advanced-sequential-modeling-approaches'></a>Advanced Sequential Modeling Approaches: 5 papers found.
Advanced Sequential Modeling Approaches have seen significant advancements in recent years, with a focus on improving the modeling of user behavior sequences in recommendation systems. Traditional methods, such as Markov Chains and Recurrent Neural Networks, have limitations in capturing long-term dependencies and handling sparse or dense datasets effectively \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed bidirectional models like BERT4Rec, which utilize deep self-attention mechanisms to capture both left and right context in user behavior sequences, thereby enhancing the representation power \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Similarly, the S3-Rec model introduces self-supervised learning techniques based on mutual information maximization, enabling better utilization of context and sequence data through auxiliary objectives \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\]. Additionally, the UBCS framework tackles the issue of long user behavior histories by employing behavior sampling and item clustering to reduce noise and improve inference efficiency \[[20](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e772424bcf7e6f7cb6bf0d4a46cec66b259c670f)\]. The Caser model, on the other hand, leverages convolutional filters to capture local sequential patterns from item sequences, offering a flexible approach to model both general preferences and sequential dynamics \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the shift towards more sophisticated and efficient methods for sequential recommendation, emphasizing the importance of bidirectional modeling, self-supervised learning, and efficient handling of long-term user behavior.
