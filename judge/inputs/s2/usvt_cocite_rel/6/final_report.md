## Related Works

Text summarization has seen significant advancements with the advent of neural sequence-to-sequence models, which enable abstractive summarization by generating novel content rather than simply rephrasing source text \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1409.3215)\]. Early work on abstractive summarization used attentional encoder-decoder recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on various corpora \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.06023)\]. However, these models often struggled with repetition and inaccuracy, prompting the development of pointer-generator networks that allow for both copying source text and generating novel content \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\]. This architecture significantly improved performance on the CNN/Daily Mail dataset, achieving a 2-point ROUGE score improvement over prior methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\].

Further improvements were made with the introduction of coverage mechanisms, which help prevent repetition by tracking what has been summarized \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\]. Additionally, the PEGASUS model introduced a novel pre-training objective for abstractive summarization, where important sentences are masked and generated from the remaining content, leading to state-of-the-art results across 12 diverse summarization tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08777)\]. This approach also demonstrated strong performance on low-resource datasets, surpassing previous results on six datasets with only 1,000 examples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.08777)\].

The use of pre-trained transformers has also gained traction, with models like BERT being adapted for summarization tasks. These models leverage pre-trained encoders to capture document-level semantics and improve both extractive and abstractive summarization \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08345)\]. Moreover, the unified text-to-text transformer framework has enabled transfer learning across multiple NLP tasks, including summarization, by converting all problems into a common format \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. This has led to state-of-the-art results on benchmarks covering summarization, question answering, and text classification \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\].

For extreme summarization, where the goal is to produce a single-sentence summary capturing the essence of an article, convolutional neural networks have been employed to model long-range dependencies and recognize pertinent content \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.08745)\]. This approach outperformed both extractive and abstractive baselines, demonstrating the potential of abstractive modeling for such tasks \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.08745)\]. Similarly, bottom-up abstractive summarization has been explored, where content selectors are used to identify key phrases before generating the summary, leading to improved compression and fluency \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10792)\].

### <a id='category-coverage-mechanisms-in-summarization'></a>Coverage Mechanisms in Summarization: 2 papers found.
Coverage mechanisms in summarization have been actively explored to address issues such as factual inaccuracy and repetition in neural sequence-to-sequence models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\]. These models, while effective in generating fluent summaries, often struggle with content selection, frequently copying full sentences from the source text \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10792)\]. To mitigate these challenges, researchers have introduced hybrid pointer-generator networks that allow models to both copy words from the source and generate novel content, improving the accuracy of information reproduction \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\]. Additionally, coverage mechanisms have been employed to track what has been summarized, thereby reducing repetition by ensuring the model does not revisit previously covered content \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\]. Another approach involves using data-efficient content selectors to identify key phrases from the source document, which are then used to constrain the model's attention during summary generation \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10792)\]. This two-step process not only enhances the ability to compress text but also maintains fluency in the output \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10792)\]. These advancements highlight the importance of integrating coverage and content selection strategies to improve the quality and coherence of generated summaries, particularly in tasks requiring high fidelity to the source material.

The evaluation of text summarization systems remains a critical area of research, with existing methods facing significant challenges in aligning with human judgment and ensuring robustness. Current evaluation protocols are often weakly correlated with human assessment, failing to account for essential aspects such as factual accuracy and coherence \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08960)\]. Additionally, automatically collected datasets introduce noise and biases, which can hinder model training and evaluation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08960)\]. These limitations are further compounded by the overfitting of models to dataset-specific layouts, resulting in limited output diversity \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08960)\]. Recent studies have attempted to address these shortcomings by re-evaluating a wide range of automatic evaluation metrics using neural summarization model outputs alongside expert and crowd-sourced annotations \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12626)\]. This work highlights the need for more comprehensive and standardized evaluation frameworks that better reflect human judgment, particularly in complex summarization tasks involving long-form narratives \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12626)\].
