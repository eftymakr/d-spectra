## Related Works

Taxation and tax compliance have been extensively studied using agent-based models (ABMs) to simulate heterogeneous taxpayer behavior and evaluate enforcement mechanisms. Early work in this area focused on modeling tax evasion as a social phenomenon, often drawing parallels to statistical mechanics models such as the Ising model \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1112.0233)\]. This approach was later extended to incorporate more complex social networks and heterogeneous agent types, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of how tax compliance varies across different societal structures \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fba9c1bee4a5bda0d5dbf01f97ef5a8e212833e)\]. These models typically assume that agents interact with their neighbors, and their decisions are influenced by both individual incentives and social norms \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/0806.0344)\].

Several studies have explored the role of enforcement mechanisms in reducing tax evasion. For instance, one paper demonstrated that even minimal audit rates can significantly reduce tax evasion when incorporated into an Ising-based model \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. This finding was corroborated by another study that showed that punishment-based enforcement strategies can effectively control tax evasion dynamics \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/0801.2980)\]. These results suggest that policy interventions, even when modest, can have a substantial impact on compliance behavior.

The concept of tax morale, or the general attitude toward taxation, has also been integrated into ABMs. Research has shown that trust in governmental institutions plays a critical role in shaping taxpayer behavior, with higher trust leading to lower rates of evasion \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/0806.0344)\]. This insight has been used to calibrate models with real-world data, allowing for more accurate predictions of compliance patterns under different policy scenarios \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3b38a079d28ee6945549ba16119bb95978fa1cc1)\].

In addition to traditional ABMs, some studies have explored the use of multi-agent systems to simulate the interactions between different types of taxpayers, such as high-income earners and low-income individuals. These models often incorporate behavioral economics principles to better reflect real-world decision-making processes \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3b38a079d28ee6945549ba16119bb95978fa1cc1)\]. For example, one study used an inhomogeneous Ising model to simulate the interactions between agents with varying levels of social temperature, leading to more realistic outcomes in terms of compliance behavior \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1112.0233)\].

Recent advances in computational modeling have also led to the development of hybrid approaches that combine ABMs with machine learning techniques. These models are designed to adapt to changing economic conditions and optimize tax policies in real time. For instance, one paper introduced a novel framework that integrates large language models (LLMs) with ABMs to create adaptive tax policies that balance equity and productivity \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\]. This approach has shown promising results in benchmarking against traditional models such as Saez Optimal Taxation and U.S. federal income tax systems \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\].
