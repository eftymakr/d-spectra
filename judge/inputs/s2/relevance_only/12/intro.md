## Related Works

Event cameras, which capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis, have gained significant attention due to their high temporal resolution \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces challenges in terms of bandwidth, memory, and computational demands \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. Prior work has explored event downsampling as a means to mitigate these challenges, but most approaches rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit their adaptability \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. For instance, some methods use simple thresholds to discard events based on their magnitude or frequency \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\]. These methods, while effective in reducing data volume, lack the flexibility to adapt to varying scene dynamics and application requirements.

In contrast, recent research has proposed learning-based approaches that adapt to the specific characteristics of event streams \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\]. For example, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\] introduces a framework that converts event streams into grid-based representations and learns the input event representation together with the task-specific network in an end-to-end manner. This allows for more flexible and task-aware processing of event data. Similarly, \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.09148)\] proposes an asynchronous sparse convolutional network that directly leverages the sparsity and asynchronous nature of event data, reducing computational complexity and latency without sacrificing accuracy.

Another line of work focuses on the reconstruction of intensity images from event streams, which is essential for bridging the gap between event-based and traditional vision tasks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08245)\]. For example, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.08283)\] presents a self-supervised learning approach for intensity reconstruction, leveraging the inner workings of event cameras and photometric constancy to train neural networks without requiring ground-truth or synthetic data. This approach has shown promising results, achieving performance comparable to state-of-the-art methods.

The importance of adaptive and intelligent sampling strategies has also been highlighted in the context of event-based vision. \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.13436)\] addresses the challenge of object detection using event cameras by introducing a novel recurrent architecture and a temporal consistency loss. This method compactly represents the sequence of events into the internal memory of the model, enabling high accuracy without requiring intensity image reconstruction. Similarly, \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.03095)\] proposes a method to convert existing video datasets into synthetic event data, enabling the use of large-scale datasets for training event-based models. This approach has shown that models trained on synthetic events can generalize well to real event data, even in challenging conditions.
