## Related Works

Continual learning (CL) aims to enable models to learn from a sequence of tasks without forgetting previously acquired knowledge, a challenge often referred to as catastrophic forgetting (CF) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07446)\]. Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a prominent strategy for mitigating CF by storing and reusing a subset of past data during training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05836)\]. These methods typically involve training on both new and old data simultaneously, a strategy known as concurrent rehearsal \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.17091)\]. However, the optimality of this approach remains unclear, particularly in comparison to sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first, followed by revisiting past data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14544)\]. 

The effectiveness of rehearsal-based methods has been extensively studied in the context of both linear and non-linear models. For instance, \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8eaed8eafb25d5c41cf0fce53d276765ddded613)\] highlights that models trained sequentially with rehearsal tend to stay in the same low-loss region after a task has finished, risking overfitting on the sample memory and harming generalization. This suggests that concurrent rehearsal may not always be the best approach, especially when tasks are dissimilar \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02464)\]. 

Several studies have explored the theoretical underpinnings of rehearsal-based CL. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07492)\] provides a theoretical analysis under overparameterized linear models, showing that the expected forgetting and generalization error can be explicitly characterized. This analysis reveals that task similarity plays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of rehearsal strategies \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02251)\]. Furthermore, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06916)\] demonstrates that the performance of CL methods can be significantly influenced by the characteristics of the data and the learning algorithms used. 

In addition to theoretical insights, empirical studies have shown that sequential rehearsal can offer advantages over concurrent rehearsal, particularly when tasks are less similar. For example, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07812)\] shows that sequential rehearsal outperforms concurrent rehearsal in scenarios where tasks are dissimilar, suggesting that revisiting past data sequentially can help mitigate forgetting. This aligns with findings from \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.01133)\], which indicates that models trained on robust features can enhance adversarial robustness and reduce catastrophic forgetting. 

The concept of hybrid rehearsal, which combines elements of both concurrent and sequential rehearsal, has also been explored. \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00316)\] proposes a hybrid approach that trains similar tasks concurrently and revisits dissimilar tasks sequentially, leading to improved performance on benchmark datasets. This method is supported by empirical evidence from \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13917)\], which demonstrates that a simple baseline, Repeated Augmented Rehearsal (RAR), outperforms vanilla rehearsal by 9%-17% across multiple benchmarks. 

Moreover, the role of pre-training in mitigating catastrophic forgetting has been investigated.
