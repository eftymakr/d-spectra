## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area in natural language processing, aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions and highlight specific attributes of text \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. Early approaches primarily relied on encoder-only models, which were specialized for particular tasks and domains \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. However, these models often required extensive training and lacked the flexibility to adapt to new instructions without re-encoding the entire corpus \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. 

Recent work has introduced methods that allow for instruction-tuned embeddings without retraining from scratch. INSTRUCTOR, for instance, is a single embedder that can generate text embeddings tailored to different downstream tasks and domains without further training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. This model was trained on a multitask mixture with a contrastive loss, achieving state-of-the-art performance across 70 diverse datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. Similarly, InBedder treats instructions as questions about the input text and encodes the expected answers to obtain the representation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. This approach significantly improves instruction-following capabilities, especially when applied to both large language models (LLMs) and smaller encoder-based models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\].

Another line of research focuses on the robustness and interpretability of instruction-following models. The work in \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09382)\] explores how deep reinforcement learning can be used to train instruction-following agents that are robust to natural human instructions. This method leverages pre-trained text-based language models like BERT to achieve zero-shot transfer from synthetic template commands to natural instructions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09382)\]. Additionally, the paper \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07467)\] discusses unsupervised cross-modal alignment of speech and text embedding spaces, which has applications in spoken word classification and translation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07467)\]. While this work is not directly related to instruction-following, it highlights the importance of alignment in multimodal tasks.

The paper \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17998)\] introduces T-MASS, a stochastic text modeling method that enriches text embeddings with a flexible and resilient semantic range, enabling more accurate text-video retrieval \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17998)\]. This method introduces a similarity-aware radius module and a support text regularization to improve retrieval performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17998)\]. These techniques, while focused on retrieval, share similarities with instruction-following embeddings in their need for dynamic and context-aware representations.

### <a id='category-instruction-tuned-embedding-methods'></a>Instruction-tuned Embedding Methods: 6 papers found.
Instruction-tuned embedding methods have gained significant attention for their ability to generate text embeddings that adapt to specific user instructions, enhancing the relevance and performance of downstream tasks. These methods differ from traditional encoders by incorporating task-specific instructions during the embedding generation process, allowing for more flexible and context-aware representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. For instance, INSTRUCTOR introduces a single embedder that can generate embeddings tailored to various tasks and domains without additional training, achieving state-of-the-art results across 70 diverse datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09741)\]. Similarly, InBedder treats instructions as questions about the input text, encoding expected answers to produce more semantically aligned embeddings, demonstrating improved instruction-following capabilities across both large and smaller models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. The InF-IR corpus further advances this field by providing a high-quality training dataset with expressivetriplets, enabling efficient representation learning for encoder-only models and improving instruction-aware retrieval \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09382)\]. GRIT extends this by unifying generative and embedding tasks through instruction-based differentiation, achieving superior performance on benchmark tasks and enabling faster Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07467)\]. Explore-Instruct enhances domain-specific instruction coverage by actively exploring diverse instruction-tuning data, improving model performance in specialized contexts \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17998)\]. Finally, JsonTuning addresses limitations in existing text-to-text methods by using structured JSON inputs to improve generalization, robustness, and controllability in instruction-tuned models \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01196)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the growing importance of instruction-tuned embeddings in enabling more adaptable and effective language models.

### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-and-their-adaptation'></a>Pre-trained Language Models and Their Adaptation: 6 papers found.
Instruction-following in large language models (LLMs) has been a focal point of recent research, with various approaches aiming to improve the models' ability to follow natural language instructions. Early work focused on training agents with reinforcement learning (RL) to execute language-like commands in simulated environments, but this often relied on synthetic, template-based instructions that lacked the variability of real-world human input \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09382)\]. To address this, researchers proposed instruction-tuning methods that fine-tune LLMs using data augmented with pseudo-code representations of natural language instructions, which improved instruction-following performance by up to 21% across multiple benchmarks \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18011)\]. Additionally, some studies explored the use of non-instructional data, such as random text from OpenWebText, to fine-tune LLMs, finding that this approach could still yield instruction-following capabilities comparable to models trained on instructional data \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. These findings highlight the potential of transfer learning and fine-tuning strategies in enhancing LLMs' ability to follow instructions, while also raising questions about the nature of what models learn from such training \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683)\]. Furthermore, recent work has examined how models utilize instruction data during fine-tuning, revealing that they may rely on superficial patterns rather than deep understanding, underscoring the need for more robust instruction-tuning methods \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11383)\]. Overall, these studies reflect a growing interest in improving the alignment of LLMs with human instructions through diverse training strategies.
