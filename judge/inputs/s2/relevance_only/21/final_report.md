## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction from 2D images, leveraging implicit neural representations to model complex geometry and appearance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. These methods typically take multiple views as input and optimize the reconstructed scene by minimizing the uncertainty in geometry and appearance across the views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. However, the reconstruction quality is often limited by the number of input views, particularly in complex and dynamic scenes where certain angles of objects are never captured \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. To address this limitation, researchers have explored various data augmentation techniques, including video frame interpolation, to generate additional views and improve the robustness of neural rendering models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\].

Video frame interpolation has been extensively studied as a means to enhance the temporal resolution of video sequences \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. Early methods focused on estimating optical flow between frames and synthesizing intermediate frames based on the estimated motion \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/23c9b2b3d315dd81563f57bbec8fda28d53700a6)\]. However, these approaches often suffer from artifacts and poor performance in the presence of complex motion patterns \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/23c9b2b3d315dd81563f57bbec8fda28d53700a6)\]. More recent works have proposed deep learning-based methods that integrate motion estimation and image synthesis into a single end-to-end pipeline, enabling higher quality and more robust interpolation \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14005)\]. For instance, Super SloMo \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09900)\] introduces a multi-frame interpolation approach that jointly models motion interpretation and occlusion reasoning, achieving consistent results across different motion scenarios \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09900)\].

In addition to improving interpolation quality, recent methods have also focused on reducing computational complexity and memory usage \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. For example, Video Frame Interpolation via Adaptive Separable Convolution \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\] formulates frame interpolation as a local separable convolution over input frames, significantly reducing the number of parameters and computational cost \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Similarly, Video Frame Interpolation Transformer \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\] leverages the self-attention mechanism of Transformers to enable content-aware aggregation and long-range dependency modeling, leading to improved performance on benchmark datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\].

The integration of scene priors into neural rendering has also been explored to improve the generalization and robustness of 3D reconstruction \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/43e2e7359854f0deb48f8e9ac4b0277c28b229e6)\]. For instance, Neural 3D Mesh Renderer \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\] proposes an approximate gradient for rasterization, enabling the integration of rendering into neural networks and facilitating single-image 3D mesh reconstruction \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\]. Similarly, 3D Reconstruction with Generalizable Neural Fields using Scene Priors \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\] introduces a method that maps single-view RGB-D images into signed distance and radiance values, allowing for fast adaptation to new scenes with fewer views \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\].

### <a id='category-neural-field-based-3d-reconstruction'></a>Neural Field-Based 3D Reconstruction: 7 papers found.
Neural field-based 3D reconstruction has seen significant advancements, particularly in addressing challenges related to shape-radiance ambiguity, dynamic scenes, and limited input views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. Implicit neural representations have demonstrated strong potential for offline 3D reconstruction and even online SLAM systems, though their application to autonomous 3D reconstruction, where a robot must explore and plan view paths, remains underexplored \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. To tackle this, researchers have proposed methods that use implicit neural representations to measure viewpoint quality and learn these criteria from data, enabling more effective autonomous reconstruction \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. Additionally, leveraging geometric priors such as Manhattan-world assumptions has helped improve the accuracy of implicit neural radiance fields, especially in indoor scenes with planar structures \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01331)\]. This approach allows for self-supervision of surface normals and orthogonality constraints, enhancing the quality of reconstructed scenes \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01331)\]. Furthermore, methods like Neural Fields incorporating scene priors (NFPs) have been introduced to enable fast adaptation to new scenes with fewer views, demonstrating scalability and efficiency \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. These techniques also support single-image novel-view synthesis, a less explored area in neural fields \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. For dynamic scenes, approaches that use topology-aware deformation and signed distance fields have been developed to learn complex dynamic surfaces without scene-specific priors, enabling high-fidelity reconstruction from multi-view videos \[[25](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/089f2072c95c244f3b7f4df404562bb4afe24448)\]. Moreover, the integration of planar constraints into implicit neural representations has improved the reconstruction of low-textured regions in indoor scenes, enhancing the overall quality of 3D reconstructions \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02836)\]. These developments highlight the growing trend of using implicit neural representations to overcome limitations in traditional 3D reconstruction methods, particularly in handling complex and dynamic environments.

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-methods'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Methods: 19 papers found.
Video frame interpolation methods have seen significant advancements, particularly with the integration of deep learning techniques. These methods aim to generate smooth transitions between frames, enhancing temporal resolution and visual quality. Early approaches relied on optical flow estimation and image synthesis, often using U-Net architectures for feature extraction and motion modeling \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14005)\]. More recent works have focused on improving efficiency and reducing artifacts by employing adaptive weight maps and residue refinement techniques \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. Some methods leverage implicit neural representations to model video as a space-time continuum, enabling interpolation at any temporal resolution \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/23c9b2b3d315dd81563f57bbec8fda28d53700a6)\]. Others introduce lightweight designs that minimize memory usage while maintaining high accuracy, especially for high-resolution videos \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09900)\]. Additionally, there are methods that combine motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a single process, using spatially adaptive convolution kernels to handle complex motion patterns \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\]. Unsupervised learning strategies have also been explored, allowing for the synthesis of high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs without requiring ground truth data \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01331)\]. These approaches collectively address key challenges in video frame interpolation, including motion estimation accuracy, artifact reduction, and computational efficiency.
