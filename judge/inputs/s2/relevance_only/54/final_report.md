## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommendation systems, with recent advancements leveraging graph neural networks (GNNs) to model user-item interactions more effectively \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Traditional CF methods often rely on matrix factorization, which can struggle with data sparsity and fail to capture high-order interactions \[[17](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4bbf796dc9639452da4dce4df3ecd9e3d1461581)\]. Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have emerged as a promising alternative, as they can model complex relationships in user-item graphs by aggregating information from neighbors \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. However, existing GCN-based methods face several limitations. For instance, they often suffer from over-smoothing, where high-order neighbor information dilutes the representation of users and items \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Additionally, the use of linear aggregation operations restricts the model's ability to capture non-linear patterns in user preferences \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. 

To address these issues, several works have explored different graph filtering techniques. LightGCN simplifies GCN by removing non-linearities and nonlinear activation functions, leading to improved performance and easier training \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, LR-GCCF introduces a residual network structure to alleviate over-smoothing and improve scalability \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. The work in \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10988)\] proposes a stacked graph filter approach, allowing for more adaptive and robust vertex classification by learning polynomial coefficients. This approach relaxes the low-frequency assumption, enabling the model to capture a broader range of spectral properties \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10988)\]. 

Another key challenge in graph-based CF is the feature leakage problem, where label information is inadvertently incorporated into node embeddings during the aggregation process \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8ea804423af4e3d0d9f8486d477a89c334c1fcb6)\]. This issue is particularly problematic in sparse interaction scenarios, as it can lead to poor generalization. To mitigate this, EFLEC proposes an accurate removal algorithm that evaluates embeddings without the influence of specific edges, significantly improving performance on sparse datasets \[[18](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8ea804423af4e3d0d9f8486d477a89c334c1fcb6)\]. 

Moreover, the design of graph filters has been a focal point for enhancing CF performance. PolyCF introduces a flexible graph signal filter that leverages polynomial graph filters to process interaction signals, capturing spectral features across multiple eigenspaces \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. This approach is complemented by the work in \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04755)\], which proposes Automatic Graph Convolutional Networks (AutoGCN) to automatically adjust the bandwidth of graph filters, capturing the full spectrum of graph signals \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04755)\]. 

In addition, the integration of contrastive learning with graph-based models has shown promise. SCCF introduces a simple yet effective algorithm based on a naive embedding model and a modified contrastive loss, demonstrating significant improvements across multiple benchmarks \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13996)\]. ### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks-in-recommendation-systems'></a>Graph Neural Networks in Recommendation Systems: 19 papers found.
Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have become a cornerstone in collaborative filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, leveraging the ability to model high-order user-item interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. However, existing GCN-based methods face significant limitations, such as the inability to effectively capture complex node dependencies and the over-smoothing problem when stacking multiple layers \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08827)\]. These issues hinder the model's capacity to learn rich and discriminative user and item embeddings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various improvements. For instance, LightGCN simplifies the GCN architecture by removing non-linear activation functions and focusing solely on neighborhood aggregation, leading to better performance and easier training \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. Similarly, NGCF introduces embedding propagation on the user-item graph to explicitly inject collaborative signals into the embedding process, enhancing the model's ability to capture high-order connectivity \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c0ee3379f6489049803d55c4b80f6b258515ed04)\]. Additionally, methods like RGCF refine the graph convolution structure to better handle non-semantic ID inputs and incorporate popularity features into the embedding learning process \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. The work in \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] further highlights the importance of integrating user-item interactions into the embedding process, proposing a framework that propagates embeddings on the graph to capture collaborative signals. Moreover, the over-smoothing problem is tackled through residual network structures and the removal of non-linearities, as demonstrated in \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04384)\]. These advancements collectively contribute to the evolving landscape of GCN-based recommendation systems, aiming to enhance both the expressiveness and efficiency of collaborative filtering models.

### <a id='category-spectral-graph-theory-applications'></a>Spectral Graph Theory Applications: 3 papers found.
The application of spectral graph theory in recommendation systems has seen significant advancements, particularly in addressing the cold-start problem and improving the modeling of user-item interactions. Recent work has focused on leveraging spectral domain operations to capture both proximity and connectivity information in bipartite graphs, enabling more accurate and robust recommendations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. For instance, Spectral Collaborative Filtering (SpectralCF) introduces a novel spectral convolution operation that operates directly in the spectral domain, allowing for the discovery of deep connections between users and items by exploiting the rich connectivity information inherent in the graph \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. This approach not only mitigates the cold-start problem but also outperforms existing state-of-the-art models on standard datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. Similarly, other studies have explored the generalization of deep learning models to non-Euclidean domains by introducing new spectral domain convolutional architectures that utilize parametric rational complex functions, such as Cayley polynomials, to generate localized and frequency-specific spectral filters \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07664)\]. These filters are designed to scale efficiently with input size and accommodate different Laplacian constructions, enhancing their applicability to diverse tasks like community detection and matrix completion \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07664)\]. Additionally, research on distributed graph filters has emphasized the importance of localized operations and edge-variant implementations, which reduce communication rounds while maintaining approximation accuracy \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03004)\]. These developments highlight the growing interest in spectral graph theory for enhancing the performance of recommendation systems and other graph-based applications.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-in-graph-based-models'></a>Contrastive Learning in Graph-Based Models: 3 papers found.
Graph-based models and contrastive learning have become increasingly prominent in Collaborative Filtering (CF) for recommendation systems, with recent research focusing on enhancing their theoretical foundations and practical effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. While many existing models integrate these methods, there is still limited depth in analyzing their underlying principles, particularly regarding how graph convolution interacts with contrastive learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. This gap has led to the development of frameworks that bridge these concepts, such as Simple Contrastive Collaborative Filtering (SCCF), which leverages a naive embedding model and a modified contrastive loss to achieve high-order connectivity modeling without relying on graph convolutional layers \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Additionally, the challenge of distinguishing between fascinating and unfascinating items in user-item interaction graphs has prompted the introduction of Feedback Reciprocal Graph Collaborative Filtering (FRGCF), which partitions interactions into separate graphs based on user feedback and employs feedback-reciprocal contrastive learning to improve recommendation quality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08827)\]. Furthermore, the robustness of neural graph collaborative filtering against noisy interactions has been addressed by integrating both structural denoising and embedding perturbation techniques, combined with contrastive learning to enhance model performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. These advancements highlight the evolving landscape of graph-based CF, emphasizing the importance of theoretical insights and practical improvements in leveraging contrastive learning for more accurate and robust recommendations.
