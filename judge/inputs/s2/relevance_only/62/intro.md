## Related Works

Generative retrieval has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional retrieval methods, which rely on external indices and separate encoding and retrieval stages \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. Instead, generative retrieval consolidates all information from a document corpus into a single model, enabling the direct generation of document identifiers (docids) in response to a query \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. This paradigm has been explored in various applications, including book search \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11034)\], e-commerce search \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19829)\], and knowledge-intensive language tasks \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07652)\]. However, despite its potential, generative retrieval faces significant challenges, particularly when scaling to large corpora and ensuring robust generalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\].

Early work on generative retrieval focused on the use of sequence-to-sequence models to map queries to relevant docids \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5beaeee1e27007bd12967480bb9c379cebd15e6e)\]. This approach has been extended to incorporate hierarchical information in book search, where outline-based encoding helps maintain the structure of book content \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11034)\]. Similarly, in e-commerce search, generative retrieval has been adapted to handle the complexity of item titles and long-tail queries by using multi-span identifiers and preference optimization \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.19829)\]. These methods highlight the flexibility of generative retrieval but also reveal limitations in handling complex, multi-faceted information without additional architectural modifications.

A key challenge in generative retrieval is the alignment of the autoregressive generation process with the desired ranking of documents \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. This misalignment leads to a learning gap, where the model's objective of generating docids does not directly correspond to the goal of ranking passages \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed learning-to-rank frameworks that optimize the model toward the final passage ranking target \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15222)\]. Additionally, the use of constrained decoding has been explored to improve the accuracy and interpretability of generated docids, particularly in scenarios where the model must retrieve fine-grained evidence from a corpus \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11919)\].

Another important consideration is the scalability of generative retrieval to large-scale corpora. While early studies evaluated generative retrieval on datasets with up to 100k documents, recent work has pushed the boundaries by scaling to millions of passages \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. This scaling has revealed several challenges, including the importance of synthetic queries during indexing and the limitations of naively increasing model parameters \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11841)\]. Furthermore, the use of discrete auto-regressive biasing has been proposed to improve the control of text generation while maintaining fluency \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03685)\].
