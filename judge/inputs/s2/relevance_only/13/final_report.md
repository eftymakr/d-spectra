## Related Works

Long-tailed recognition remains a challenging problem in computer vision due to the significant imbalance in class distribution, where a few dominant (head) classes occupy most of the data while the majority of classes (tail) are underrepresented \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. This imbalance leads to biased feature spaces and poor generalization for tail classes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. Various approaches have been proposed to address this issue, primarily focusing on data augmentation, loss reweighting, and contrastive learning strategies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\].

Contrastive learning has shown promising results in representation learning, particularly in balanced datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. However, its application to long-tailed recognition has been limited due to the inherent biases introduced by class imbalance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. Supervised contrastive learning (SCL) has been widely used, as it pulls positive samples and pushes negative samples apart to optimize embeddings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. However, in long-tailed scenarios, treating positive samples equally leads to biased optimization for intra-class distances \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. This issue is further compounded by the lack of consideration for the semantic relationships among negative samples, which are often ignored by SCL \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\].

To address these limitations, several works have introduced modifications to contrastive learning. For instance, Rebalanced Contrastive Learning (RCL) was proposed to address class imbalance by enforcing feature space balancedness, intra-class compactness, and regularization for tail classes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. Similarly, Decoupled Contrastive Learning (DCL) was introduced to decouple the training objectives for head and tail classes, improving performance on long-tailed recognition benchmarks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. The method also incorporates patch-based self-distillation to transfer knowledge from head to tail classes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\].

Another approach, Subclass-Balancing Contrastive Learning (SBCL), clusters head classes into subclasses to enforce a two-layer class hierarchy, capturing both instance and subclass-level balance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15925)\]. This method avoids overemphasizing tail classes while preserving the semantic substructures of head classes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15925)\]. Similarly, Long-Tailed Augmented Graph Contrastive Learning (LAGCL) was proposed for recommendation systems, addressing the long-tail effect in graph-based models by introducing a learnable long-tail augmentation approach \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11177)\].

The problem of gradient imbalance in long-tailed recognition has also been explored. Equalization Losses \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05566)\] and Equalization Loss v2 \[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08548)\] were introduced to re-balance gradients between positive and negative samples, improving performance on long-tailed object detection and classification tasks. These methods dynamically adjust the training process to achieve balanced gradient ratios \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05566)\].

### <a id='category-gradient-balancing-techniques-for-long-tailed-recognition'></a>Gradient Balancing Techniques for Long-Tailed Recognition: 3 papers found.
In the context of long-tailed recognition, gradient balancing techniques have emerged as a critical approach to address the performance disparity among tail categories. Research has shown that imbalanced gradients—both positive (from same-category samples) and negative (from other categories)—are a primary cause of suboptimal model performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. To mitigate this, methods such as equalization losses have been introduced, which dynamically re-balance the positive/negative gradient ratios during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. These losses aim to achieve a more uniform training process by adjusting the contribution of each category based on accumulated gradients \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01753)\]. However, early implementations like equalization loss (EQL) faced limitations, particularly in long-tailed object detection, where they underperformed compared to decoupled training methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. To address this, EQL v2 was proposed, introducing a more refined gradient-guided reweighing mechanism that independently balances training for each category, leading to significant improvements in both overall and rare category performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06151)\]. Additionally, alternative approaches such as reflecting learning have been explored, which integrate processes like reviewing past predictions, summarizing feature relations, and correcting gradient conflicts to enhance learning from imbalanced distributions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.15925)\]. These methods highlight the ongoing efforts to improve gradient dynamics in long-tailed recognition, with a focus on achieving more balanced and effective training strategies.
