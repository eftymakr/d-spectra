## Related Works

Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) have emerged as a promising approach to capture user preferences through multi-turn interactions, offering a more dynamic and personalized recommendation experience compared to traditional static models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00646)\]. Early research primarily focused on one-shot interactions, where user preferences were inferred based on historical data, and recommendations were presented as a ranked list \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00646)\]. However, this approach was limited in its ability to adapt to evolving user preferences and engage in meaningful dialogue \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00646)\].

Recent advances in natural language processing (NLP) and chatbot technology have enabled more sophisticated conversational interactions, allowing users to ask questions, provide feedback, and refine their preferences in real-time \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00646)\]. This has led to the development of conversational recommendation systems that support richer user interactions, such as clarifying questions and explicit attribute-based feedback \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05783)\]. However, evaluating these systems remains challenging, particularly in offline settings where user simulators are commonly used \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05783)\].

User simulators are essential for training and evaluating CRSs, as they simulate user behavior and provide feedback to the system \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05783)\]. However, traditional simulators often focus on a single target item, which can lead to an overestimation of the system's effectiveness and an underrepresentation of the exploratory nature of recommendation systems \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05783)\]. To address this, recent work has introduced improved simulators that allow users to consider alternative items and exhibit varying levels of patience \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05783)\]. These simulators have shown significant improvements in evaluating CRS models, particularly in scenarios where users are allowed to explore multiple options \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05783)\].

To further enhance the interaction between the system and the user, researchers have explored the use of generative models to simulate user feedback, enabling more natural and realistic conversations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13874)\]. These models can provide both coarse-grained and fine-grained feedback, such as generative item scoring and attribute-based critiques, which help the system better understand user preferences \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13874)\]. Additionally, the use of beam search and efficient candidate ranking methods has been proposed to balance effectiveness and efficiency in the interaction process \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13874)\].

The integration of knowledge graphs and federated learning has also been explored to improve the accuracy and privacy of CRSs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04032)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00999)\]. Knowledge graphs help bridge the semantic gap between natural language and item-level preferences, while federated learning enables decentralized training and deployment, preserving user privacy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04032)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00999)\]. Furthermore, the use of reinforcement learning has been proposed to optimize the decision-making process in CRSs, including when to ask for attribute information and when to recommend items \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.09710)\].
