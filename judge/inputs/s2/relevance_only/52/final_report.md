## Related Works

Dense retrieval (DR) has emerged as a powerful approach in information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to generate dense vector representations of text that can be compared efficiently \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. These representations are typically learned through contrastive learning, where the model is trained to align relevant text pairs and push apart irrelevant ones \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. The effectiveness of DR relies heavily on the quality of these representations, which are often fine-tuned from pre-trained models like BERT or LLaMA \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\].

Recent studies have questioned the role of fine-tuning versus pre-training in dense retrievers, arguing that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during pre-training, and that fine-tuning only adjusts neuron activations rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. This claim was initially tested using BERT-based encoders with DPR as a representative dense retriever \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\]. However, this analysis was limited in scope, focusing only on a specific encoder architecture and dataset \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. 

To extend this analysis, we examine different representation approaches, such as using CLS tokens versus mean pooling, and compare encoder-only models like BERT with decoder-only models like LLaMA \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. We also test additional datasets, including MSMARCO and Natural Questions, to evaluate the generalizability of these findings \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.05934)\]. Our results confirm that pre-trained knowledge underpins retrieval performance in DPR tuning, with fine-tuning primarily adjusting activation rather than reorganizing knowledge \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\]. However, this pattern does not hold universally, as seen in mean-pooled models like Contriever and decoder-based models like LLaMA \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c7f8acb0bdae40eeb0505bf87744fa29270073ac)\].

These methods often focus on improving the quality of text representations by leveraging contrastive learning strategies \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2f184d2e7182dd69dfb708aa088ada7abc47597d)\].

Contrastive learning has emerged as a critical approach in dense retrieval models, aiming to improve the quality of text representations and retrieval performance. Recent studies have explored various strategies to enhance the discriminative power of representations, such as using autoencoder-based language models with lightweight decoders \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\], and introducing contrastive pre-training to generate pseudo-positive examples from unlabeled data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. ### <a id='category-pre-trained-language-models-in-dense-retrieval'></a>Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval: 11 papers found.
The category "Pre-trained Language Models in Dense Retrieval" explores advancements in leveraging pre-trained models for dense retrieval tasks, focusing on improving representation learning, training efficiency, and robustness. Research highlights the challenges of fine-tuning dense retrievers, which often require extensive data engineering and large batches to effectively learn embedding spaces \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. To address these issues, novel architectures like Condenser and coCondenser have been proposed, where Condenser pre-trains models to condense information into dense vectors, and coCondenser introduces an unsupervised contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Additionally, studies have shown that standard LMs' attention structures are not inherently suitable for dense encoders, prompting the development of new Transformer architectures that better align with the requirements of dense retrieval \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. Autoencoder-based models have also been explored, where the encoder is trained to produce high-quality embeddings that can reconstruct input texts, though issues like decoder shortcuts and representation anisotropy have been identified \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. To mitigate these, methods such as variational auto-encoder latent space sampling and supervised contrastive learning have been introduced to generate isotropic and uniform sentence embeddings \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.00511)\]. Furthermore, recent work has focused on adapting large language models (LLMs) for dense retrieval through unsupervised adaptation techniques, such as Llama2Vec, which introduces pretext tasks to align LLMs with dense retrieval requirements \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02616)\]. These studies collectively emphasize the importance of pre-training strategies, architectural innovations, and contrastive learning in enhancing the performance of dense retrievers while reducing the need for extensive fine-tuning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\].

The Role of Fine-tuning vs. Pre-training

Fine-tuning pre-trained contextualized embedding models has become an integral part of the NLP pipeline, with studies highlighting its impact on the linguistic knowledge encoded by these models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Research has shown that fine-tuning can lead to substantial changes in probing accuracy for certain tasks, suggesting it may introduce or remove linguistic knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. This effect varies across different models, fine-tuning strategies, and probing tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]. Additionally, large language models (LLMs) rely heavily on pre-trained weights for factual knowledge, but this knowledge is limited by the training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. While unsupervised fine-tuning offers some improvement, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) consistently outperforms it, especially for both existing and new knowledge \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Furthermore, the pre-train-then-align paradigm, where models are fine-tuned to align with human preferences or downstream tasks, reveals that continual pre-training can improve models in ways that become evident after fine-tuning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. However, fine-tuning may also lead to forgetting previously known domain knowledge, particularly for tasks not encountered during the fine-tuning phase \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\]. These findings underscore the complex interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning, emphasizing the need for careful interpretation of their effects on model performance and knowledge retention \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09846)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03166)\].
