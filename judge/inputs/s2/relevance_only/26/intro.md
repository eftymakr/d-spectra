## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy protection in data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, especially in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These vulnerabilities include incorrect noise sampling or generating correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Several works have addressed these issues by proposing verifiable differentially private mechanisms.

Verifiable differential privacy (VDPP) has been explored in the local model, where clients verify the correctness of their randomized responses \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Similarly, the shuffle model has been studied for its ability to disconnect clients from the aggregator, enhancing privacy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. However, these approaches often require additional assumptions or are not efficient enough for practical deployment \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. 

The idea of incorporating zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) into DP mechanisms has been investigated. ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, but they are not necessary \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. This insight is consistent with prior work that has shown how ZKPs can be used to verify the correctness of DP mechanisms \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. For instance, interactive proofs for DP have been proposed to ensure that the output is both differentially private and reliable \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. 

In the context of local differential privacy (LDP), several mechanisms have been proposed to prevent malicious clients from manipulating their outputs. For example, verifiable randomization mechanisms have been introduced to ensure that the data collector can verify the completeness of the randomization process \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These mechanisms are effective against output manipulation attacks and are more robust than previous countermeasures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. 

In the shuffle model, recent studies have shown that existing protocols are vulnerable to local data poisoning attacks and collusion attacks \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07362)\]. To address these issues, the augmented shuffle model has been proposed, allowing the shuffler to perform additional operations such as random sampling and dummy data addition \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07362)\]. This approach improves the robustness of shuffle protocols against adversarial attacks.

Efforts to optimize the utility of DP mechanisms have also been explored. For instance, per-instance differential privacy (pDP) has been introduced to measure privacy loss on a per-instance basis, leading to more efficient noise addition \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15686)\]. Similarly, utility-optimized LDP mechanisms have been proposed to provide equivalent privacy guarantees while improving the utility of the results \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.11317)\].
