## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for privacy protection in data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for privacy, its implementation is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, especially in distributed settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These vulnerabilities include incorrect noise sampling or generating correlated noise while appearing to follow established protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Several works have addressed these issues by proposing verifiable differentially private mechanisms.

Verifiable differential privacy (VDPP) has been explored in the local model, where clients verify the correctness of their randomized responses \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. Similarly, the shuffle model has been studied for its ability to disconnect clients from the aggregator, enhancing privacy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. However, these approaches often require additional assumptions or are not efficient enough for practical deployment \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. 

The idea of incorporating zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) into DP mechanisms has been investigated. ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, but they are not necessary \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. This insight is consistent with prior work that has shown how ZKPs can be used to verify the correctness of DP mechanisms \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. For instance, interactive proofs for DP have been proposed to ensure that the output is both differentially private and reliable \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. 

In the context of local differential privacy (LDP), several mechanisms have been proposed to prevent malicious clients from manipulating their outputs. For example, verifiable randomization mechanisms have been introduced to ensure that the data collector can verify the completeness of the randomization process \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These mechanisms are effective against output manipulation attacks and are more robust than previous countermeasures \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. 

In the shuffle model, recent studies have shown that existing protocols are vulnerable to local data poisoning attacks and collusion attacks \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07362)\]. To address these issues, the augmented shuffle model has been proposed, allowing the shuffler to perform additional operations such as random sampling and dummy data addition \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07362)\]. This approach improves the robustness of shuffle protocols against adversarial attacks.

For instance, per-instance differential privacy (pDP) has been introduced to measure privacy loss on a per-instance basis, leading to more efficient noise addition \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.15686)\]. ### <a id='category-verifiable-differential-privacy-(vdp)-frameworks'></a>Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) Frameworks: 3 papers found.
Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) frameworks aim to address the vulnerabilities in differential privacy (DP) by introducing mechanisms to verify the correctness of DP implementations. These frameworks ensure that the randomness and noise introduced by DP mechanisms are faithfully generated and that the results are both private and reliable \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. One approach involves Interactive Proofs for Differential Privacy, which requires a zero-knowledge proof to confirm that the output adheres to DP principles without revealing the randomness itself \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. This is achieved by combining private and public randomness, enabling verifiable DP counting queries with theoretical guarantees \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. Another method, shadow execution, allows for generating proofs of differential privacy with minimal programmer annotations, reducing the complexity of verification and enabling the verification of sophisticated algorithms like Report Noisy Max and a new variant of Sparse Vector \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\]. Additionally, SampCert provides a mechanized foundation for differential privacy, offering formally verified implementations of Laplace and Gaussian sampling algorithms, which are critical for avoiding vulnerabilities in real-world DP systems \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These approaches highlight the growing trend toward usability and expressiveness in formal verification of DP, ensuring both correctness and practical deployment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\].

The category "Adversarial Attacks and Countermeasures in Privacy Mechanisms" focuses on vulnerabilities and defenses within privacy-preserving frameworks like Local Differential Privacy (LDP) and Differential Privacy (DP). Researchers have identified that LDP protocols, which rely on local perturbation of data before transmission, are susceptible to malicious data providers who can manipulate outputs to skew statistical results \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These attacks exploit the normalization process based on randomization probabilities, allowing attackers to control estimations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Similarly, DP mechanisms, such as the Gaussian and Laplace mechanisms, face implementation flaws, including floating-point representation attacks and timing side channels, which can compromise privacy guarantees by revealing sensitive information \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\]. These vulnerabilities highlight the need for robust countermeasures, such as verifiable randomization mechanisms that enable data collectors to verify the correctness of randomization processes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Additionally, data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users to manipulate frequency estimates or heavy hitter identification, further underscore the security challenges in LDP protocols \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed solutions like zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) to ensure verifiability while maintaining privacy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. However, ZKPs are not strictly necessary, and alternative approaches, such as verifiable distributed differential privacy (VDDP), have been developed to enhance security in distributed settings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. These advancements aim to improve both the utility and security of privacy-preserving mechanisms by introducing verification layers and efficient proof generation techniques \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\].

The category "Per-Instance Differential Privacy (pDP) Approaches" focuses on improving the utility of differential privacy (DP) by addressing the issue of excessive noise addition in traditional DP mechanisms, particularly for small datasets. Unlike conventional DP, which applies a uniform privacy budget across all data instances and often leads to significant utility loss, pDP measures privacy loss on a per-instance basis, allowing for more tailored noise addition. This approach aims to balance privacy and utility by optimizing noise variance for each data instance individually \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. The concept of pDP is framed as a sequential game, where the goal is to find a Nash equilibrium that ensures privacy for each instance while preserving data utility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. This method has shown promising results, with experiments demonstrating up to a 99.53% improvement in performance compared to traditional DP, as measured by KL divergence \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Additionally, there is a related body of work that explores partial differential privacy, which allows for per-attribute privacy guarantees that are smaller than the overall privacy budget for a full record \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. This suggests that pDP and its variants are part of a broader trend toward more flexible and instance-specific privacy mechanisms.
