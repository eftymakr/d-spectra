## Related Works

Fairness evaluation in machine learning has garnered significant attention, particularly in the context of large language models (LLMs), due to their increasing integration into high-stakes applications. Traditional fairness metrics often focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, such as Equalized Odds \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\], which may fail to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty. This limitation has been highlighted in several studies, which demonstrate that conventional metrics overlook the nuanced ways in which models express bias \[[2](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f64670a5f54fcce339a916497a001cbf02a9a04f)\]. For instance, models may exhibit high confidence in incorrect predictions, leading to unfair outcomes that are not detected by traditional fairness measures \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10223)\].

To address these limitations, recent work has proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metrics. One such approach is the introduction of metrics that incorporate model confidence into fairness evaluation, allowing for a more granular assessment of bias \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13738)\]. This aligns with the findings of a study that showed fairness risks cannot be reliably assessed from benchmark performance alone, as results on one prompt dataset may overstate or understate risks for another \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04053)\]. This underscores the importance of context-specific fairness evaluation, which is also emphasized in frameworks that map use cases to appropriate metrics based on task type and stakeholder priorities \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04422)\].

In addition to fairness metrics, the quality of evaluation datasets has been identified as a critical factor in fairness assessment. Current datasets often suffer from issues related to data size, diversity, and clarity, which can hinder the accurate evaluation of LLMs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\]. To address this, researchers have developed new datasets that offer more diverse and suitable benchmarks for evaluating modern LLMs. For example, a recent study introduced a gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset with 31,756 samples, specifically designed for co-reference resolution \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03015)\]. This dataset provides a more robust foundation for assessing fairness in LLMs, particularly in scenarios involving gender and occupation stereotypes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\].

Several studies have also explored the effectiveness of fairness-enhancing interventions in machine learning. These interventions can be categorized into pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing methods, each aiming to mitigate bias at different stages of the model development pipeline \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00770)\]. In-processing methods, in particular, have gained traction as they directly incorporate fairness considerations into model design, leading to more intrinsically fair models \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\]. However, fairness-preserving algorithms are often sensitive to dataset composition and preprocessing, indicating that fairness interventions may be more brittle than previously thought \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07887)\].
