## Related Works

Representation learning has become a cornerstone in modern recommender systems, enabling efficient and effective user-item matching through vectorized embeddings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Early approaches primarily relied on matrix factorization, which decomposes user-item interaction matrices into latent factors \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04163)\]. However, these methods often struggle with scalability and sparsity, prompting the development of more sophisticated models such as deep learning-based approaches \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.02801)\]. Recent research has also explored the integration of graph structures into collaborative filtering, leveraging the rich relational information present in user-item interactions \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03502)\].

Graph-based collaborative filtering has gained traction due to its ability to model high-order interactions and capture latent patterns in user-item relationships \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03502)\]. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) explicitly incorporates the bipartite graph structure by propagating embeddings across the graph, leading to more expressive and accurate representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Multi-Graph Convolution Collaborative Filtering (Multi-GCCF) extends this idea by integrating multiple graphs, including user-user and item-item relationships, to better capture proximal and high-order interactions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These models demonstrate the importance of embedding propagation in capturing collaborative signals \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Binarization of embeddings has also been explored as a means to reduce memory usage and computational overhead, particularly in resource-constrained environments \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. Binarized Collaborative Filtering with Distilling Graph Convolutional Networks (Binarized GCN) introduces a framework that converts binary constrained optimization into a continuous optimization problem, enabling efficient training with popular solvers like SGD and Adam \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. This approach addresses the challenges of information loss during binarization, which is a common issue in existing quantization-based methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\].

Furthermore, the use of graph attention mechanisms has been proposed to enhance collaborative similarity learning by capturing both explicit and implicit relationships in the user-item graph \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03135)\]. The Graph Attention Collaborative Similarity Embedding (GACSE) model incorporates attention mechanisms to learn user-item associations and auxiliary losses to capture user-user and item-item similarities, leading to improved recommendation performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03135)\]. These models highlight the importance of incorporating attention and auxiliary tasks to enhance the learning of collaborative signals \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03135)\].

### <a id='category-graph-based-collaborative-filtering'></a>Graph-Based Collaborative Filtering: 11 papers found.
Graph-Based Collaborative Filtering has emerged as a powerful approach in recommendation systems, leveraging user-item interaction data to learn more accurate and expressive embeddings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Unlike traditional methods that rely solely on pre-defined features, recent works integrate the bipartite graph structure directly into the embedding process, allowing for the explicit modeling of high-order connectivity and collaborative signals \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. For instance, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) propagates embeddings on the user-item graph, significantly improving performance over models like HOP-Rec and Collaborative Memory Network \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Similarly, Graph Attention Collaborative Similarity Embedding (GACSE) introduces attention mechanisms and auxiliary losses to capture both explicit and implicit collaborative information, leading to superior results on benchmark datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03135)\]. Addressing data sparsity, methods such as the light RGCN-based collaborative filtering approach utilize NLP models to incorporate textual information, enhancing embeddings with semantic richness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Additionally, the use of graph neural networks (GNNs) has shown great potential, though challenges like over-smoothing and scalability remain \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. To mitigate these, SimRec combines knowledge distillation and contrastive learning, achieving better efficiency and performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06120)\]. Furthermore, models like Weighted Graph-based Collaborative Filtering (W-GCF) and Attentive Graph-based Collaborative Filtering (A-GCF) address limitations in implicit feedback handling by considering both user and item-side signals and learning adaptive weights \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07027)\]. These advancements collectively highlight the evolving landscape of graph-based collaborative filtering, emphasizing the integration of graph structures, attention mechanisms, and semantic-rich embeddings to enhance recommendation accuracy and robustness.

### <a id='category-embedding-representation-techniques'></a>Embedding Representation Techniques: 4 papers found.
Graph representation learning models aim to encode graph structure and features into low-dimensional vectors, enabling downstream tasks like node classification and link prediction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. These models have gained traction due to their ability to capture complex relationships within graphs, ranging from homogeneous to heterogeneous structures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01829)\]. Recent advancements include matrix factorization-based methods, random-walk algorithms, and graph neural networks, which automatically learn embeddings without relying on manual feature engineering \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Notably, inductive frameworks such as GraphSAGE address the limitation of transductive models by generating embeddings for unseen nodes through local neighborhood aggregation \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03135)\]. Additionally, collaborative filtering has benefited from distributional representation models that jointly learn user and item embeddings, achieving state-of-the-art performance in recommendation tasks \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09065)\]. The proposed BiGeaR++ framework further advances this by incorporating pseudo-positive samples and fine-grained inference distillation to mitigate information loss during binarization, leading to improved performance in collaborative filtering \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3c8fba26e95c922d2616ecfb891de2542d01aea8)\].
