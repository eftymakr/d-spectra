## Related Works

Traditional approaches to addressing economic inequality often rely on static tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, which lack the adaptability needed to respond to dynamic economic conditions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\]. These systems are typically designed with fixed tax brackets and rates, which may not effectively balance equity and productivity \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\]. In contrast, models like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework attempt to dynamically adjust tax rates based on economic conditions, but they often fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\]. This limitation highlights the need for more flexible and adaptive tax policy frameworks.

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has emerged as a powerful tool for simulating complex economic systems and exploring the effects of policy interventions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. ABMs allow researchers to model heterogeneous agents, such as households and firms, and study their interactions within a simulated economy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. This approach is particularly useful for understanding how different tax policies might affect economic outcomes, as it enables the simulation of diverse agent behaviors and responses \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. However, traditional ABMs often rely on heuristic decision-making rules or regression-based models, which may not fully capture the complexity of real-world economic behavior \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\].

Recent advances in integrating large language models (LLMs) into agent-based modeling have opened new possibilities for simulating more realistic and adaptive economic systems \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. LLMs can provide agents with rich contextual knowledge and the ability to generate more nuanced decision-making strategies \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11970)\]. For instance, the ABIDES-Economist framework demonstrates how LLMs can be used to model economic agents with heterogeneous behaviors, enabling more accurate simulations of real-world economic systems \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09563)\]. This aligns with the goals of the TaxAgent framework, which leverages LLMs to iteratively optimize tax rates based on real-time economic data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\].

The use of reinforcement learning (RL) in ABMs has also gained traction as a means of improving the accuracy and adaptability of agent behavior \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. RL models allow agents to learn from their environment and adapt their strategies over time, which is particularly useful in dynamic economic settings \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. This approach has been applied to simulate economic systems, such as the minority game and influenza transmission models, demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing complex economic behaviors \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. The TaxAgent framework builds on this by using LLMs to provide agents with the ability to learn and adapt to changing economic conditions \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18693)\].

In addition to these developments, research on agent-based models of tax compliance and evasion has highlighted the importance of social and behavioral factors in shaping taxpayer behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\].
