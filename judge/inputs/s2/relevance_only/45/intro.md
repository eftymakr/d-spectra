## Related Works

Opinion mining has gained significant attention in software engineering, with applications ranging from identifying developers’ emotions in code comments to extracting user critiques from mobile apps \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e0a7e1c24edc628b093daf4dca1dca86a583c377)\]. While most studies focus on sentiment polarity (e.g., positive, negative, neutral), fine-grained emotion classification in app reviews remains underexplored \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. This gap is addressed by several recent works that have explored emotion annotation in various domains, including social media, literature, and app reviews \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02256)\].

Fine-grained emotion classification has been extensively studied in the context of Reddit comments, where the GoEmotions dataset was introduced with 58k manually annotated English comments labeled for 27 emotion categories or Neutral \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. This dataset has been used to evaluate the performance of BERT-based models, achieving an average F1-score of 0.46, indicating room for improvement \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00547)\]. Similarly, the SemEval 2016 dataset was extended with emotion annotations, and the reliability of annotation merging was analyzed \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7d0531aaaa4dbf4033da964289843e7812262702)\]. These efforts highlight the challenges of creating high-quality, fine-grained emotion datasets.

The use of large language models (LLMs) for emotion annotation has also been explored, particularly with GPT-4, which has shown promising results in human evaluation studies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. However, differences between human and LLM annotations have been observed, underscoring the importance of human input in annotation studies \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906)\]. This aligns with findings from the CROWDSOURCING A WORD–EMOTION ASSOCIATION LEXICON study, which demonstrated that crowdsourcing can be used to generate high-quality emotion lexicons, though challenges such as malicious data entry and annotator unfamiliarity with terms must be addressed \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.6297)\].

In the context of app reviews, several studies have focused on feature extraction and sentiment analysis, often relying on manual annotation \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a0df60b33f3fae7685f1d8f4236e2b5e7bc65693)\]. The quality of automatic feature extraction from app reviews depends on annotation guidelines and training data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05187)\]. For example, the Impact of Annotation Guidelines and Annotated Data on Extracting App Features from App Reviews study showed that changes to annotation guidelines can reduce noise and improve the informativeness of extracted features \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.05187)\]. Additionally, the use of encoder-only large language models for feature extraction has been explored, with empirical evaluations showing improvements in precision and recall \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.01063)\].
