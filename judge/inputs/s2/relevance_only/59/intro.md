## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has garnered significant attention in recent years, driven by the increasing societal impact of recommendation systems and the growing awareness of algorithmic biases. Prior research has primarily focused on the trade-off between ranking accuracy and fairness, often treating these as opposing objectives \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826)\]. This tension has been explored in various contexts, including recommendation systems, where fairness considerations have been primarily directed toward user-side outcomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. However, recent work has begun to shift focus toward item-side fairness, recognizing that ranking algorithms can also introduce biases in how items are exposed to users \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\].

Several studies have examined the accuracy-fairness trade-off in recommendation systems, highlighting the challenges of balancing these competing objectives. For instance, \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/469a0611bccbed8007875354bdf253b39bc63d95)\] introduces a fairness-aware re-ranking approach that learns individual preferences across multiple fairness dimensions, aiming to achieve a better trade-off between accuracy and fairness. Similarly, \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/13b0444d079bea1c8c57a6082200b67ab5f4616e)\] proposes a re-ranking algorithm that balances ranking quality and provider-side fairness, incorporating personalized user tolerance towards provider diversification. These works demonstrate that fairness considerations must be integrated into the ranking process, rather than treated as post-hoc adjustments.

The concept of fairness in rankings has also been extended to include exposure fairness, which ensures that items receive appropriate visibility based on their utility \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01203)\]. This idea is closely related to the notion of item-side fairness, where the exposure of an item is considered in relation to its utility, rather than solely based on user interactions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04250)\]. In this context, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14713)\] introduces a fairness-aware re-ranking method that uses a labeled dataset to incorporate fairness with respect to demographic groups, demonstrating that even a limited amount of labeled data can be used to compute fair representations.

Moreover, the relationship between fairness and accuracy has been analyzed from a causal perspective, with \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06840)\] introducing the notion of a path-specific excess loss (PSEL) to quantify how much the predictor's loss increases when a causal fairness constraint is enforced. This approach provides a more nuanced understanding of the trade-offs involved in enforcing fairness constraints \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.15443)\].

In addition, the idea of amortizing fairness across multiple rankings has been explored, with \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.03577)\] proposing a method that ensures individual fairness by distributing attention proportionally to relevance. This approach is particularly relevant in the context of two-sided markets, where both users and item providers are stakeholders \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.01132)\].
