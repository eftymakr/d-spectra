## Related Works

Natural language (NL)-driven table discovery has gained significant attention in recent years due to the increasing availability of tabular data repositories. Traditional methods typically follow a representation-index-search pipeline, where tables are encoded into dense vectors, indexed, and then retrieved based on semantic similarity with queries \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15849)\]. However, these methods face several limitations, including error accumulation during the indexing phase and insufficient query-table interaction, which hinders effective semantic alignment \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09207)\]. 

Several works have explored deep learning-based approaches for table retrieval. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15849)\] investigates the use of BERT for ad hoc table retrieval, emphasizing the importance of encoding table structure and handling input length constraints. While BERT-based models have shown promising results, they often suffer from performance degradation when dealing with complex table layouts or multi-granularity queries \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.01736)\]. 

To address these challenges, \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.01736)\] proposes Graph-based Table Retrieval (GTR), which converts tables into tabular graphs and uses a Graph Transformer to capture both content and layout structures. This approach improves generalization over complex tables and supports cross-dataset generalization. However, it still relies on traditional indexing and retrieval pipelines, which can introduce error accumulation during the indexing phase \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09207)\]. 

The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) framework has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional indexing-retrieval pipelines. DSI encodes the entire corpus into a single neural model, allowing direct mapping from queries to document identifiers \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. However, DSI faces challenges in handling data distribution mismatches between indexing and retrieval phases, particularly in cross-lingual settings \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. To mitigate this, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\] introduces DSI-QG, which generates relevant queries during indexing to better align with retrieval queries. This approach improves retrieval performance by addressing the mismatch between long documents and short queries \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.10128)\]. 

Another key limitation of existing methods is the inability to handle dynamic updates to the index. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.10323)\] proposes IncDSI, an incrementally updatable DSI model that allows for real-time addition of new documents without retraining the entire model. This significantly reduces computational overhead while maintaining retrieval accuracy \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.10323)\]. 

In addition to addressing these limitations, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02788)\] explores the importance of diversity in retrieval results, proposing a method inspired by Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) to balance relevance and novelty. This approach enhances the diversity of retrieved results without compromising relevance, making it suitable for sub-topic retrieval tasks \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02788)\].

### <a id='category-differentiable-search-index-frameworks'></a>Differentiable Search Index Frameworks: 5 papers found.
Differentiable Search Index (DSI) frameworks have emerged as a novel paradigm in information retrieval, aiming to unify indexing and retrieval into a single differentiable model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02073)\]. These frameworks, such as the original DSI \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02073)\], leverage neural architectures to directly map queries to document identifiers, eliminating the need for traditional index-retrieval pipelines \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\]. However, early implementations faced challenges in distinguishing relevant documents from random ones, impacting retrieval effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09207)\]. To address this, researchers proposed multi-task distillation approaches to enhance retrieval quality without altering the model structure \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09207)\]. Additionally, semantic enhancements were introduced, such as the Semantic-Enhanced DSI (SE-DSI), which uses elaborative descriptions and rehearsal strategies inspired by cognitive psychology to improve document memorization and retrieval \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\]. More recently, decentralized variants like De-DSI have been developed, integrating large language models (LLMs) with decentralized architectures to enable efficient query-docid mapping without direct document access \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12237)\]. These models often employ ensemble techniques and beam search to aggregate results from multiple shards, improving scalability and accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12237)\]. Furthermore, efforts have been made to balance relevance and diversity in DSI training, inspired by Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR), allowing models to generate diverse yet relevant results without post-processing \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02788)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing evolution of DSI frameworks, addressing key limitations in accuracy, scalability, and semantic alignment \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02073)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15115)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12237)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02788)\].

Recent work has highlighted the limitations of traditional dense vector search pipelines, which suffer from error accumulation during representation and indexing, as well as insufficient query-table interaction that hinders semantic alignment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15849)\]. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed neural architectures that integrate semantic understanding with table retrieval, such as Neural Enquirer, which uses distributed representations of queries and knowledge-base tables to execute complex natural language queries \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00965)\]. Similarly, a neural architecture incorporating both semantic and relevance matching has been introduced to enhance ad hoc table retrieval by capturing interactions between query tokens and structured table data \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8e9cb4ee78cc6405139edd0509554a6d1bae3ca0)\]. These approaches emphasize the importance of end-to-end learning and differentiable operations to improve the accuracy and robustness of query-table interactions. Additionally, efforts have been made to develop large-scale datasets with diverse table structures to better evaluate and train these models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.09207)\]. Overall, the field is moving towards more sophisticated methods that enable deeper semantic alignment and more accurate retrieval of relevant tables based on natural language queries.

The presence of these queries at indexing allows the DSI models to connect a document identifier to a set of queries, hence mitigating data distribution mismatches present between the indexing and the retrieval phases. Empirical results on popular mono-lingual and cross-lingual passage retrieval datasets show that DSI-QG significantly outperforms the original DSI model.»

[Snippet]: «Differentiable Search Indices (DSIs) encode a corpus of documents in model parameters and use the same model to answer user queries directly. Despite the strong performance of DSI models, deploying them in situations where the corpus changes over time is computationally expensive because reindexing the corpus requires re-training the model. In this work, we introduce DSI++, a continual learning challenge for DSI to incrementally index new documents while being able to answer queries related to both previously and newly indexed documents. Across different model scales and document identifier representations, we show that continual indexing of new documents leads to considerable forgetting of previously indexed documents. We also hypothesize and verify that the model experiences forgetting events during training, leading to unstable learning. To mitigate these issues, we investigate two approaches. The first focuses on modifying the training dynamics. Flatter minima implicitly alleviate forgetting, so we optimize for flatter loss basins and show that the model stably memorizes more documents ($+12\%$). Next, we introduce a generative memory to sample pseudo-queries for documents and supplement them during continual indexing to prevent forgetting for the retrieval task. Extensive experiments on novel continual indexing benchmarks based on Natural Questions (NQ) and MS MARCO demonstrate that our proposed solution mitigates forgetting significantly. Concretely, it improves the average Hits@10 by $+21.1\%$ over competitive baselines for NQ and requires $6$ times fewer model updates compared to re-training the DSI model for incrementally indexing five corpora in a sequence.»
