## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to the traditional next-token prediction (NTP) objective in language model pretraining. While NTP focuses on predicting one token at a time, MTP extends this by predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously, often using multiple prediction heads \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This shift has been motivated by the potential to improve downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency, particularly for large models \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, prior work has demonstrated that smaller language models (SLMs) struggle with the MTP objective, often due to the increased complexity and computational demands \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. 

Several studies have explored the implications of MTP in different contexts. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\] highlights that MTP can lead to significant gains in inference speed, with models trained using 4-token prediction being up to 3 times faster at inference, even with large batch sizes. Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04060)\] introduces VocalNet, a speech LLM that leverages MTP to achieve faster and higher-quality generation, demonstrating the versatility of MTP across modalities. 

The challenges of integrating MTP into existing models have also been investigated. \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)\] shows that while MTP capabilities are inherent in models pre-trained for NTP, performance is data-dependent and improves with model scale. Moreover, integrating MTP heads into frozen LLMs is non-trivial due to the strong specialization of hidden layers for NTP. \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10518)\] further explores this by proposing MuToR, a method that introduces learnable register tokens to facilitate MTP without significant architectural changes, ensuring compatibility with existing models.

Curriculum learning has also been proposed as a strategy to address the difficulties of MTP for SLMs. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10194)\] explores curriculum learning for code language models, showing that a well-designed curriculum can significantly improve performance on code execution tasks. This aligns with the current work, which proposes a curriculum learning strategy for MTP training, including both forward and reverse variants. The forward curriculum gradually increases complexity from NTP to MTP, while the reverse curriculum does the opposite. 

The effectiveness of curriculum learning in MTP training has been validated through experiments. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\] demonstrates that the forward curriculum enables SLMs to better leverage the MTP objective during pretraining, improving downstream NTP performance and generative output quality. In contrast, the reverse curriculum achieves stronger NTP performance but fails to provide the benefits of self-speculative decoding.

### <a id='category-multi-token-prediction-(mtp)-objectives'></a>Multi-token Prediction (MTP) Objectives: 6 papers found.
Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to next-token prediction (NTP) in language model pretraining, offering benefits in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)\]. Unlike NTP, which predicts one token at a time, MTP predicts multiple future tokens simultaneously using multiple output heads, enabling the model to capture long-range dependencies more effectively \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to enhance MTP, such as leap multi-token prediction (L-MTP), which introduces a leap-based mechanism to skip intermediate tokens and improve inference efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17505)\], and joint multi-token prediction (JTP), which enriches hidden state representations by jointly predicting multiple future tokens with a carefully designed representation bottleneck \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Additionally, MuToR introduces learnable register tokens into the input sequence, allowing for efficient multi-token prediction without significant architectural changes or parameter overhead \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18619)\]. These methods demonstrate that MTP can be adapted to various settings, including speech LLMs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02007)\], and can be combined with curriculum learning strategies to better leverage MTP for smaller language models \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. However, challenges remain, such as ensuring compatibility with existing training frameworks and addressing potential limitations in fine-tuning scenarios \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04060)\]. Overall, MTP represents a significant shift in language model training, offering new opportunities for improving both model capabilities and training efficiency.

The dominant training paradigm for LLMs is based on next token prediction (NTP). [Snippet]: «Causal decoder-only transformer models used for generative language modelling, such as Generative Pre-trained Transformers (GPT), are trained to predict the next token in a sequence based only on its previous tokens. Despite this simple training objective, they have proved to be powerful AI tools. However, only predicting the next token results in top layer embedding vectors that are highly token-focused. There may be benefits in generating embedding vectors at each token position that better capture the overall meaning of longer sequences of future text. Recent studies matching brain scans with deep language models suggest that humans also predict upcoming words when listening or reading but consider multiple future tokens rather than just one. This research investigates a new pretraining method called Future Token Prediction (FTP). In FTP, a large transformer encoder generates top layer embedding vectors for each token position, which, instead of being passed to a language head, are linearly and expansively projected to a pseudo-sequence, which is cross attended to by a small transformer decoder to predict the next N tokens forward from that position in the sequence. The top layer embedding vectors from FTP models exhibit distinct properties compared to those from standard GPT models, varying smoothly along a text sequence as measured by cosine similarity between adjacent tokens. Text generated by FTP models show improved topic coherence compared to standard GPT-like models trained with the same prediction perplexity for the next single token. The vectors are shown to better represent the topic of text based on the results of text classification examples. On a toy, but complex, coding problem, FTP networks produce significantly better results than GPT networks.»
