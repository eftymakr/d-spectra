## Related Works

Nonverbal communication plays a crucial role in human interaction, encompassing gestures, facial expressions, and body language that convey intent and emotion \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. Existing large language models (LLMs) often lack the ability to effectively integrate these nonverbal elements, limiting their capacity to create immersive conversational experiences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. This section reviews relevant works that address nonverbal communication in AI systems, focusing on multimodal models, datasets, and methods for incorporating nonverbal cues into language understanding and generation.

Several studies have explored the integration of nonverbal cues into AI systems. For instance, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\] discusses the importance of modeling nonverbal behaviors, such as vocal patterns and facial expressions, to better capture speaker intentions. This work proposes the RAVEN model, which dynamically shifts word representations based on nonverbal cues, achieving competitive performance in multimodal sentiment analysis and emotion recognition \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. Similarly, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\] introduces the concept of nonverbal alphabets, which can be used in situated and multimodal communication, emphasizing the use of wearable sensors and machine learning to estimate the effectiveness of such systems \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\].

The integration of nonverbal cues into language models has also been explored in the context of human-robot interaction. \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11293)\] discusses the importance of nonverbal cues in human-robot communication, proposing a set of nonverbal codes that can be used to enhance the social agency of robots \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11293)\]. This work highlights the potential of nonverbal cues to make robots feel more "alive" and engaging to users.

In addition, \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\] presents a study on the use of facial affect in automated deception detection, demonstrating that facial valence and arousal can be used to distinguish between truthful and deceptive speech \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. This work highlights the importance of nonverbal cues in understanding human behavior and emotions, which is a key aspect of conversational AI.

Recent advances in multimodal models have also focused on the integration of nonverbal cues. \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10857)\] introduces a model that uses implicit behavior cloning to replicate nonverbal cues such as gaze behaviors, demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach in social interaction tasks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10857)\]. Similarly, \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00841)\] evaluates the advantages of multimodal versus verbal-only communication in robot-to-human interaction, showing that multimodal approaches lead to more natural and effective communication \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00841)\].

The category "Multimodal Models for Nonverbal Understanding" encompasses a range of research efforts aimed at integrating nonverbal cues—such as facial expressions, gestures, and vocal patterns—into language models to enhance their comprehension and generation of human-like communication \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. Several studies have explored the challenges of modeling nonverbal behaviors alongside text, emphasizing the need for fine-grained analysis of visual and acoustic patterns \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. In educational settings, models have been developed to assess nonverbal immediacy by analyzing classroom videos, demonstrating the potential of such approaches in evaluating teaching behaviors \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21652)\]. Additionally, the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) as knowledge bases has been explored to enhance visual word sense disambiguation by resolving contextual ambiguities \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17209)\]. The ability of LLMs to reason about multimodal data, such as visual and auditory inputs, has also been leveraged to perform zero-shot video classification without additional fine-tuning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10574)\]. ### <a id='category-nonverbal-cues-in-human-robot-interaction'></a>Nonverbal Cues in Human-Robot Interaction: 3 papers found.
The category "Nonverbal Cues in Human-Robot Interaction" explores how nonverbal communication can be effectively integrated into human-robot and human-human interactions, particularly in immersive environments like social VR and conversational agents. Research highlights the importance of nonverbal cues such as eye contact, head shaking, and nodding, which are essential for conveying attention, emotion, and social norms \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. In social VR, these cues are often inaccessible to blind and low vision users, prompting the development of alternative modalities like audio and haptics to represent nonverbal behaviors \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.09362)\]. Studies also emphasize that nonverbal cues across the five human sensory systems—visual, auditory, haptic, olfactory, and gustatory—can be systematically translated into design patterns for human-robot interaction (HRI), enhancing the perceived "aliveness" or "social agency" of robots \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.04314)\]. Furthermore, nonverbal emotional cues from conversational agents, such as anger and sadness, can significantly influence human decision-making in moral dilemmas, demonstrating the nuanced impact of these cues on user perception and behavior \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07214)\]. These findings underscore the need for further research into the integration of nonverbal cues in AI systems to improve accessibility, naturalness, and ethical considerations in human-AI interactions.

These results underscore a gap between human-level visual understanding and what current MLLMs can achieve, emphasizing the need for more sophisticated integration strategies and better alignment with human perception capabilities \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12390)\].

### <a id='category-facial-affect-and-deception-detection'></a>Facial Affect and Deception Detection: 1 papers found.
Facial affect and deception detection have emerged as critical areas within affective computing, with recent research focusing on leveraging dimensional representations of valence and arousal to improve automated deception detection systems \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. These systems aim to enhance societal applications in health, justice, and security by identifying deception in high-stakes scenarios such as courtrooms \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. Existing machine learning approaches have primarily relied on interpretable visual, vocal, and verbal features, but recent advances have emphasized the importance of continuous, multimodal representations of facial affect \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. By utilizing a video dataset of individuals communicating truthfully or deceptively, researchers have demonstrated that unimodal models trained on facial affect achieved an AUC of 80%, while multimodal approaches, particularly those incorporating adaptive boosting, achieved an AUC of 91% \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. This performance surpasses previous methods that did not incorporate facial affect, highlighting its significant contribution to deception detection \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. Furthermore, the study found that deceptive and truthful speakers exhibited notable differences in facial valence and arousal, supporting psychological theories linking affect to deception \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.13369)\]. These findings underscore the potential of affect-aware machine learning models to better understand and detect deception in real-world settings.
