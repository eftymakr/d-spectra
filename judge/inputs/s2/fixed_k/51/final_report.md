## Related Works

Federated unlearning has emerged as a critical research area in the context of privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in response to the "right to be forgotten" regulation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. This concept allows users to request the removal of their data from the global model trained across distributed clients, without requiring full retraining \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) is a promising paradigm that integrates federated learning with online learning to rank, enabling continuous model updates while preserving user privacy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. However, FOLTR faces challenges in effectively and securely unlearning client contributions, especially due to the dynamic nature of online learning and the potential for data distribution inconsistencies across clients \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19218)\].

Several studies have explored federated unlearning, focusing on its effectiveness, efficiency, and security. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\] introduces a comprehensive framework for federated unlearning, emphasizing the need for mechanisms that allow the removal of specific clients' contributions without retraining the entire model. This is crucial for maintaining model performance while respecting privacy constraints. Similarly, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\] presents a survey on federated unlearning, highlighting the challenges of unlearning in distributed settings, such as the need for efficient communication and the risk of malicious clients injecting backdoors into the global model.

To address these challenges, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12502)\] proposes a novel approach called FedRemoval, which enables the server to unlearn a target client without requiring client communication or additional storage. This method is certified and secure, making it a promising solution for federated unlearning. Additionally, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02216)\] introduces the concept of verifiable federated unlearning, where the unlearning effect can be independently verified, ensuring compliance with legal and ethical standards. This is particularly important in scenarios where users need to confirm that their data has been successfully removed from the model.

Another significant contribution is the development of evaluation metrics for federated unlearning. \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\] presents a detailed analysis of existing methods, emphasizing the need for multiple metrics to comprehensively assess unlearning success. This aligns with the findings of \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\], which highlights the limitations of single-metric evaluations and advocates for a more holistic approach to measuring unlearning effectiveness.

### <a id='category-federated-learning-and-online-learning-to-rank-(foltr)'></a>Federated Learning and Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR): 6 papers found.
Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) represents an integration of federated learning (FL) and online learning to rank (OLTR) to enhance privacy while maintaining ranking effectiveness \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Unlike traditional OLTR methods that rely on static datasets with human annotations, FOLTR continuously updates models using implicit user feedback such as clicks, without sharing raw data \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. This approach addresses privacy concerns inherent in centralized data collection, as FL enables collaborative model training without exposing private local data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. However, FOLTR faces challenges in ranking effectiveness, robustness across client data distributions, and susceptibility to attacks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Additionally, the need for interpretable models in FL has emerged as a critical research direction, especially in sensitive domains like healthcare and finance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]. Privacy threats in FL, including those targeting data types in horizontal, vertical, and federated transfer learning settings, have been systematically reviewed, highlighting the necessity for robust defense mechanisms \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Recent advancements in FL also address non-i.i.d data and signal multi-modality by proposing novel aggregation strategies that maintain model performance while preserving data privacy \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\]. Furthermore, the integration of domain adaptation with FL has shown improvements in model accuracy, particularly under differential privacy constraints \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\]. These developments underscore the evolving landscape of FOLTR, emphasizing the balance between privacy, performance, and practical deployment.

### <a id='category-unlearning-methods-in-federated-settings'></a>Unlearning Methods in Federated Settings: 17 papers found.
The category "Unlearning Methods in Federated Settings" explores the challenges and advancements in enabling users to remove their data's influence from federated learning (FL) models, driven by privacy regulations such as the "right to be forgotten." This area has gained traction due to the increasing demand for privacy-preserving machine learning, particularly in sectors like healthcare and finance, where data sensitivity is paramount \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Federated unlearning (FU) aims to selectively eliminate specific clients' contributions without requiring full model retraining, addressing both privacy concerns and the risk of malicious data injection \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. However, the unique characteristics of FL—such as distributed training, data heterogeneity, and limited server access—introduce complexities that traditional unlearning methods cannot easily accommodate \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03078)\]. Several studies have proposed novel frameworks, such as FedEraser, which leverages historical parameter updates to reconstruct unlearned models efficiently \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02216)\], and FATS, which ensures exact unlearning by leveraging total variation stability \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20709)\]. Additionally, approaches like FedRemoval and (F²L²) have been introduced to enhance efficiency and security, enabling unlearning without client cooperation or additional storage \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12502)\]. Despite these advances, challenges remain, including the need for more robust evaluation metrics, the impact of unlearning on model accuracy, and the balance between privacy, efficiency, and performance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20448)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19218)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02437)\]. The field continues to evolve, with a growing emphasis on practical implementations and compliance with legal and ethical standards \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13891)\]\[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\].

### <a id='category-security-and-verification-in-federated-unlearning'></a>Security and Verification in Federated Unlearning: 3 papers found.
The category "Security and Verification in Federated Unlearning" focuses on the challenges and solutions related to ensuring data privacy and verifiability in federated learning environments, particularly in the context of unlearning. This includes the implementation of the right to be forgotten (RTBF), where participants can request the deletion of their private data from the global model \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. However, unlearning alone is insufficient without verification, as the unlearning effect can be undermined by other participants' contributions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed verifiable federated unlearning frameworks, such as VeriFi, which allow leaving participants to verify the unlearning effect through marking and checking processes \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. Additionally, verification mechanisms based on backdoor attacks have been introduced to ensure that data deletion requests are effectively executed and verified \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04247)\]. These approaches aim to provide robust and non-invasive verification methods, ensuring that unlearning is both effective and secure \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12709)\]. Furthermore, security challenges in federated unlearning, such as poisoning attacks, have been identified, leading to the development of robust frameworks like UnlearnGuard to mitigate these threats \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17396)\]. Overall, this category highlights the importance of combining unlearning with verification and security measures to achieve trustworthy federated learning systems.

### <a id='category-ethical-and-compliance-considerations'></a>Ethical and Compliance Considerations: 2 papers found.
The "Right to be Forgotten" is a foundational principle in data privacy, particularly under regulations like the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which allows individuals to request the deletion of their personal data from machine learning models \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. This principle is especially relevant in federated learning systems such as Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR), where user data is processed across decentralized sources while maintaining privacy \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\]. However, naive retraining to accommodate such requests is computationally infeasible, prompting the development of efficient unlearning methods that approximate retraining without full retraining \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. Despite these advances, existing methods often overlook the impact of unlearning on critical properties like fairness, which is essential for real-world applications \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. Recent research has begun to address these gaps by proposing fair machine unlearning techniques that maintain both predictive performance and fairness during the unlearning process \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14754)\]. These developments underscore the growing need for robust evaluation frameworks that assess unlearning effectiveness and fairness, ensuring that federated systems like FOLTR can uphold data privacy and ethical standards while delivering high-quality search results \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02164)\].

### <a id='category-evaluation-metrics-for-unlearning'></a>Evaluation Metrics for Unlearning: 2 papers found.
The category "Evaluation Metrics for Unlearning" highlights the critical need for reliable and comprehensive evaluation frameworks to assess the success of unlearning in machine learning models. Existing approaches often rely on adversarial attacks or comparisons to retrained models, which may not accurately reflect real-world performance \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. Recent studies have identified limitations in these methods, particularly in diffusion models, where unlearning techniques can lead to concealment rather than true forgetting \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. To address these shortcomings, researchers have proposed new evaluation metrics such as the Concept Retrieval Score (CRS) and Concept Confidence Score (CCS), which measure the retrieval of forgotten concepts and the model's confidence in aligning with original domain knowledge, respectively \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. These metrics provide a more robust and nuanced evaluation of unlearning effectiveness, enabling a deeper understanding of the strengths and limitations of current methods \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05146)\]. Additionally, the use of epistemic uncertainty as a basis for evaluation metrics has been introduced to offer a general framework for assessing unlearning algorithms, emphasizing the importance of reliability and practical applicability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19069)\]. These developments underscore the ongoing efforts to refine evaluation practices in unlearning, ensuring they align with both theoretical goals and practical implementation needs.
