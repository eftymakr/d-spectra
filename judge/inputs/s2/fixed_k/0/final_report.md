Economic inequality remains a critical challenge, influencing disparities in education, healthcare, and social stability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. Traditional tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, aim to reduce inequality but often lack adaptability to evolving socio-economic conditions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. Models like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework attempt to address this by introducing dynamic tax rate adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\], yet they often fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. This paper introduces TaxAgent, a novel integration of large language models (LLMs) with agent-based modeling (ABM) to design adaptive tax policies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. 

Agent-based models (ABMs) have been widely used to simulate economic systems and analyze the effects of policy interventions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. These models allow for the exploration of emergent behaviors in multi-agent decision-making settings, making them valuable tools for policy analysis \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. However, the validity of inferences drawn from ABMs depends on the quality of the agents' behavioral models \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. Standard specifications often rely on heuristic decision-making rules or regression models trained on past data, which may not fully capture the complexity of human behavior \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. Recent work has explored the use of reinforcement learning (RL) models as adaptive and behaviorally-valid representations of agent decision-making in ABMs \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. RL models have shown effectiveness in producing reward-seeking behaviors and outperforming traditional adaptive behavioral models in various economic simulations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\].

### <a id='category-agent-based-modeling-in-economic-policy'></a>Agent-Based Modeling in Economic Policy: 5 papers found.
Agent-based modeling (ABM) has emerged as a powerful tool for analyzing economic policy, particularly in understanding complex behaviors and interactions within economic systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. Researchers have developed various ABM frameworks to simulate economic phenomena, such as tax evasion, income distribution, and policy impacts \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09563)\]. For instance, one study introduced an ABM that integrates heterogeneous agents, including households, firms, a central bank, and a government, allowing for the simulation of diverse economic behaviors and the evaluation of policy interventions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09563)\]. Another study applied the Ising model of ferromagnetism to analyze income tax evasion dynamics, incorporating social and behavioral factors to better understand how enforcement policies affect taxpayer behavior \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1112.0233)\]. Additionally, ABM has been used to explore the role of social discrimination, such as aporophobia, in perpetuating poverty, highlighting the need for policies that address both economic and social barriers \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13994)\]. These models often leverage real-world data and behavioral rules to enhance realism and provide insights into the effectiveness of different policy approaches. Furthermore, ABM has been integrated with machine learning techniques, such as reinforcement learning, to improve the adaptability of economic agents and the accuracy of policy simulations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. Overall, these studies demonstrate the versatility of ABM in capturing the complexities of economic systems and informing more effective policy design.

### <a id='category-reinforcement-learning-in-policy-simulations'></a>Reinforcement Learning in Policy Simulations: 2 papers found.
Agent-based models (ABMs) are increasingly used in policy simulations to explore the effects of interventions in complex, multi-agent systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. These models rely on accurate behavioral representations of agents, which can be derived from heuristic rules or historical data regression \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. However, both approaches have limitations in capturing adaptive, utility-maximizing behaviors \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. Recent research has explored the use of reinforcement learning (RL) as a more effective method for modeling agent decision-making in ABMs, as RL agents can learn reward-seeking behaviors and outperform traditional models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. This aligns with broader efforts to develop generalist AI agents capable of rapid adaptation to new environments, such as the REGENT framework, which uses retrieval-based in-context learning to achieve efficient generalization with fewer parameters and training data \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. These advancements highlight the growing intersection between RL and ABMs in policy analysis, offering more realistic and adaptive simulations for evaluating economic and social policies.

### <a id='category-behavioral-models-and-decision-making-rules'></a>Behavioral Models and Decision-Making Rules: 2 papers found.
Behavioral models and decision-making rules play a crucial role in designing adaptive systems that can handle heterogeneity and unpredictability, particularly in dynamic environments like pervasive computing and financial markets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. In pervasive computing, the challenge lies in integrating devices with varying computational resources to form a cohesive system that meets the needs of all participants. Existing approaches often focus on optimizing individual variables or low-level Service Level Objectives (SLOs), which can introduce significant complexity in dynamic settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. To address this, researchers have proposed leveraging Active Inference (AIF), a neuroscientific framework, to create conceptual agents that can set global system constraints as high-level SLOs, allowing for adaptive equilibrium in resource-heterogeneous environments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.08146)\]. Similarly, in financial markets, agent-based modeling is used to capture behavioral peculiarities such as herding, which can influence macroscopic market dynamics \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. These models integrate both exogenous and endogenous noise sources to simulate real-world market behaviors and derive stochastic differential equations that describe macroscopic trends \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05048)\]. Both domains highlight the importance of behavioral models in creating adaptive systems that can respond to environmental changes while maintaining system coherence and performance.

To resolve this problem, previous work has first collected interaction trajectories between LLMs and environments, using only trajectories that successfully finished the task to fine-tune smaller models, making fine-tuning data scarce and acquiring it both difficult and costly. In this paper, we argue that unsuccessful trajectories offer valuable insights, and LLMs can learn from these trajectories through appropriate quality control and fine-tuning strategies.
