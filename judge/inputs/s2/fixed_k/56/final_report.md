## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to transfer user preferences across domains and alleviate the cold-start problem \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Early approaches primarily focused on modeling intra-sequence item interactions and transferring knowledge between domains using predefined modules \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. However, these methods often overlooked the importance of inter-sequence relationships and the potential for leveraging semantic information from large language models (LLMs) \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19862)\]. 

A notable work, C2DSR, addresses these limitations by simultaneously capturing intra- and inter-sequence item relationships and jointly learning single- and cross-domain user preferences \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07761)\]. This approach introduces a contrastive cross-domain infomax objective to enhance the correlation between single- and cross-domain user representations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12473)\]. Additionally, it corrects information leak issues in prior datasets, improving the reliability of experimental results \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.04218)\].

Another line of research explores the integration of LLMs into CDSR to better capture semantic information. The URLLM framework introduces a user retrieve-generation model to seamlessly integrate structural information into LLMs, leveraging their emergent inferencing ability \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.17890)\]. Furthermore, it proposes domain-specific strategies and refinement modules to prevent out-of-domain generation \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13254)\]. Similarly, LLM4CDR constructs context-aware prompts using users' purchase history and shared features between domains, demonstrating that LLMs can outperform state-of-the-art CDR baselines when prompted effectively \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.08050)\].

The challenge of non-overlapping cross-domain sequential recommendation (NCSR) has also been addressed. PLCR, an automated prompting-based framework, learns domain-invariant and domain-specific representations without requiring explicit domain alignment constraints \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00999)\]. It also introduces a pre-trained sequence encoder to capture users' sequential interaction patterns and conducts dual-learning targets to enhance recommendations in both domains \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09261)\].

In addition to these methods, several works focus on improving the efficiency and performance of LLMs in sequential recommendation. Additionally, the transition complexity of mixed behavior sequences remains a challenge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Recent work has focused on improving item representation alignment, as misaligned representations can hinder sequential modeling and user alignment \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Furthermore, the partial representation alignment problem is addressed through an adaptive spectrum filter, allowing for more flexible and effective alignment \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03085)\]. Privacy concerns have also emerged as a critical issue, prompting the development of privacy-preserving methods like PriCDSR, which integrates differential privacy to protect user data while maintaining recommendation quality \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve both the technical and ethical aspects of CDSR.
