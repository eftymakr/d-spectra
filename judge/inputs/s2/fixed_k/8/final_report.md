## Related Works

The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) has underscored the critical need for robust fairness benchmarks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, which fail to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. This limitation has prompted researchers to explore alternative fairness metrics that account for model confidence and decision uncertainty. For instance, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13738)\] introduces a decision framework that maps LLM use cases to relevant bias and fairness metrics based on task type, stakeholder priorities, and the presence of protected attribute mentions. This work highlights the importance of context-specific fairness evaluation, emphasizing that fairness risks cannot be reliably assessed from benchmark performance alone \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13738)\]. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.15941)\] proposes a novel fairness metric, UCerF, which evaluates model fairness with uncertainty awareness, offering a more fine-grained assessment of internal bias compared to conventional measures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\].

Existing fairness benchmarks often suffer from data size, diversity, and clarity issues, which can limit their effectiveness in evaluating modern LLMs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. To address this, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.09838)\] introduces a new gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset with 31,756 samples, designed for co-reference resolution and offering a more diverse and suitable dataset for evaluating LLMs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10853)\]. This dataset is particularly relevant for assessing gender bias in occupational scenarios, where LLMs frequently exhibit significant deviations from demographic statistics \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\]. For example, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\] reports that LLMs often depict female characters more frequently than male ones in various occupations, showing a 37% deviation from U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data. These findings underscore the need for more representative and diverse datasets to accurately evaluate LLM fairness \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14583)\].

Several studies have investigated gender bias in LLMs, comparing model outputs with human perceptions and official statistics \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07887)\]. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07887)\] evaluates five LLMs by predicting the gender for each role using single-word answers and finds that all models show significant deviation from gender neutrality, aligning more with statistical data than human perceptions \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07887)\].
