Early works such as \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\] highlighted the importance of fairness-aware recommendation systems, particularly in domains with high social impact, such as employment and housing. These studies emphasized the need to balance recommendation accuracy with fairness metrics, often focusing on single sensitive attributes like race or gender \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\]. However, they largely ignored the role of individual preferences in limiting the ability of algorithms to produce fair recommendations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974)\].

The concept of fairness-aware re-ranking has been further advanced by works such as \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921)\], which introduced the idea of personalized fairness-aware re-ranking. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01203)\] proposed a method that uses representations of demographic groups to incorporate fairness into the re-ranking process, demonstrating that even limited labeled data can be effective in achieving fairness \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01203)\].

A key challenge in fairness-aware re-ranking is the trade-off between accuracy and fairness, which has been explored from various perspectives. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04250)\] introduced the notion of provider fairness, emphasizing the need to ensure that items from different providers have a fair chance of being recommended.
