## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has been widely studied as a formal framework for protecting individual privacy in data analysis. While DP is often considered the de facto standard for privacy-preserving data release \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05609)\], its implementation in distributed settings is vulnerable to malicious behavior by servers, such as incorrect noise sampling or correlated noise generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These issues have motivated the development of verifiable DP mechanisms that ensure the correctness of DP execution \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\]. 

Verifiable differential privacy (VLDP) has been explored in the local model, where clients add noise to their data before sending it to a server \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. However, these approaches typically require multiple rounds of interaction between the client and server, which can be inefficient \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\]. In contrast, our proposed Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP) framework introduces a novel definition that incorporates verification mechanisms to ensure the correctness of DP execution in a distributed setting \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. This is a significant advancement over previous work that assumes trust in the server's execution of DP mechanisms \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\].

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) have been used to verify the correctness of DP mechanisms, ensuring that the output is both differentially private and reliable \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as demonstrated by our analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. This insight is crucial for designing efficient verification mechanisms that do not rely on ZKPs.

In the context of verifiable DP mechanisms, several approaches have been proposed. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\] introduces a scheme that prevents both input and output manipulation attacks against generic LDP mechanisms, requiring only one-time interaction between client and server. Similarly, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\] proposes a scheme for verifiable LDP in the shuffle model, which is more efficient than existing alternatives. Our VDDP framework builds on these ideas, offering significant improvements in efficiency and verification capabilities.

In addition to verification, the efficiency of DP mechanisms is a critical consideration. For example, \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\] describes a scheme for verifiable LDP in the local model, with client run times of less than 2 seconds and server run times of 5-7 milliseconds per client. Our VDDP framework further improves upon these results by achieving up to a $4 \times 10^5$x improvement in proof generation efficiency with only 0.1-0.2x error compared to the previous state-of-the-art verifiable differentially private mechanism \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\].

### <a id='category-verifiable-differential-privacy-(vdp)-mechanisms'></a>Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) Mechanisms: 2 papers found.
Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) mechanisms aim to address the vulnerabilities of traditional differential privacy (DP) by introducing verification steps to ensure compliance with privacy guarantees. In the local model, LDP mechanisms protect client data by adding noise before sending data to a central server, but they are susceptible to input and output manipulation by malicious clients \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed verifiable LDP (VLDP) schemes that allow clients to prove the correctness of their inputs and the validity of the results returned by the server, requiring only a one-time interaction between client and server \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These schemes are particularly important in the shuffle model, where data is shuffled before aggregation, further obscuring the link between clients and the aggregator \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. While existing VLDP approaches have focused on the local model, recent work has extended these ideas to the shuffle model, demonstrating that efficient and practical VLDP mechanisms can be achieved \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Additionally, automated verification of DP systems has gained attention, with researchers developing formal models and proof techniques to ensure that implemented systems adhere to differential privacy guarantees \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. These efforts highlight the growing interest in combining verification techniques with DP to enhance trust and security in privacy-preserving data analysis.

However, in many embodiments, DP introduces a new attack surface: a malicious entity entrusted with releasing statistics could manipulate the results and use the randomness of DP as a convenient smokescreen to mask its nefariousness. The category "Differential Privacy (DP) Frameworks" encompasses a range of research efforts aimed at improving the security, efficiency, and practicality of differential privacy mechanisms. Existing work highlights vulnerabilities in traditional DP implementations, such as susceptibility to floating-point representation attacks and timing side channels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These issues undermine the reliability of DP systems, particularly in distributed settings where noise generation and timing can be exploited by adversaries. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed alternative mechanisms that generate bounded and unbiased outputs, offering better utility while maintaining privacy guarantees \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. Additionally, there is growing interest in federated learning and decentralized systems, where DP is applied to protect user data across distributed nodes, often with significant overhead \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\]. Some studies focus on enhancing DP for specific applications, such as $k$-means clustering and matrix factorization, by introducing novel techniques that balance privacy and accuracy \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05307)\]. Furthermore, the integration of advanced mathematical tools, such as composite probability density functions and automatic differentiation, has enabled more precise sensitivity analysis and noise calibration \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02324)\]. These developments reflect a broader trend toward making differential privacy more robust, flexible, and suitable for real-world applications. The concept of heterogeneous differential privacy has also emerged, allowing for personalized privacy guarantees that account for varying user preferences \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1601.06426)\]. Overall, the field is advancing through a combination of theoretical innovations and practical implementations that aim to strengthen the foundations of differential privacy.

### <a id='category-privacy-utility-trade-offs-in-dp'></a>Privacy-Utility Trade-offs in DP: 9 papers found.
The category "Privacy-Utility Trade-offs in DP" explores the balance between preserving privacy and maintaining data utility in differentially private systems. Research in this area highlights that differential privacy (DP) introduces inherent trade-offs, as increasing privacy typically reduces the utility of the data \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1212.1186)\]. For example, the Laplacian mechanism, widely used for real-valued queries, is asymptotically optimal in high privacy regimes but less efficient in low privacy settings, where staircase-shaped noise distributions perform better \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1212.1186)\]. Additionally, the choice of noise distribution significantly impacts the privacy-utility trade-off, with mechanisms like the two-sided Geometric distribution and Discrete Gaussian distribution offering different privacy guarantees and error levels \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08662)\]. In distributed settings, the risk of unfaithful execution of DP mechanisms by servers—such as sampling noise from incorrect distributions or generating correlated noise—poses challenges, prompting the development of verifiable DP frameworks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. These frameworks, like Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP), incorporate verification mechanisms to ensure compliance with DP protocols \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18940)\]. Furthermore, the application of DP in smart grids and machine learning systems underscores the need to balance privacy, security, and utility, with studies showing that DP can be feasible while maintaining acceptable QoS \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2208.09011)\]. Bayesian differential privacy has also been introduced to address privacy for outlier data by incorporating prior distributions, enhancing the protection of distant data pairs \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.10667)\]. Overall, the category emphasizes the complexity of designing DP mechanisms that effectively balance privacy and utility across various applications and data types.

### <a id='category-local-differential-privacy-(ldp)-and-vrr'></a>Local Differential Privacy (LDP) and VRR: 4 papers found.
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) and Verifiable Randomized Response (VRR) have been extensively studied for their role in preserving user privacy during data aggregation and graph analysis. LDP ensures privacy by adding noise to individual data contributions before they are aggregated, preventing the disclosure of sensitive information \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12621)\]. In the context of graph neural networks (GNNs), LDP is used to protect user features and edges while maintaining the graph structure, though early approaches lacked robustness for unattributed graphs \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10209)\]. Recent work has introduced the DPRR algorithm, which preserves node degrees under edge LDP by combining Warner's randomized response with Laplacian mechanisms and strategic sampling \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10209)\]. This advancement allows for accurate graph classification while maintaining strong privacy guarantees. Additionally, the integration of LDP with federated learning has been explored to enhance machine learning performance while mitigating privacy risks, leveraging momentum gradient descent to reduce communication costs \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.14086)\]. Meanwhile, the challenges of explaining differential privacy to data subjects, particularly in local and central models, have also been addressed, emphasizing the need for clear, accurate privacy expectations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08475)\]. These developments highlight the growing interest in LDP and VRR for achieving both privacy and utility in modern data systems.
