## Related Works

Collaborative filtering (CF) has been a cornerstone in recommendation systems, with recent advancements leveraging graph neural networks (GNNs) to model user-item interactions more effectively \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Traditional CF methods, such as matrix factorization, often fail to capture high-order connectivity and complex user-item relationships \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. Graph-based approaches, particularly graph convolutional networks (GCNs), have emerged as a powerful alternative by explicitly modeling the bipartite user-item graph \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. However, these models still face limitations in capturing the full spectrum of interaction signals and handling non-linear patterns \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Several works have attempted to improve the expressiveness of GCN-based CF models. For instance, NGCF integrates user-item interactions directly into the embedding process, propagating embeddings through the graph to capture high-order connectivity \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.04384)\]. Similarly, Multi-GCCF explicitly incorporates multiple graphs—user-item, user-user, and item-item—to better model high-order information and proximal relationships \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13996)\]. These approaches, however, often suffer from over-smoothing, where deeper layers lose distinguishing features due to repeated aggregation \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\].

To mitigate over-smoothing and improve expressiveness, researchers have proposed various techniques. For example, RGCF introduces a refined graph convolution structure and a symmetric normalization to better capture node popularity and improve embedding quality \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.10699)\]. Additionally, LR-GCCF revisits GCN-based CF models, showing that removing non-linearities can enhance recommendation performance and propose a residual network structure to alleviate over-smoothing \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04755)\]. These findings align with the idea that linear models can be more effective in certain scenarios \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14734)\].

Another key challenge in CF is the lack of fine-grained modeling of diverse preference patterns. Some works, such as MCCF, attempt to distinguish latent purchasing motivations by decomposing and recombining edges in the user-item graph \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02404)\]. This approach allows for more nuanced modeling of user preferences but may still fall short in capturing the full spectrum of interaction signals \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05962)\].

For example, SCCF introduces a simple yet effective algorithm based on a naive embedding model and a modified contrastive loss, demonstrating significant improvements over existing methods \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01376)\]. Similarly, XSimGCL proposes an extremely simple graph contrastive learning method that outperforms more complex graph augmentation-based approaches \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.12843)\].

### <a id='category-spectral-graph-theory-applications'></a>Spectral Graph Theory Applications: 2 papers found.
Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have been widely adopted for deep learning on graph-structured data, yet they face significant limitations. Most existing GCNs act as low-pass filters, neglecting the middle and high-frequency components of graph signals, which may contain valuable information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Additionally, their filters have fixed bandwidths, limiting their ability to adapt to varying frequency needs without expert domain knowledge \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. These shortcomings are addressed by Automatic Graph Convolutional Networks (AutoGCN), which dynamically adjust the bandwidth of graph convolutional filters to capture the full spectrum of graph signals \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Similarly, Directional Graph Networks (DGNs) overcome expressive limitations in graph neural networks (GNNs) by introducing vector flows over graphs, enabling globally consistent directional and asymmetric aggregation functions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. DGNs generalize convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on grids and allow for directional derivatives and smoothing through Laplacian eigenvectors \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. These advancements highlight the importance of spectral graph theory in improving the performance and adaptability of graph-based models.

### <a id='category-neighborhood-aggregation-techniques'></a>Neighborhood Aggregation Techniques: 3 papers found.
Graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have been extensively studied for their ability to handle graph-structured data, with a particular focus on neighborhood aggregation techniques \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. These methods typically involve message-passing mechanisms where node features are aggregated from neighboring nodes, often using differentiable protocols such as Laplacian smoothing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. Recent work has explored adaptive communication strategies, such as the adaptive propagation GCN (AP-GCN), which allows nodes to independently decide the number of communication steps based on a halting unit \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12590)\]. This approach improves performance while maintaining a minimal parameter overhead. Additionally, researchers have addressed challenges like over-smoothing and over-fitting by introducing regularization techniques, such as DropNode, which randomly discards parts of the graph to enhance generalization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.03383)\]. In heterogeneous graph settings, methods like MECCH have been developed to efficiently aggregate information across diverse node and edge types by leveraging metapath contexts and convolutional fusion \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to refine neighborhood aggregation in GCNs, balancing accuracy, efficiency, and robustness across various applications.
