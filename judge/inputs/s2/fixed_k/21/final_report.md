## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful technique for reconstructing 3D scenes from 2D images by leveraging implicit neural representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. These representations allow for high-quality surface and radiance field reconstruction, often using multi-view inputs to minimize geometric and appearance uncertainty \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is constrained by the number of input views, especially in complex or dynamic scenes where certain angles are not captured \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. This limitation has motivated the exploration of data augmentation techniques to improve the robustness and generalization of neural rendering models \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\].

Video frame interpolation has been widely studied as a method to generate additional views, thereby enriching the input data for neural rendering \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\]. Traditional methods rely on optical flow estimation and pixel synthesis, often requiring large kernels to handle significant motion \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. More recent approaches, such as those based on adaptive separable convolutions, have improved efficiency by reducing the number of parameters needed to estimate motion \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01331)\]. These methods are particularly useful for high-resolution video processing, where computational demands are significant \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. Additionally, the use of neural fields (NFs) for video interpolation has shown promise, enabling continuous spatiotemporal representation of scenes \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. CURE, a method based on neural fields, introduces a new architecture that conditions the neural network on input frames to ensure space-time consistency \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05928)\].

Several works have explored the use of neural implicit representations for 3D reconstruction, particularly in indoor scenes \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15164)\]. For instance, NeuralRoom addresses shape-radiance ambiguity by incorporating geometric priors and multiview stereo results to guide the optimization process \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.13817)\]. Similarly, the use of scene priors has been proposed to improve the generalizability of neural fields, allowing for fast adaptation to new scenes with limited views \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00050)\]. These approaches highlight the importance of incorporating domain-specific knowledge to enhance the performance of implicit models \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11745)\].

In the context of video frame interpolation, recent methods have focused on improving the quality of interpolated frames by incorporating perceptual and structural cues \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13454)\]. For example, the use of flow transformers has enabled the modeling of long-range dependencies and motion dynamics, leading to more accurate and visually pleasing results \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08936)\]. Additionally, depth-aware interpolation techniques have been proposed to handle large object motion and occlusion by explicitly considering depth information during the interpolation process \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08013)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of combining geometric and temporal information to improve the robustness of frame interpolation \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11170)\].

### <a id='category-scene-prior-integration-in-3d-reconstruction'></a>Scene Prior Integration in 3D Reconstruction: 8 papers found.
The category "Scene Prior Integration in 3D Reconstruction" encompasses a range of approaches that leverage scene priors to enhance the accuracy and efficiency of 3D reconstruction from 2D inputs. These methods often address the limitations of implicit neural representations, which struggle with shape-radiance ambiguity and require dense input views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. For instance, NeuralRoom integrates geometric priors to constrain the optimization space and improve reconstruction of texture-less areas in indoor scenes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. Similarly, methods like NFP incorporate scene priors trained on large-scale datasets to enable fast adaptation to new scenes with fewer views, demonstrating improved reconstruction performance and flexibility \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\]. Other works, such as CoCo-INR, inject prior information into coordinate-based networks to enrich feature representations, enabling more photo-realistic results with fewer calibrated images \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10985)\]. Additionally, approaches like the one using Manhattan scene priors exploit high-level geometric constraints to self-supervise surface normals in implicit neural fields, enhancing the quality of 3D reconstructions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These strategies highlight the importance of integrating scene priors to overcome challenges in 3D reconstruction, particularly in complex and dynamic environments.

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-methods'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Methods: 14 papers found.
Video frame interpolation methods have evolved significantly, particularly with the integration of deep learning techniques. Traditional approaches often separate motion estimation and pixel synthesis, which can limit performance due to reliance on optical flow \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. Recent advancements, such as those using deep fully convolutional neural networks, have merged these steps into a single process, enabling more efficient and accurate frame synthesis \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. These methods often employ spatially adaptive kernels, which allow for better handling of large motions while reducing computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.06853)\]. Furthermore, some approaches have introduced splatting-based synthesis, which not only accelerates the process but also achieves state-of-the-art results at high resolutions \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10075)\]. The use of Transformers in video interpolation has also gained traction, offering content-aware aggregation and long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\]. However, these models often face challenges with computational complexity and memory usage, prompting innovations like local attention and space-time separation strategies to enhance efficiency \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\]. Additionally, unsupervised techniques have been proposed to synthesize high frame rate videos from low frame rate inputs, leveraging cycle consistency and pseudo supervised losses to improve performance without requiring extensive annotated data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These developments highlight a shift towards more robust, efficient, and versatile video frame interpolation solutions that can handle diverse and challenging scenarios \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11745)\].

Recent studies have explored their application in autonomous 3D reconstruction, where robots must plan view paths for scene exploration \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. A key challenge in this area is developing a reliable criterion to evaluate viewpoint quality, which has been addressed by proposing a proxy for Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) to quantify viewpoint quality and optimize it jointly with the neural network parameters \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07021)\]. Additionally, implicit neural representations have been leveraged to reconstruct dynamic scenes from multi-view videos, utilizing topology-aware deformation and signed distance fields to learn complex dynamic surfaces without prior knowledge \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18897)\]. This approach has demonstrated high-fidelity surface reconstruction and photorealistic novel view synthesis. Furthermore, in the context of computed tomography (4D-CT), implicit neural representations have been combined with parametric motion field warping to enable limited view reconstruction of rapidly deforming scenes, achieving robust results without training data \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility of implicit neural representations in addressing diverse challenges in 3D reconstruction, from static to dynamic environments. The integration of these methods with data augmentation techniques, such as video frame interpolation, further enhances their applicability in improving reconstruction performance across a wide range of scenarios \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.00137)\].
