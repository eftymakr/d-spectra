## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and generate text, but their capacity to encode behaviors such as refusal remains underexplored. Prior work has primarily focused on identifying refusal through predefined templates or manual analysis, which limits their generalizability and scalability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04589)\]. For instance, some studies have proposed using specific tokens or patterns to detect refusal behavior, but these methods often fail in adversarial or weakly aligned settings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16260)\]. Others have attempted to align LLMs with human values through fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, but these approaches typically require assumptions about the model's internal behavior \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. 

Recent research has also explored the use of attention mechanisms to understand how LLMs process and generate text, but these studies have not directly addressed the challenge of identifying refusal behavior in the activation space \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11651)\]. A notable exception is the work on behavioral bias in Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), which highlights how models can exhibit biases such as recency and authority bias, similar to human decision-making \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14025)\]. This suggests that models may encode implicit biases that affect their behavior, including refusal. 

Another line of research has focused on improving the alignment of LLMs with human values through methods such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting, which encourages models to break down complex reasoning into step-by-step solutions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.03934)\]. However, these methods often rely on external prompts and do not directly address the internal representation of refusal behavior. In contrast, the proposed COSMIC framework leverages cosine similarity to identify steering directions in the activation space, independent of model outputs \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08029)\]. This approach is inspired by prior work on multi-modal alignment, which has explored the use of joint embedding spaces to model interactions between different modalities \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15256)\]. 

The use of cosine similarity for document similarity has also been explored in earlier work, where it was shown to be effective for detecting semantic similarity at the word level \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09676)\]. While these methods are primarily focused on text, they share a common principle with COSMIC: the use of geometric properties to measure similarity between representations. This aligns with recent advances in multi-modal alignment, where methods such as Joint Generalized Cosine Similarity (JGCS) have been proposed to handle tasks involving an arbitrary number of vectors \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\].
