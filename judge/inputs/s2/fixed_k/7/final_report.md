## Related Works

Large Language Models (LLMs) have been extensively studied for their ability to encode and generate text, but their capacity to encode behaviors such as refusal remains underexplored. Prior work has primarily focused on identifying refusal through predefined templates or manual analysis, which limits their generalizability and scalability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04589)\]. For instance, some studies have proposed using specific tokens or patterns to detect refusal behavior, but these methods often fail in adversarial or weakly aligned settings \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16260)\]. Others have attempted to align LLMs with human values through fine-tuning or reinforcement learning, but these approaches typically require assumptions about the model's internal behavior \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. 

Recent research has also explored the use of attention mechanisms to understand how LLMs process and generate text, but these studies have not directly addressed the challenge of identifying refusal behavior in the activation space \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11651)\]. A notable exception is the work on behavioral bias in Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), which highlights how models can exhibit biases such as recency and authority bias, similar to human decision-making \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14025)\]. This suggests that models may encode implicit biases that affect their behavior, including refusal.

### <a id='category-model-alignment-techniques'></a>Model Alignment Techniques: 6 papers found.
The category "Model Alignment Techniques" encompasses various approaches aimed at improving the alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human values and safety constraints. Existing methods often rely on predefined templates or manual analysis to detect behaviors like refusal, which limits their effectiveness in dynamic or adversarial settings \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. In contrast, recent work has explored fine-grained interventions at the attention head level to steer model generations toward desired outcomes while bypassing safety guardrails \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. This approach demonstrates that interventions on specific attention heads can generalize to open-ended generation tasks and offer advantages over classical fine-tuning by requiring fewer examples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. Additionally, studies have highlighted the importance of incorporating unsuccessful trajectories during fine-tuning, as they provide valuable insights for improving model performance in tasks such as mathematical reasoning and strategic question answering \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16260)\]. Another line of research focuses on enhancing reasoning capabilities through chain-of-thought (COT) training, although challenges such as assessment misalignment persist, where models may assign higher scores to subpar COTs \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. To address this, Alignment Fine-Tuning (AFT) has been proposed, involving calibration of positive and negative responses with a constraint alignment loss to ensure high-quality reasoning \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144)\]. Furthermore, efforts to measure alignment between public will and model behavior have led to the development of frameworks like the chain of alignment (CoA), which enables non-experts to specify normative objectives that guide model behavior \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.14025)\]. These diverse techniques underscore the ongoing efforts to refine model alignment, balancing effectiveness with robustness across different alignment conditions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\].

The category "Document Similarity Using Cosine Metrics" explores various approaches to measuring document similarity, with a focus on cosine similarity and its extensions. More advanced techniques, such as Textual Spatial Cosine Similarity, aim to address this limitation by incorporating word placement information to detect semantic similitude, offering a middle ground between traditional cosine similarity and computationally intensive semantic analysis methods \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.03934)\]. In parallel, research on Large Language Models (LLMs) highlights the challenges of evaluating causal reasoning and the need for benchmarks that go beyond simple retrieval of domain knowledge, emphasizing the importance of interventional and counterfactual reasoning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08029)\]. Additionally, efforts to improve the accuracy of Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) systems have introduced methods like Frame Semantic-based retrieval, which leverage cognitive linguistic theories to enhance the relevance and effectiveness of retrieved information \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05945)\]. These developments underscore the evolving landscape of document similarity and LLM evaluation, where cosine-based metrics continue to play a central role while being complemented by more sophisticated semantic and contextual approaches.

[Snippet]: «Alignment remains a crucial task in multi-modal deep learning, and contrastive learning has been widely applied in this field. However, when there are more than two modalities, existing methods typically calculate pairwise loss function and aggregate them into a composite loss function for the optimization of model parameters. This limitation mainly stems from the drawbacks of traditional similarity measurement method (i.e. they can only calculate the similarity between two vectors). To address this issue, we propose a novel similarity measurement method: the Joint Generalized Cosine Similarity (JGCS). Unlike traditional pairwise methods (e.g., dot product or cosine similarity), JGCS centers around the angle derived from the Gram determinant.
