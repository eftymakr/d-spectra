This limitation has prompted the exploration of supervised training for genomic profile prediction as a more effective alternative to pure sequence pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05628)\].

### <a id='category-task-specific-vs.-generalized-pre-training-models'></a>Task-Specific vs. Generalized Pre-Training Models: 7 papers found.
The category "Task-Specific vs. Generalized Pre-Training Models" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the effectiveness and efficiency of pre-training models, particularly in domains such as natural language processing, machine translation, and genomic data science. Several studies highlight the limitations of purely unsupervised or generalized pre-training methods, which may fail to capture task-specific nuances or introduce issues like bias and toxicity \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00800)\]. In contrast, task-specific pre-training strategies, such as those involving synthetic data generation or domain-specific supervision, have shown promise in enhancing model performance while mitigating ethical and practical concerns \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09864)\]. For example, in genomic data science, pre-training models with task-specific supervision on genomic profiles, such as chromatin accessibility, has been demonstrated to yield more effective DNA representations than pure sequence-based pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05628)\]. This aligns with findings in machine translation, where synthetic data and obfuscated training techniques have enabled models to achieve strong performance even with limited or purely synthetic resources \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09864)\]. Additionally, the use of hyper-representations derived from model zoos has been proposed as a way to generate new, high-performing models by leveraging knowledge from existing architectures \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.10951)\]. In graph-based tasks, frameworks like GraphPrompt have been developed to unify pre-training and downstream tasks through task-specific prompting, allowing models to better locate relevant knowledge during inference \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15317)\]. These approaches underscore the importance of balancing generalization with task-specific adaptation, as overly generalized models may struggle with domain-specific challenges, while overly task-specific models may lack flexibility \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.11314)\]. Overall, the literature emphasizes the need for tailored pre-training strategies that account for the unique requirements of different domains and tasks \[[23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05340)\].
