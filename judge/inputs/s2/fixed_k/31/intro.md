## Related Works

Recent advances in pre-training methodologies have inspired researchers to apply unsupervised learning paradigms to DNA sequence analysis. However, these approaches alone yield suboptimal results because pure DNA sequences lack sufficient information, as their functions are regulated by genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05628)\]. This limitation has prompted the exploration of supervised training for genomic profile prediction as a more effective alternative to pure sequence pre-training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05628)\]. 

Several studies have investigated self-supervised pre-training for sequential transfer learning in neural networks, highlighting the benefits of leveraging unlabeled data to improve model performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00800)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in domains where labeled data is scarce, such as genomics. For instance, in land cover classification, self-supervised contrastive pre-training has been used to improve performance on downstream tasks by leveraging the large availability of satellite imagery \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.06041)\]. Similarly, in 3D point cloud segmentation, self-supervised pre-training on image data has been shown to reduce the need for manual annotations \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11801)\]. These findings underscore the importance of pre-training strategies that can effectively utilize unlabeled data to enhance downstream performance.

In the context of DNA analysis, DNAGPT was proposed as a generalized pre-trained model trained on over 200 billion base pairs from all mammals, incorporating tasks such as DNA sequence order classification and guanine-cytosine content prediction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05628)\]. This model demonstrated superior performance in genomic signal and region recognition tasks compared to models designed for specific downstream tasks. However, it primarily focused on sequence-based tasks without explicitly addressing genomic profiles such as chromatin accessibility.

The importance of genomic profiles in DNA function has also been highlighted in studies on chromatin accessibility prediction. For example, SemanticCAP introduced a gene language model to capture contextual information from gene sequences, leading to improved performance in chromatin accessibility prediction compared to traditional methods \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02130)\]. This work emphasized the need to incorporate contextual information from genomic sequences to better understand DNA function.

In addition, the use of mixture of experts (MoE) for molecular property prediction has been explored, where the GNN-MoCE architecture was introduced to exploit task commonalities while addressing homogeneity issues in the expert pool \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03292)\]. This approach has shown promising results in tasks with limited data or high imbalance. Similarly, the SPACE model leverages MoE to better capture the relationships between DNA sequences across different species and genomic profiles, thereby learning more effective DNA representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05628)\].
