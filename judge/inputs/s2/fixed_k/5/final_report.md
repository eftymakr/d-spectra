## Related Works

Instruction-following text embedding has emerged as a critical area of research, aiming to generate dynamic text embeddings that adapt to user instructions by highlighting specific attributes of the text \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. Prior work has explored various approaches to achieve this goal, but many suffer from significant computational overhead due to the necessity of re-encoding the entire corpus for each new instruction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. This challenge is particularly pronounced in large-scale datasets, where the repeated encoding process becomes computationally prohibitive \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\].

Several studies have attempted to address this issue by leveraging pre-trained models and fine-tuning them on instruction-response pairs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00096)\]. For instance, InBedder \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\] proposes a method that fine-tunes language models on abstractive question-answering tasks to improve instruction-following capabilities. This approach treats the instruction as a question about the input text and encodes the expected answers to obtain the representation accordingly \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. Similarly, InF-Embed \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21439)\] introduces a contrastive learning framework that aligns retrieval outcomes precisely with user intents, using a large-scale, high-quality training corpus tailored for instruction-following retrieval. These methods, however, often require extensive fine-tuning and are not lightweight in terms of computational efficiency \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21439)\].

Another line of research focuses on the use of instruction-following data generated by large language models to train models without explicit instruction-following data \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00096)\]. For example, non-instructional fine-tuning \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00096)\] uses the first half of a random text from OpenWebText as the instruction and GPT-3.5-turbo or GPT-4-turbo to complete the text as the response. This approach has shown that pre-trained LLMs fine-tuned on this data can gain instruction-following capabilities without requiring instruction-response pairs \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00096)\]. This is a promising direction, as it reduces the dependency on labeled data and enables more scalable training processes.

Moreover, recent work has investigated the internal mechanisms of LLMs to understand how they encode information related to instruction-following success \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14516)\]. This includes identifying a direction in the input embedding space that predicts whether a response will comply with a given instruction \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14516)\]. Instruction-following in language models has been a focal point of recent research, with various approaches aiming to enhance the alignment between models and human instructions. Early methods relied on reinforcement learning (RL) to train agents to execute language-like commands in simulated environments, but these approaches often required extensive training data and were limited by the rigidity of synthetic templates \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. To address these limitations, researchers have explored pre-trained models, such as BERT, to improve zero-shot transfer from synthetic commands to natural human instructions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. This work highlights the importance of leveraging pre-trained text-based language models to bridge the gap between agent-centric motor behavior and text-based representation learning \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09642)\]. Additionally, efforts have been made to generalize instruction-following to non-English languages with limited resources, introducing frameworks like UVLN that combine large language models (e.g., GPT3) with image caption models (e.g., BLIP) to enable cross-lingual vision-language navigation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.09382)\]. Another line of research has focused on instruction fine-tuning, where models are trained on instruction-response pairs to learn to follow instructions and align with human preferences \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07467)\]. However, recent studies have shown that instruction-following can also be achieved through implicit tuning, where models are trained on responses without explicit instruction-response pairs, leading to broad instruction-following behavior \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17998)\]. Furthermore, the creation of large-scale instruction datasets has been addressed through AI-evolved instruction generation, where models like Evol-Instruct are used to generate complex instructions, improving the quality of instruction data compared to human-created ones \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01196)\]. These approaches collectively demonstrate the evolving landscape of instruction-following methods, emphasizing the role of pre-trained models, fine-tuning strategies, and data generation techniques in enhancing model performance and adaptability.
