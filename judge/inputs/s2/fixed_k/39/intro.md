## Related Works

De novo genome assembly is a fundamental challenge in bioinformatics, requiring the reconstruction of a genome from short, overlapping DNA sequences without a reference genome \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147)\]. This process is computationally intensive, involving complex algorithms and large memory footprints, especially for large genomes \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04453)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to address these challenges, ranging from traditional de Bruijn graph-based methods to more recent distributed and parallel computing techniques \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04350)\].

Early methods such as the de Bruijn graph approach have been widely used due to their effectiveness in handling short reads \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05013)\]. However, they suffer from high memory usage, which becomes a critical bottleneck when dealing with large-scale genomic data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055)\]. To mitigate this, sparse de Bruijn graph structures have been introduced, significantly reducing memory consumption while maintaining comparable assembly quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.08785)\]. These sparse structures allow for efficient error correction and polymorphism resolution, which are essential for accurate genome assembly \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1106.2603)\].

Distributed computing frameworks have also been explored to improve scalability and performance. For example, the PPA-assembler utilizes the Pregel framework to enable efficient de novo assembly in a distributed setting \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556)\]. Similarly, the HipMER assembler employs efficient parallelization techniques to handle extreme-scale genome assembly on large supercomputers \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06719)\]. These approaches demonstrate the importance of parallelism in managing the computational demands of de novo assembly \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.01916)\].

Recent advancements have also focused on improving the efficiency of specific steps in the assembly pipeline, such as overlap detection and layout simplification \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07270)\]. The diBELLA 2D pipeline, for instance, introduces new distributed-memory algorithms based on linear-algebra operations over semirings, achieving near-linear scaling with high parallel efficiency \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07877)\]. These methods highlight the potential of leveraging mathematical structures to enhance computational performance \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03532)\].

Another critical area of research is the integration of long-read sequencing technologies, which provide longer and more accurate reads but introduce new challenges in assembly \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01385)\]. Tools like ntLink have been developed to improve genome scaffolding using long-read data, offering a resource-efficient approach to enhance draft assemblies \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1308.2012)\]. These tools demonstrate the importance of adapting assembly strategies to the characteristics of the input data \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05538)\].

Furthermore, the use of quantum computing and quantum-inspired optimization techniques has been proposed as a novel approach to solving genome assembly problems \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1305.6760)\]. These methods aim to leverage the inherent parallelism of quantum systems to address the computational complexity of de novo assembly \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1310.1653)\].
