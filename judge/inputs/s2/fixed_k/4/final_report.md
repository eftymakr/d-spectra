## Related Works

The problem of handling noisy annotations in machine learning has been extensively studied, with a growing body of literature focusing on the challenges posed by label noise in various learning settings. Early approaches to label noise typically assumed class-dependent noise, where the noise rate was uniform across all instances of a given class \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. However, recent studies have highlighted that real-world label noise is often instance-dependent, meaning that the likelihood of a label error varies with the difficulty of the instance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16966)\]. This distinction is crucial, as it affects the design of noise mitigation strategies and the evaluation of model performance.

Several methods have been proposed to address label noise in deep learning. One line of work focuses on estimating the noise rates and adjusting the loss function accordingly. For example, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\] introduces a second-order approach that leverages covariance terms between noise rates and Bayes optimal labels to correct for imbalances caused by instance-dependent noise. This method does not require access to ground truth labels or prior knowledge of noise rates, making it particularly useful in real-world scenarios where such information is unavailable. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10906)\] proposes a method that separates noisy training data into clean and unlabeled sets and uses a balanced loss based on model bias to improve performance on long-tailed noisy data.

Another approach involves generating synthetic samples to mitigate the impact of noisy labels. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02620)\] introduces a framework that creates synthetic samples by mixing original samples with their top-K nearest neighbors, using a mixture model to compute weights. This method helps the model learn more robust representations by reducing the influence of noisy labels. Additionally, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12275)\] proposes a method that estimates soft targets by gradually correcting noisy labels, leading to more accurate approximations of ground truth labels.

In the context of scene graph generation, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.05142)\] addresses the issue of noisy labels by introducing NICEST, a novel strategy that detects and corrects noisy samples. This method includes three main steps: detecting noisy samples, reassigning soft labels, and designing an effective training strategy. NICEST is particularly useful in scenarios where ground-truth annotations are unreliable, as it enables the model to learn unbiased fusion knowledge through multi-teacher knowledge distillation.

In the field of metric learning, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.01189)\] proposes a Smooth Proxy-Anchor Loss that is robust to noisy labels. This method introduces a confidence module that computes sample class confidences and uses them to weight the influence of each sample during training. This approach leads to more robust sample embeddings, improving performance in noisy labeling conditions.

### <a id='category-label-noise-assumptions-and-models'></a>Label Noise Assumptions and Models: 6 papers found.
The category "Label Noise Assumptions and Models" explores various approaches to handling label noise in machine learning, particularly in deep learning and object detection scenarios. Several studies address the challenges posed by noisy annotations, emphasizing that traditional methods often assume label noise is class-dependent or uniform, which may not hold in real-world settings where noise is instance-dependent \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. For instance, some research highlights that human-annotated labels are more likely to be error-prone based on task difficulty, leading to imbalanced training data and requiring more sophisticated models to account for this variability \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.11854)\]. Other works focus on long-tailed and noisy datasets, proposing methods that combine sample selection with balanced loss functions to mitigate class imbalance and model bias \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16966)\]. In domain adaptation, label noise from ambiguous instances can distort the source distribution, necessitating frameworks that explicitly address noisy annotations, such as the Noise Latent Transferability Exploration (NLTE) approach \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.13316)\]. Additionally, studies have examined how label noise affects debiasing techniques, showing that existing methods may fail when noisy labels are misinterpreted as difficult samples, leading to the development of new strategies like denoising after entropy-based debiasing \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.10906)\]. In specialized domains like X-ray image detection, label noise from overlapping items and incorrect bounding boxes is a significant challenge, prompting innovations such as label-aware data augmentation techniques to improve robustness \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02620)\]. These efforts collectively underscore the need for more nuanced models that account for the complex and varied nature of label noise in real-world applications.

[Snippet]: «A popular approach within the signal processing and machine learning communities consists in modeling signals as sparse linear combinations of atoms selected from a learned dictionary. While this paradigm has led to numerous empirical successes in various fields ranging from image to audio processing, there have only been a few theoretical arguments supporting these evidences. In particular, sparse coding, or sparse dictionary learning, relies on a non-convex procedure whose local minima have not been fully analyzed yet. In this paper, we consider a probabilistic model of sparse signals, and show that, with high probability, sparse coding admits a local minimum around the reference dictionary generating the signals. This paper considers the case of over-complete dictionaries, noisy signals, and possible outliers, thus extending the previous work limited to noiseless settings and/or undercomplete dictionaries. The analysis we conduct is non-asymptotic and makes it possible to understand how the key quantities of the problem, such as the coherence or the level of noise, can scale with respect to the dimension of the signals, the number of atoms, the sparsity, and the number of observations.»
