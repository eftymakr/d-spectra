## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction from 2D images, leveraging deep learning to implicitly encode geometry and appearance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\]. These methods often rely on multi-view inputs to optimize the reconstructed scene by minimizing the uncertainty across views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often limited by the number of input views, especially in complex or dynamic scenes where certain angles are never captured \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. To address this limitation, researchers have explored data augmentation techniques, such as video frame interpolation, to generate additional views \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\].

Video frame interpolation has been extensively studied, with early approaches relying on optical flow estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. More recent methods have integrated these steps into a single process, using deep neural networks to estimate spatially adaptive convolution kernels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. These models often use a combination of optical flow and visibility maps to handle occlusions and motion boundaries \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Adaptive separable convolutions have also been proposed to reduce computational costs while maintaining high-quality results \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01280)\]. A key innovation in this area is the use of channel attention mechanisms to replace optical flow estimation, leading to improved performance in challenging scenarios \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\].

In addition to frame interpolation, other approaches have focused on improving the quality of neural rendering by incorporating geometric priors. For instance, methods like NeuS and NeuRIS have introduced novel representations to enhance surface reconstruction, particularly in scenes with self-occlusion or textureless regions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13597)\]. These methods often rely on signed distance functions (SDF) and normal priors to guide the optimization process, leading to more accurate and robust reconstructions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13597)\]. Similarly, the Manhattan-world assumption has been used to regularize depth maps in indoor scene reconstruction, improving the handling of planar regions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02836)\].

The integration of implicit surface models with radiance fields has also been explored, leading to unified frameworks that enable both surface and volume rendering \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. This approach allows for more efficient sampling and accurate surface reconstruction without requiring explicit masks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. Furthermore, the use of neural implicit surfaces has been extended to handle large-scale scenes and dynamic environments, with methods like Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF) offering faster inference and higher quality results compared to traditional approaches \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\].

### <a id='category-video-frame-interpolation-techniques'></a>Video Frame Interpolation Techniques: 11 papers found.
Video frame interpolation techniques have evolved significantly, with recent approaches focusing on integrating motion estimation and pixel synthesis into a unified framework. Traditional methods often rely on optical flow estimation, which can be computationally intensive and prone to error propagation in challenging scenarios like occlusion or large motion \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. To address these limitations, several studies have proposed end-to-end deep learning models that directly estimate spatially adaptive convolution kernels for pixel synthesis, enabling high-quality frame interpolation without explicit optical flow computation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00665)\]. For instance, one method formulates frame interpolation as a single convolution process, allowing the model to handle abrupt brightness changes and motion blur effectively \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. Another approach uses separable convolutions with 1D kernels, reducing parameter count and enabling efficient estimation of motion and resampling simultaneously \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\]. Additionally, some works have introduced context-aware synthesis strategies, where contextual information from input frames is warped and blended to produce high-quality intermediate frames \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08845)\]. These methods demonstrate that combining motion and synthesis in a unified neural network can lead to robust and efficient video frame interpolation, particularly when trained on large-scale video datasets without requiring ground-truth annotations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00665)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.01692)\].

### <a id='category-implicit-surface-and-radiance-field-integration'></a>Implicit Surface and Radiance Field Integration: 10 papers found.
The category "Implicit Surface and Radiance Field Integration" explores methods that combine implicit surface representations with radiance fields to enhance 3D scene reconstruction and rendering. These approaches aim to overcome the limitations of traditional volumetric rendering by integrating geometric and appearance information more effectively. For instance, Point-NeRF uses neural 3D point clouds to model radiance fields, enabling efficient rendering and faster training compared to NeRF \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.08845)\]. Similarly, Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF) introduce a sparse voxel octree structure to accelerate rendering while maintaining high quality, achieving over 10x speed improvements over NeRF \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\]. Other works, such as those proposing implicit functions like truncated signed distance functions, address the issue of surface reconstruction by avoiding volumetric artifacts \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04532)\]. Additionally, methods like those unifying implicit surface models and radiance fields enable surface and volume rendering with the same model, improving reconstruction quality without requiring input masks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. Implicit representations are also leveraged for differentiable rendering, allowing direct learning from RGB images without 3D supervision, and enabling multi-view 3D reconstruction \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\]. These techniques collectively demonstrate the potential of integrating implicit surfaces with radiance fields to achieve more accurate and efficient 3D scene modeling.
