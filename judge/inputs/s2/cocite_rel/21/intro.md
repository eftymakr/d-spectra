## Related Works

Neural rendering has emerged as a powerful paradigm for 3D scene reconstruction from 2D images, leveraging deep learning to implicitly encode geometry and appearance \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1912.07372)\]. These methods often rely on multi-view inputs to optimize the reconstructed scene by minimizing the uncertainty across views \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. However, the quality of reconstruction is often limited by the number of input views, especially in complex or dynamic scenes where certain angles are never captured \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. To address this limitation, researchers have explored data augmentation techniques, such as video frame interpolation, to generate additional views \[[27](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.00884)\].

Video frame interpolation has been extensively studied, with early approaches relying on optical flow estimation and pixel synthesis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. More recent methods have integrated these steps into a single process, using deep neural networks to estimate spatially adaptive convolution kernels \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\]. These models often use a combination of optical flow and visibility maps to handle occlusions and motion boundaries \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00080)\]. Adaptive separable convolutions have also been proposed to reduce computational costs while maintaining high-quality results \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.01280)\]. A key innovation in this area is the use of channel attention mechanisms to replace optical flow estimation, leading to improved performance in challenging scenarios \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/bb4a9650ca3946c70a7e92007cc61dc0dfd75522)\].

In addition to frame interpolation, other approaches have focused on improving the quality of neural rendering by incorporating geometric priors. For instance, methods like NeuS and NeuRIS have introduced novel representations to enhance surface reconstruction, particularly in scenes with self-occlusion or textureless regions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13597)\]. These methods often rely on signed distance functions (SDF) and normal priors to guide the optimization process, leading to more accurate and robust reconstructions \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.10689)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13597)\]. Similarly, the Manhattan-world assumption has been used to regularize depth maps in indoor scene reconstruction, improving the handling of planar regions \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02836)\].

The integration of implicit surface models with radiance fields has also been explored, leading to unified frameworks that enable both surface and volume rendering \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. This approach allows for more efficient sampling and accurate surface reconstruction without requiring explicit masks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.10078)\]. Furthermore, the use of neural implicit surfaces has been extended to handle large-scale scenes and dynamic environments, with methods like Neural Sparse Voxel Fields (NSVF) offering faster inference and higher quality results compared to traditional approaches \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.11571)\].

In the context of video frame interpolation, recent work has focused on end-to-end learning, where models are trained directly on video data without requiring manual annotations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07514)\].
