## Related Works

Neural retrieval models have seen significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the domain of Web search. These models often rely on large-scale labeled query-document pairs for training, which are typically expensive to obtain \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. With the availability of extensive document collections such as ClueWeb22, synthetic queries generated by large language models have emerged as a scalable alternative \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. However, the quality of these synthetic queries can vary, leading to suboptimal performance in downstream retrieval tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Existing methods often rely on external re-rankers to filter out noisy query-document pairs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. In contrast, our approach integrates ranking signals directly into the query generation process using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), aiming to improve the quality of generated queries \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\].

DPO has been widely studied for its ability to align large language models with human preferences without the need for a reward model or reinforcement learning \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. It operates by fine-tuning a model to increase the likelihood of preferred responses over dispreferred ones using a simple classification loss \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. This approach has shown stability and performance comparable to or better than existing methods like PPO-based RLHF \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. However, DPO has limitations, particularly in cases where the relative probability between preferred and dispreferred examples increases, potentially reducing the likelihood of preferred examples \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. To address this, DPOP was introduced, which modifies the loss function to avoid this failure mode \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\].

Similarly, the STAR and ADORE strategies were proposed to improve the stability and performance of dense retrieval models by introducing dynamic negative sampling \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. These methods highlight the importance of carefully selecting negative examples during training to ensure effective ranking.

In addition to retrieval, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has been extensively explored for aligning language models with human preferences \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862)\]. ### <a id='category-direct-preference-optimization-(dpo)-framework'></a>Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) Framework: 3 papers found.
The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) framework represents a significant advancement in aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences, offering a more stable and efficient alternative to traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) methods \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. Unlike RLHF, which involves complex and often unstable procedures such as reward model fitting and reinforcement learning, DPO introduces a novel parameterization of the reward model that allows for the extraction of the optimal policy in closed form, simplifying the fine-tuning process and eliminating the need for sampling from the LM during training \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]. This approach is further supported by related work that explores the integration of preference signals directly into the model training process, enhancing the quality of generated queries and improving downstream retrieval performance \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11907)\]. For instance, methods like Direct Large Model Alignment (DLMA) leverage contrastive prompt pairs to automatically generate preference data and use DPO to align LLMs without relying on human-annotated data, demonstrating the framework's versatility and effectiveness \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11907)\]. Additionally, Direct Nash Optimization (DNO) extends these ideas by merging contrastive learning with general preference optimization, achieving monotonic improvement and outperforming strong baselines such as GPT-4 \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715)\]. These contributions collectively highlight the growing trend towards more direct and efficient methods for preference-based model alignment, reducing dependency on complex reward maximization frameworks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)\]\[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03715)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11907)\].

### <a id='category-dpo-limitations-and-variants'></a>DPO Limitations and Variants: 3 papers found.
The category "DPO Limitations and Variants" highlights ongoing research into improving the effectiveness and reliability of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for aligning large language models with human preferences. DPO, which eliminates the need for reward modeling and reinforcement learning, has shown promise in enhancing model performance on downstream tasks like reasoning and alignment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. However, it relies on the assumption that pairwise preferences can be substituted with pointwise rewards, which can lead to issues such as reduced likelihood of preferred examples during training \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)\]. To address these limitations, variants like DPO-Positive (DPOP) have been introduced, which modify the loss function to avoid the failure mode of likelihood reduction while maintaining performance across diverse datasets \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228)\]. Additionally, DPO with an offset (ODPO) has been proposed to handle varying preference strengths by introducing an offset value that ensures a minimum likelihood difference between preferred and dispreferred responses \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10571)\]. These advancements demonstrate a growing effort to refine DPO's theoretical foundations and practical implementation, ensuring more robust and generalizable alignment with human preferences.
