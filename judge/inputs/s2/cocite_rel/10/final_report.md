## Related Works

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) in the training of large language models (LLMs). While NTP involves predicting one token at a time, MTP extends this by predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously, often using multiple independent output heads that operate on top of a shared model trunk \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to improve downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency, especially for large models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. However, smaller language models (SLMs) have struggled with the MTP objective, necessitating the development of curriculum learning strategies to gradually adapt them to MTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

Curriculum learning has been widely used in machine learning to improve training efficiency and model performance. In the context of sequence prediction, scheduled sampling has been proposed to gradually shift the training process from fully guided prediction using true previous tokens to less guided prediction using generated tokens \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. This approach helps mitigate error accumulation during inference. Similarly, in the context of MTP, a forward curriculum strategy has been introduced, which gradually increases the complexity of the pre-training objective from NTP to MTP \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This strategy enables SLMs to better leverage MTP during pre-training, improving downstream NTP performance and generative output quality while retaining the benefits of self-speculative decoding \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\].

In contrast, a reverse curriculum strategy has also been explored, which starts with MTP and gradually reduces the number of tokens predicted \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach achieves stronger NTP performance and output quality but fails to provide the self-speculative decoding benefits observed with the forward curriculum \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. The effectiveness of curriculum learning in MTP training highlights the importance of carefully designing the training process to accommodate the unique challenges of multi-token prediction.

Several prior works have investigated the implications of MTP on model behavior and performance. For example, the use of pause tokens during training and inference has been shown to improve the quality of generated responses by allowing the model to process more computation before committing to an answer \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02226)\]. Additionally, the introduction of the Medusa framework, which adds extra decoding heads to predict multiple subsequent tokens in parallel, has demonstrated significant speedups in inference while maintaining generation quality \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774)\]. These approaches align with the broader trend of exploring parallel and speculative decoding techniques to enhance the efficiency of LLMs.

### <a id='category-multi-token-prediction-(mtp)-frameworks'></a>Multi-token Prediction (MTP) Frameworks: 4 papers found.
Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a promising alternative to traditional next-token prediction (NTP) in pre-training language models, offering benefits in downstream performance, inference speed, and training efficiency \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Unlike NTP, which predicts one token at a time, MTP trains models to predict multiple future tokens simultaneously using independent output heads, while sharing a common model trunk \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. This approach has been shown to improve generative capabilities, particularly in coding tasks, where models trained with MTP outperform next-token models by significant margins \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. For instance, 13B parameter models trained with MTP solve 12% more problems on HumanEval and 17% more on MBPP compared to comparable NTP models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.19737)\]. Additionally, MTP has been applied to speech large language models (LLMs), enabling simultaneous improvements in generation speed and quality by shifting from standard NTP to MTP \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.04060)\]. The ProphetNet model further advances MTP by introducing a novel self-supervised objective called future n-gram prediction and an n-stream self-attention mechanism, which allows the model to plan for future tokens and avoid overfitting to local correlations \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04063)\]. However, integrating MTP into frozen LLMs remains challenging, as their hidden layers are highly specialized for NTP, making adaptation non-trivial \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09419)\]. These findings highlight the potential of MTP, while also identifying key challenges in its implementation and adaptation across different model architectures and tasks.

### <a id='category-error-accumulation-mitigation'></a>Error Accumulation Mitigation: 3 papers found.
Error accumulation during sequence generation remains a critical challenge in training language models, with several studies highlighting its implications for both training and inference phases. Research has shown that the discrepancy between teacher-forced training, where the model is provided with the true previous token, and autoregressive inference, where the model generates its own previous token, can lead to error propagation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. This issue is particularly pronounced in tasks where teacher-forcing fails to learn an accurate next-token predictor, even when the task is straightforward \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. To mitigate this, curriculum learning has been proposed as a strategy to gradually shift the training process from fully guided to less guided schemes, reducing the gap between training and inference \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. This approach has demonstrated significant improvements in sequence prediction tasks and was successfully applied in image captioning \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03099)\]. Additionally, alternative training methods such as teacherless training, which uses dummy tokens to predict multiple tokens in advance, have been explored as potential solutions to the limitations of teacher-forcing \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06963)\]. These findings underscore the importance of addressing error accumulation through careful design of training objectives and strategies.

### <a id='category-parallel-decoding-frameworks'></a>Parallel Decoding Frameworks: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Large Language Models (LLMs) employ auto-regressive decoding that requires sequential computation, with each step reliant on the previous one's output. This creates a bottleneck as each step necessitates moving the full model parameters from High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) to the accelerator's cache. While methods such as speculative decoding have been suggested to address this issue, their implementation is impeded by the challenges associated with acquiring and maintaining a separate draft model. In this paper, we present Medusa, an efficient method that augments LLM inference by adding extra decoding heads to predict multiple subsequent tokens in parallel. Using a tree-based attention mechanism, Medusa constructs multiple candidate continuations and verifies them simultaneously in each decoding step. By leveraging parallel processing, Medusa substantially reduces the number of decoding steps required. We present two levels of fine-tuning procedures for Medusa to meet the needs of different use cases: Medusa-1: Medusa is directly fine-tuned on top of a frozen backbone LLM, enabling lossless inference acceleration. Medusa-2: Medusa is fine-tuned together with the backbone LLM, enabling better prediction accuracy of Medusa heads and higher speedup but needing a special training recipe that preserves the backbone model's capabilities. Moreover, we propose several extensions that improve or expand the utility of Medusa, including a self-distillation to handle situations where no training data is available and a typical acceptance scheme to boost the acceptance rate while maintaining generation quality. We evaluate Medusa on models of various sizes and training procedures. Our experiments demonstrate that Medusa-1 can achieve over 2.2x speedup without compromising generation quality, while Medusa-2 further improves the speedup to 2.3-3.6x.»
