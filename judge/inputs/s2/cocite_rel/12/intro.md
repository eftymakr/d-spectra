## Related Works

Event cameras, also known as dynamic vision sensors (DVS), have gained significant attention in recent years due to their ability to capture asynchronous, high-temporal-resolution scene changes on a per-pixel basis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. This capability makes them particularly suitable for applications involving high-speed motion or challenging lighting conditions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06310)\]. However, the asynchronous nature of event data introduces unique challenges, including high bandwidth, memory, and computational demands \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. Prior work has attempted to mitigate these issues through event downsampling techniques, which aim to reduce the volume of event data while preserving useful information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\].

Most existing approaches to event downsampling rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. These methods, while simple, often lack adaptability to varying scene dynamics and application-specific requirements \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. For instance, threshold-based strategies may fail in scenes with low motion or high contrast, leading to loss of critical event information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. Furthermore, these methods typically do not account for the structural importance of events, which can be crucial for maintaining performance in downstream tasks such as object classification or detection \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\].

To address these limitations, recent research has explored more sophisticated, adaptive methods for event sampling. One such approach is the use of probabilistic models to estimate the importance of individual events \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. For example, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06898)\] proposes a method that learns the importance of events through a probabilistic framework, allowing for scene-specific adaptation. Similarly, \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07913)\] introduces a contour-preserving representation of event data, which prioritizes structurally important events to improve performance in tasks like image interpolation and surface normal estimation \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07913)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of adaptive, scene-aware event sampling strategies.

In addition to adaptive sampling, there is growing interest in zero-shot event downsampling, where downsampled events must remain usable for models trained on the original event stream without task-specific adaptation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. This approach is particularly relevant for applications where retraining models is impractical or inefficient \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01310)\]. For instance, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\] presents a method that enables zero-shot event downsampling by preserving the structural information of events, ensuring compatibility with pre-trained models \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.08405)\]. This aligns with the broader goal of making event cameras more accessible for a wide range of vision tasks.

Several datasets and benchmarking frameworks have been developed to evaluate event-based vision algorithms \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10202)\]. For example, the Multi Vehicle Stereo Event Camera Dataset \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10202)\] provides synchronized event streams, grayscale images, and inertial measurements, enabling comprehensive evaluation of event-based perception systems \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.10202)\].
