## Related Works

Diffusion models have gained significant traction in image and video generation due to their ability to produce high-quality, diverse outputs \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. However, the computational and memory demands of these models hinder their deployment on edge devices \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a promising technique to reduce model size and accelerate inference without retraining \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\]. Early PTQ methods, however, were not tailored for diffusion models, which exhibit unique characteristics such as multi-time-step structures and bimodal activation distributions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03458)\]. 

Several works have attempted to address these challenges. Q-Diffusion \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\] introduced a novel PTQ method specifically tailored for diffusion models, focusing on timestep-aware calibration and split shortcut quantization. This approach enabled the quantization of full-precision models into 4-bit while maintaining performance \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10752)\]. Similarly, PTQ4DiT \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\] was proposed to handle the unique challenges of diffusion transformers, including salient channels with extreme magnitudes and temporal variability in activation distributions. It introduced Channel-wise Salience Balancing (CSB) and Spearmen's ρ-guided Salience Calibration (SSC) to mitigate quantization errors \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]. 

Temporal Dynamic Quantization \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\] further advanced the field by dynamically adjusting the quantization interval based on time step information, significantly improving output quality while maintaining low computational overhead \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. This method is compatible with both PTQ and quantization-aware training (QAT), offering flexibility in deployment scenarios \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]. 

In the realm of vision transformers, PTQ4ViT \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\] proposed twin uniform quantization to reduce quantization error on activation values, achieving near-lossless prediction accuracy at 8-bit quantization \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. FQ-ViT \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\] further improved this by introducing Power-of-Two Factor (PTF) and Log-Int-Softmax (LIS) to sustain extreme non-uniform distributions in attention maps \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748)\]. 

For diffusion transformers, ViDiT-Q \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\] was designed to address the challenges of text-to-image and video tasks, achieving W8A8 and W4A8 quantization with negligible degradation in visual quality \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\]. This work highlighted the importance of tailoring quantization schemes to the specific characteristics of diffusion transformers \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.02316)\].

These works collectively demonstrate the ongoing efforts to develop efficient quantization methods for diffusion models, addressing the unique challenges posed by their architecture and generation process. ### <a id='category-post-training-quantization-techniques'></a>Post-Training Quantization Techniques: 9 papers found.
Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as a critical technique for accelerating diffusion models and reducing their computational footprint without requiring retraining \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. However, traditional PTQ methods face significant challenges when applied to diffusion models due to the changing output distributions of noise estimation networks across multiple time steps and the bimodal activation distributions within these networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]. To address these issues, researchers have proposed specialized PTQ methods tailored for diffusion models, such as PTQ4DM \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\], which focuses on compressing the noise estimation network by exploring quantized operations, calibration datasets, and metrics \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]. Similarly, other works like PTQD \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\] and Q-DiT \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\] have introduced advanced calibration strategies and mixed-precision schemes to mitigate quantization noise and improve performance. These methods aim to maintain or even enhance the quality of generated samples while reducing bit operations and memory usage \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]. Additionally, PTQ4DiT \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\] has been specifically designed for Diffusion Transformers (DiTs), addressing challenges such as spatial and temporal variance in weights and activations by employing techniques like Channel-wise Salience Balancing and Spearmen's ρ-guided calibration \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in PTQ for diffusion models, particularly in enabling efficient deployment on edge devices and real-time applications \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15736)\]\[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2302.04304)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10657)\]\[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17343)\]\[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16005)\].

### <a id='category-diffusion-models-in-image/video-generation'></a>Diffusion Models in Image/Video Generation: 11 papers found.
The category "Diffusion Models in Image/Video Generation" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving the efficiency, quality, and versatility of generative models for video synthesis. Researchers have explored latent diffusion models (LDMs) as a way to reduce computational demands while maintaining high-resolution output, such as the Video LDM that leverages pretrained image LDMs and introduces temporal alignment \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\]. Similarly, the Stable Video Diffusion model extends latent diffusion principles to video generation, emphasizing the importance of curated pretraining datasets and finetuning strategies for high-quality results \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\]. Other works, such as MagicVideo, focus on efficient text-to-video generation by utilizing a 3D U-Net design and latent space modeling, achieving significant computational savings compared to pixel-based methods \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11018)\]. Additionally, Latte introduces a transformer-based approach for video generation, offering scalable and high-quality results through efficient token processing and temporal modeling \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03048)\]. LaVie further advances the field by integrating cascaded video latent diffusion models with temporal interpolation and super-resolution components, demonstrating the effectiveness of joint image-video fine-tuning \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15103)\]. Imagen Video builds on these ideas by employing a cascade of video diffusion models, incorporating spatial and temporal super-resolution techniques and leveraging classifier-free guidance for high-quality sampling \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02303)\]. These efforts collectively highlight the ongoing innovation in diffusion models for video generation, focusing on scalability, quality, and the integration of advanced architectural components such as transformers and attention mechanisms \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08818)\]\[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.15127)\]\[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11018)\]\[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03048)\]\[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15103)\]\[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02303)\].
