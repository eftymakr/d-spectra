## Related Works

Personalized outfit generation has gained significant attention in recent years due to its potential to enhance user experience in fashion recommendation systems. Prior works have primarily focused on two aspects: (1) modeling the compatibility between fashion items and (2) capturing user preferences to generate personalized outfits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. Compatibility modeling is crucial as it ensures that the generated outfits are visually and stylistically coherent \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03133)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to learn compatibility between fashion items, including the use of bidirectional LSTMs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05691)\], Siamese networks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03703)\], and attention-based models \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07273)\]. These methods typically rely on user-item interaction data and aim to learn a compatibility metric that captures the relationships between different types of clothing items \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03703)\].

User preference modeling is another critical component in personalized outfit generation. Existing methods often assume that user preferences are either explicitly provided or can be inferred from historical interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. However, this approach may not be sufficient for generating outfits that align with the user's unique style \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. To address this, some studies have introduced attention mechanisms to better capture the user's preferences \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/762ec45448e915142f355848265d10dd55ab470f)\]. For instance, the OutfitNet framework uses an attention-based multiple instance learning approach to learn user preferences for fashion outfits \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/762ec45448e915142f355848265d10dd55ab470f)\]. Similarly, the $A^3$-FKG model incorporates a two-level attention mechanism to capture both user-specific preferences and the relevance of items to the user \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b5f488dd55024f0db2cabb73b055002b5d995be9)\].

In addition to compatibility and personalization, the diversity of generated outfits is also an important consideration. Some methods have attempted to improve diversity by incorporating global style information into the generation process \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03133)\]. For example, the $A^3$-FKG model leverages outfit- and item-level attributes to build a fashion knowledge graph that captures both user preferences and item compatibility \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b5f488dd55024f0db2cabb73b055002b5d995be9)\]. Similarly, the POG model connects user preferences with a Transformer architecture to generate personalized outfits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. These approaches demonstrate that combining compatibility modeling with user preference learning can lead to more effective and diverse outfit recommendations.

Recent advances in generative models have also contributed to the field of personalized outfit generation. For instance, the FashionNet model uses a two-stage training strategy to transfer a general compatibility model to a model that embeds personal preference \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02443)\]. This approach enables the model to generate outfits that are both compatible and aligned with the user's preferences.
