## Related Works

Personalized outfit generation has gained significant attention in recent years due to its potential to enhance user experience in fashion recommendation systems. Prior works have primarily focused on two aspects: (1) modeling the compatibility between fashion items and (2) capturing user preferences to generate personalized outfits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. Compatibility modeling is crucial as it ensures that the generated outfits are visually and stylistically coherent \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03133)\]. Several approaches have been proposed to learn compatibility between fashion items, including the use of bidirectional LSTMs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1707.05691)\], Siamese networks \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03703)\], and attention-based models \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.07273)\]. These methods typically rely on user-item interaction data and aim to learn a compatibility metric that captures the relationships between different types of clothing items \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03703)\].

User preference modeling is another critical component in personalized outfit generation. Existing methods often assume that user preferences are either explicitly provided or can be inferred from historical interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. However, this approach may not be sufficient for generating outfits that align with the user's unique style \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. To address this, some studies have introduced attention mechanisms to better capture the user's preferences \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/762ec45448e915142f355848265d10dd55ab470f)\]. For instance, the OutfitNet framework uses an attention-based multiple instance learning approach to learn user preferences for fashion outfits \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/762ec45448e915142f355848265d10dd55ab470f)\]. Similarly, the $A^3$-FKG model incorporates a two-level attention mechanism to capture both user-specific preferences and the relevance of items to the user \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b5f488dd55024f0db2cabb73b055002b5d995be9)\].

In addition to compatibility and personalization, the diversity of generated outfits is also an important consideration. Some methods have attempted to improve diversity by incorporating global style information into the generation process \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03133)\]. Similarly, the POG model connects user preferences with a Transformer architecture to generate personalized outfits \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. These approaches demonstrate that combining compatibility modeling with user preference learning can lead to more effective and diverse outfit recommendations.

The category "Attention-Based Architectures" encompasses several approaches aimed at improving personalized fashion outfit recommendation and generation. These methods focus on modeling both item compatibility and user preferences through attention mechanisms, enabling more accurate and personalized recommendations. For instance, the Personalized Outfit Generation (POG) model leverages Transformer architecture to connect user preferences with outfit generation, emphasizing compatibility and personalization \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.01866)\]. Similarly, the Attentive Attribute-Aware Fashion Knowledge Graph ($A^3$-FKG) incorporates a two-level attention mechanism to capture user-specific preferences and latent interests, enhancing the modeling of both outfit and item attributes \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b5f488dd55024f0db2cabb73b055002b5d995be9)\]. The OutfitNet framework also employs attention mechanisms to learn compatibility between fashion items and users' tastes, with a two-stage approach that first generates relevancy embeddings and then learns user preferences \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/762ec45448e915142f355848265d10dd55ab470f)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the importance of attention-based architectures in addressing the challenges of sparse user-outfit interactions and the need to model both item-level and outfit-level compatibility. By integrating attention mechanisms, these models aim to produce more accurate and personalized recommendations while maintaining the aesthetic and functional compatibility of fashion outfits.

### <a id='category-compatibility-modeling-techniques'></a>Compatibility Modeling Techniques: 9 papers found.
The category of compatibility modeling techniques in fashion recommendation systems focuses on improving the accuracy and personalization of outfit suggestions by addressing the compatibility between items and aligning them with user preferences. Existing approaches often tackle either the compatibility of items within an outfit or the alignment with user preferences, leading to suboptimal results \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12566)\]. To overcome this, researchers have proposed frameworks that integrate both aspects, such as the Hierarchical Fashion Graph Network (HFGN), which models relationships among users, items, and outfits simultaneously \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12566)\]. This approach constructs a hierarchical structure based on user-outfit interactions and outfit-item mappings, then employs graph neural networks to aggregate item information into an outfit representation and refine user representations using historical data \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12566)\]. Additionally, methods like the Compatibility Family introduce end-to-end trainable systems that embed items into latent vectors and project queries into compatible prototypes, capturing broader notions of compatibility \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01262)\]. Other works, such as the Style2Vec model, learn vector representations of fashion items based on their co-occurrence in matching outfits, enabling recommendations that align with users' style preferences \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04014)\]. Furthermore, frameworks like the one proposed in \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.07473)\] use Siamese CNNs to learn compatibility in a latent space by modeling co-occurrence in user behavior data, allowing for cross-category compatibility assessments. These techniques collectively highlight the importance of integrating both item compatibility and user preferences to achieve more effective and personalized outfit recommendations \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.07473)\].
