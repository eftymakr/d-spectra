## Related Works

The rapid adoption of large language models (LLMs) has underscored the critical need for benchmarking their fairness, as these models can perpetuate and amplify societal biases \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. Traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, focus on discrete accuracy-based evaluations, which fail to capture the implicit impact of model uncertainty \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. This limitation is well-documented in prior work, which highlights that conventional metrics overlook the internal bias in model decisions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. For instance, studies have shown that LLMs are 3–6 times more likely to choose occupations that stereotypically align with a person’s gender \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.14921)\]. These findings emphasize the need for fairness metrics that account for model confidence and uncertainty.

To address this gap, several researchers have proposed fairness evaluation frameworks that consider bias in different NLP tasks. For example, the HONEST dataset measures hurtful sentence completions in language models, revealing that 4.3% of the time, models complete sentences with hurtful words, often following gender-specific patterns \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4f76d26369ee573cb03db4b9f6e4ab5d61806095)\]. Similarly, the CrowS-Pairs dataset evaluates social biases in masked language models by presenting stereotypical versus non-stereotypical sentences, showing that models consistently favor stereotypical content \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.00133)\]. These datasets highlight the importance of diverse and representative benchmarks for evaluating fairness in LLMs.

The development of fairness-aware benchmarks has also been a focus of recent research. The CALM benchmark, for instance, provides a comprehensive assessment of language model bias by integrating sixteen datasets across question-answering, sentiment analysis, and natural language inference \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12539)\]. This benchmark is designed to measure gender and race bias robustly, demonstrating that larger models may exhibit higher bias than smaller ones \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12539)\]. Additionally, the BBQ dataset focuses on bias in question answering, showing that models often rely on stereotypes when the context is under-informative \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.08193)\]. These datasets underscore the need for diverse and context-aware benchmarks to evaluate fairness in LLMs.

In the realm of fairness evaluation, the HELM benchmark provides a holistic evaluation of language models, covering 16 core scenarios and 7 metrics \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ce913026f693101e54d3ab9152e107034d81fce1)\]. This benchmark aims to improve transparency by standardizing evaluation conditions across models, revealing important trade-offs in model performance \[[9](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ce913026f693101e54d3ab9152e107034d81fce1)\]. Furthermore, the MT-GenEval dataset introduces a counterfactual and contextual benchmark for evaluating gender accuracy in machine translation, offering realistic, gender-balanced data across eight language pairs \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01355)\]. These benchmarks highlight the growing effort to create more rigorous and diverse evaluation frameworks for fairness in LLM

The related works section focuses on the challenges and approaches to measuring fairness in AI systems, particularly in the context of classification tasks and algorithmic bias. Existing research has highlighted that traditional fairness metrics, such as Equalized Odds, often fail to capture the nuanced impact of model uncertainty on fairness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\]. This is because conventional metrics primarily rely on accuracy-based evaluations, which do not account for differences in model confidence across groups \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. For instance, a model may exhibit similar overall accuracy across groups but display higher confidence in incorrect predictions for certain groups, leading to biased outcomes \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. To address this gap, recent studies have proposed uncertainty-aware fairness metrics, such as UCerF, which incorporate model confidence into fairness evaluation, enabling a more granular assessment of bias \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. Additionally, there is growing recognition of the limitations of existing fairness metrics in capturing the complexities of real-world applications, especially in domains like NLP, where models can inadvertently amplify societal biases \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15859)\]. Researchers have also emphasized the importance of using extrinsic measures of bias, which evaluate fairness in downstream tasks, as these provide a more reliable indication of model fairness across different scenarios \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15859)\]. Furthermore, the development of diverse and representative datasets, such as the newly introduced gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset, is crucial for improving the fairness of AI systems \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1412.3756)\]. These efforts underscore the need for more comprehensive and context-aware fairness metrics that can better reflect the underlying biases in AI decision-making \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09635)\].
