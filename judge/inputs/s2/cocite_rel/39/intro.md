## Related Works

De novo genome assembly has been a focal point of bioinformatics research, driven by the advent of next-generation sequencing technologies that generate massive volumes of short reads \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7279acd9c61a8de019ea2d55d23124dfbb6b5269)\]. Early approaches, such as string-based and overlap-layout-consensus (OLC) assemblers, were well-suited for small genomes and short reads \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7279acd9c61a8de019ea2d55d23124dfbb6b5269)\]. However, the computational challenges of assembling large genomes from millions of short reads have persisted, necessitating the development of more efficient and scalable methods \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7279acd9c61a8de019ea2d55d23124dfbb6b5269)\]. 

Several studies have explored the use of de Bruijn graphs for short-read assembly, which has proven effective for large datasets \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7279acd9c61a8de019ea2d55d23124dfbb6b5269)\]. For example, PASHA leverages de Bruijn graphs and hybrid parallelism to improve scalability and assembly quality, achieving an NG50 contig size of 503 for human genome assembly \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7bf2f1c55f90e11a4d1df4dd608ac621a08f8b33)\]. Similarly, ABySS 2.0 introduced a Bloom filter-based approach to reduce memory usage and improve efficiency, achieving a NG50 scaffold contiguity of 3.5 Mbp using less than 35 GB of RAM \[[26](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/9549ebb6dfe8498ec6cf69d876add3db1cfe7c0c)\]. These methods demonstrate the importance of optimizing memory and computational resources for large-scale de novo assembly.

Long-read sequencing technologies, such as Oxford Nanopore and PacBio, have also been integrated into de novo assembly pipelines to improve contiguity and accuracy \[[24](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e18b489f02530ff144db28cb44537173e4296616)\]. For instance, Canu was developed specifically for noisy single-molecule sequences, introducing adaptive k-mer weighting and repeat separation to enhance assembly continuity and reduce runtime \[[4](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2b59fd18ada769ef76fb7fcc7382f063791abfab)\]. Similarly, Shasta, a long-read assembler, enabled the rapid de novo assembly of eleven human genomes using nanopore data, achieving high contiguity and accuracy within a short time \[[21](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/f9d407ce133ec096ab4321e6d98e8f0081f5bdcc)\]. These advancements highlight the potential of long-read technologies in overcoming the limitations of short-read assembly.

Despite these improvements, challenges such as memory-bound behavior, irregular data patterns, and high computational demands remain \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7279acd9c61a8de019ea2d55d23124dfbb6b5269)\]. To address these, distributed and parallel assembly methods have been proposed, such as SWAP-Assembler, which scales up to 2048 cores and generates high-quality contigs with good N50 sizes \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fe8d86bc5520b5049104ff58ee7e980982816ebf)\]. Additionally, Ray, a parallel assembler, demonstrated the ability to handle mixed sequencing technologies, reducing the number of contigs and errors by systematically integrating reads from different platforms \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/38410204a2977a9484e6d1205697b4103b5482f7)\]. These approaches underscore the need for flexible and scalable solutions that can handle the growing complexity of genome assembly tasks.
