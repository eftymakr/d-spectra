## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems have significantly enhanced speech naturalness, leading to increased demand for precise prosody control and mispronunciation correction \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Traditional approaches for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their capacity for post-hoc adjustments \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Similarly, conventional mispronunciation correction methods depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, making them less practical in low-resource settings \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. This section reviews related works that address these challenges.

Several studies have explored methods for prosody control in TTS systems. For example, \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\] introduces a hierarchical prosody modeling approach for non-autoregressive TTS, enabling the prediction of phoneme-level prosody features conditioned on word-level features. This approach improves audio quality and prosody naturalness \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.06465)\]. Similarly, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\] proposes a non-autoregressive parallel neural TTS front-end model hierarchically conditioned on both coarse and fine-grained acoustic speech features, allowing for effective control of each prosodic dimension \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of hierarchical modeling for prosody control.

In addition to prosody control, several works have focused on improving the quality and expressiveness of synthesized speech. \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\] introduces a sequence-to-sequence neural network conditioned on acoustic speech features to learn a latent prosody space with intuitive and meaningful dimensions, enabling the generation of a wide variety of speaking styles \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\] presents FastSpeech 2, which addresses the limitations of FastSpeech by directly training the model with ground-truth targets and introducing more variation information as conditional inputs, resulting in improved voice quality and faster inference speed \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\].

Another line of research has focused on controllable TTS systems that allow for fine-grained prosody transfer. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13443)\] presents CopyCat2, a model capable of synthesizing speech with different speaker identities and transferring prosody at a fine-grained level between any pair of seen speakers \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13443)\]. This work demonstrates the feasibility of multi-speaker TTS with contextually appropriate prosody. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\] introduces Non-Attentive Tacotron, which replaces the attention mechanism with an explicit duration predictor, enabling both utterance-wide and per-phoneme control of duration \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.04301)\].

### <a id='category-prosody-control-in-tts-systems'></a>Prosody Control in TTS Systems: 11 papers found.
Modern neural text-to-speech (TTS) systems have achieved high naturalness in speech synthesis, but they often lack fine-grained control over prosody, which is essential for expressive and versatile speech generation \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]. Most existing approaches rely on specialized modules or additional training for prosody manipulation, limiting their ability to perform post-hoc adjustments \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Similarly, traditional methods for mispronunciation correction depend on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are impractical in low-resource scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Recent work has focused on improving prosody control by conditioning models on acoustic speech features such as pitch, duration, and energy, enabling more interpretable and disentangled control over prosodic dimensions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]. Some models use hierarchical structures to condition on both coarse and fine-grained acoustic features, allowing for sentence-wise control of speaking pace and expressiveness \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]. Others propose latent variable models that learn interpretable prosody spaces, enabling the generation of diverse speaking styles while maintaining speech quality \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Additionally, models that incorporate pre-trained text embeddings, such as BERT, have been shown to improve prosody and pronunciation by leveraging semantic information from the input text \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\]. These advancements highlight the growing interest in making TTS systems more flexible and controllable, particularly through the use of interpretable latent spaces and disentangled representations \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10302)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02952)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06775)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]\[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03785)\].

### <a id='category-non-autoregressive-tts-models'></a>Non-Autoregressive TTS Models: 5 papers found.
Non-autoregressive text-to-speech (NAR-TTS) models have emerged as a promising direction for efficient and high-quality speech synthesis, enabling parallel generation of speech from text without the sequential dependencies of autoregressive models \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\]. Models like FastSpeech and Glow-TTS have been developed to address the challenges of one-to-many mapping and alignment in TTS, with FastSpeech relying on teacher-student distillation for duration and mel-spectrogram prediction, while Glow-TTS introduces a flow-based generative model that eliminates the need for external aligners \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11129)\]. FastSpeech 2 further improves upon FastSpeech by directly using ground-truth targets for training and incorporating more variation information such as pitch, energy, and duration as conditional inputs, leading to better voice quality and faster inference \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04558)\]. Similarly, ParaNet and Tacotron have been explored for parallel TTS, with Tacotron demonstrating end-to-end generation and faster inference compared to sample-level autoregressive methods \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10135)\]. Recent works like PortaSpeech combine lightweight VAEs with flow-based post-nets to achieve high-quality speech synthesis with efficient model size and memory usage, while also incorporating linguistic encoders for improved prosody modeling \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2109.15166)\]. These advancements highlight the growing focus on non-autoregressive approaches for TTS, emphasizing parallel generation, reduced training complexity, and enhanced control over prosody and speech quality.

### <a id='category-multi-speaker-tts-with-prosody-transfer'></a>Multi-Speaker TTS with Prosody Transfer: 6 papers found.
In the realm of multi-speaker text-to-speech (TTS) with prosody transfer, several approaches have been proposed to enhance the naturalness and expressiveness of synthesized speech. Models like CopyCat2 \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13443)\] introduce a two-stage training framework, where the first stage focuses on learning speaker-independent word-level prosody representations, and the second stage leverages contextual information from text to predict these representations, enabling multi-speaker TTS with contextually appropriate prosody. Similarly, a neural network-based system \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.04558)\] employs a speaker encoder trained on a large dataset of noisy speech to generate speaker embeddings, which are then used to condition a Tacotron 2-based sequence-to-sequence synthesis network and a WaveNet vocoder, allowing for the synthesis of natural speech from unseen speakers. Deep Voice 3 \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.07654)\] further advances the field by introducing a fully-convolutional attention-based system that matches state-of-the-art naturalness while offering faster training times and scalability to large datasets. Additionally, global style tokens (GSTs) \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09017)\] provide a method to model acoustic expressiveness without explicit labels, enabling style transfer and control over speech synthesis. These approaches collectively highlight the importance of speaker representation, prosody modeling, and efficient training strategies in achieving high-quality, multi-speaker TTS systems.
