## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to leverage user behavior across multiple domains to improve recommendation performance. Early approaches primarily relied on sequential models such as RNNs and Markov Chains to capture user preferences \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, these models often struggled with modeling complex sequential patterns and capturing cross-domain interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. To address these limitations, several studies have proposed graph-based methods, such as DA-GCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\], which utilize graph convolution networks to learn user-specific node representations and incorporate attention mechanisms to guide message passing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Additionally, mixed information flow networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\] have been introduced to consider both behavioral and knowledge transfer between domains, improving recommendation performance by modeling mixed information flow \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\].

Another line of research has focused on contrastive learning to address representation degeneration in sequential recommendation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]. The work by DuoRec introduces a contrastive regularization to reshape the distribution of sequence representations, improving item embedding quality and alleviating the representation degeneration problem \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]. Furthermore, the use of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a promising direction in recommendation systems \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. LLMs are capable of capturing rich semantic information from textual data, which can be leveraged to enhance recommendation quality \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. For instance, BERT4Rec \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] employs a bidirectional encoder to model user behavior sequences, allowing for more accurate predictions by considering both left and right contexts \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Similarly, Chat-Rec \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\] integrates LLMs into the recommendation process, enabling interactive and explainable recommendations through in-context learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\].

In the context of cross-domain recommendation, several studies have explored transfer learning approaches to bridge the gap between domains. CoNet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] introduces a deep transfer learning framework that enables dual knowledge transfer across domains by connecting hidden layers of neural networks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Similarly, DDTCDR \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] proposes a dual transfer mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains until the learning process stabilizes \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods aim to improve recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge from one domain to another \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. Additionally, the work by π-Net \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\] introduces a parallel information-sharing network to address the challenges of shared-account cross-domain sequential recommendations \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\].

Recent advances in self-supervised learning have also contributed to the field of sequential recommendation. S3-Rec \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07873)\] proposes a self-supervised learning approach that utilizes mutual information maximization to learn correlations between items, subsequences, and sequences
