## Related Works

Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has gained significant attention in recent years due to its ability to leverage user behavior across multiple domains to improve recommendation performance. Early approaches primarily relied on sequential models such as RNNs and Markov Chains to capture user preferences \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. However, these models often struggled with modeling complex sequential patterns and capturing cross-domain interactions \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. To address these limitations, several studies have proposed graph-based methods, such as DA-GCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\], which utilize graph convolution networks to learn user-specific node representations and incorporate attention mechanisms to guide message passing \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Additionally, mixed information flow networks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\] have been introduced to consider both behavioral and knowledge transfer between domains, improving recommendation performance by modeling mixed information flow \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\].

Another line of research has focused on contrastive learning to address representation degeneration in sequential recommendation \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]. The work by DuoRec introduces a contrastive regularization to reshape the distribution of sequence representations, improving item embedding quality and alleviating the representation degeneration problem \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.05730)\]. Furthermore, the use of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a promising direction in recommendation systems \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. LLMs are capable of capturing rich semantic information from textual data, which can be leveraged to enhance recommendation quality \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\]. For instance, BERT4Rec \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\] employs a bidirectional encoder to model user behavior sequences, allowing for more accurate predictions by considering both left and right contexts \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Similarly, Chat-Rec \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\] integrates LLMs into the recommendation process, enabling interactive and explainable recommendations through in-context learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\].

In the context of cross-domain recommendation, several studies have explored transfer learning approaches to bridge the gap between domains. CoNet \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\] introduces a deep transfer learning framework that enables dual knowledge transfer across domains by connecting hidden layers of neural networks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1804.06769)\]. Similarly, DDTCDR \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] proposes a dual transfer mechanism that iteratively transfers information between domains until the learning process stabilizes \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. These methods aim to improve recommendation performance by leveraging knowledge from one domain to another \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\]. ### <a id='category-cross-domain-recommendation-approaches'></a>Cross-Domain Recommendation Approaches: 10 papers found.
Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) has been an active research area, aiming to predict future interactions based on users' historical behaviors across multiple domains \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. Existing methods often face challenges such as the overlap dilemma, where models rely heavily on users with interactions across all domains, limiting practicality \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\], and transition complexity, which involves learning intricate patterns from mixed behavior sequences \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\]. To address these issues, recent approaches have explored graph-based models like DA-GCN \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\], which constructs domain-specific graphs and employs attention mechanisms to capture user preferences \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03300)\]. Similarly, C2DSR \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\] introduces a framework that leverages both intra- and inter-sequence item relationships to jointly learn single- and cross-domain user preferences, enhancing the model's ability to generalize across domains. Additionally, PSJNet \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02448)\] tackles shared-account settings by using a split-join architecture to manage user roles and cross-domain interactions. π-Net \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/fa990aee9a8f157b5d393f5f3eaa014e1e5c67aa)\] further refines this by introducing a shared account filter and cross-domain transfer unit to generate recommendations across domains. The alignment of item representations has also been a focus, with CA-CDSR \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12473)\] proposing a model-agnostic framework that aligns item representations adaptively to improve sequential modeling. Moreover, DDTCDR \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.05189)\] utilizes dual learning and latent orthogonal mapping to transfer information between domains iteratively, enhancing user preference modeling. Lastly, NATR \[[19](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ddbef126585ccb7356a0bca564c27915af596f67)\] addresses privacy concerns by sharing only item embeddings, allowing cross-domain recommendations without exposing user behavior data. These approaches collectively highlight the evolving strategies in CDSR, focusing on improving representation learning, handling shared accounts, and ensuring privacy while enhancing recommendation accuracy.

### <a id='category-large-language-models-in-recommendation-systems'></a>Large Language Models in Recommendation Systems: 4 papers found.
Large Language Models (LLMs) have been increasingly explored for enhancing recommendation systems, particularly in addressing challenges such as poor interactivity, explainability, and cross-domain generalization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of LLMs to improve recommendation quality by leveraging their strong representation and reasoning capabilities, enabling more effective transfer learning through techniques like fine-tuning and prompt tuning \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.00485)\]. For instance, the Chat-Rec framework utilizes LLMs to build conversational recommender systems by converting user profiles and historical interactions into prompts, thereby improving interactivity and explainability while enabling cross-domain recommendations \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.14524)\]. Similarly, the TALLRec framework proposes an efficient tuning approach to align LLMs with recommendation tasks, demonstrating significant improvements in recommendation performance even with limited datasets \[[26](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00447)\]. Additionally, existing surveys have categorized LLM-based recommendation systems into two main paradigms: Discriminative LLM for Recommendation (DLLM4Rec) and Generative LLM for Recommendation (GLLM4Rec), with the latter being systematically reviewed for the first time \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19860)\]. These works highlight the growing interest in integrating LLMs into recommendation systems to overcome traditional limitations and enhance user preference modeling through semantic understanding \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.02046)\].

Sequential recommendation models have been extensively studied to capture dynamic user preferences from historical interactions. Early approaches relied on unidirectional architectures like RNNs and CNNs, which limited the ability to model bidirectional context \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03891)\]. More recent methods, such as BERT4Rec, introduced bidirectional self-attention mechanisms to better capture sequential patterns by leveraging both left and right contexts \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06690)\]. Similarly, SASRec combined the strengths of Markov Chains and RNNs by using self-attention to identify relevant items from a user’s history, improving performance on both sparse and dense datasets \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09781)\]. The Caser model further advanced sequential modeling by using convolutional filters to extract local features from item sequences, offering a flexible and unified approach \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07426)\]. These models primarily focused on intra-sequence modeling, but overlooked global collaborative information across sequences, leading to suboptimal recommendations \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02816)\]. To address this, the APGL4SR framework incorporated adaptive and personalized global item graphs, enhancing user representations by capturing inter-sequence interactions \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02816)\]. Additionally, methods like UniSRec and VQ-Rec explored transferability by utilizing item descriptions and vector-quantized representations, respectively, to improve cross-domain performance \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2206.05941)\]\[[24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12316)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing efforts to enhance sequential recommendation systems through better modeling of user behavior, context, and cross-domain transferability.

Although some recent work has employed deep learning for recommendation, they primarily used it to model auxiliary information, such as textual descriptions of items and acoustic features of musics. When it comes to model the key factor in collaborative filtering --- the interaction between user and item features, they still resorted to matrix factorization and applied an inner product on the latent features of users and items. By replacing the inner product with a neural architecture that can learn an arbitrary function from data, we present a general framework named NCF, short for Neural network-based Collaborative Filtering. NCF is generic and can express and generalize matrix factorization under its framework. To supercharge NCF modelling with non-linearities, we propose to leverage a multi-layer perceptron to learn the user-item interaction function. Extensive experiments on two real-world datasets show significant improvements of our proposed NCF framework over the state-of-the-art methods. Empirical evidence shows that using deeper layers of neural networks offers better recommendation performance.»
