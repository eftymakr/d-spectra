## Related Works

Differential privacy (DP) has become the de facto standard for ensuring privacy in data analysis \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06569)\]. However, its implementation in distributed settings is vulnerable to malicious behaviors by servers, such as incorrect noise sampling or correlated noise generation \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1909.09630)\]. These issues have been extensively studied, with several works highlighting the limitations of existing DP protocols. For instance, \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\] shows that local differential privacy (LDP) protocols are highly vulnerable to adversarial manipulation, where a small fraction of users can obscure the true distribution of the data. Similarly, \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00010)\] demonstrates that noninteractive LDP protocols can be manipulated to a greater extent, especially when the privacy level is high or the domain size is large. These vulnerabilities underscore the need for mechanisms that ensure the faithful execution of DP protocols.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various countermeasures. One line of work focuses on improving the robustness of LDP protocols against data poisoning attacks, where attackers inject fake users to manipulate the results \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/85983d70db7bef99103c3833793f503c18445546)\]. For example, \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02046)\] introduces data poisoning attacks that can skew frequency estimates or identify heavy hitters, highlighting the need for more secure protocols. In response, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01524)\] proposes a verifiable randomization mechanism that allows the data collector to verify the completeness of the randomization process, thereby preventing output manipulation. This approach is further extended in \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0023582fde36430c7e3ae81611a14e558c8f4bae)\], where the authors describe secure versions of three state-of-the-art LDP protocols and show that they cause acceptable overheads.

Another approach involves leveraging cryptographic techniques to enhance the verifiability of DP mechanisms. For instance, \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08320)\] introduces the concept of interactive proofs for differential privacy, which requires the publishing entity to output a zero-knowledge proof that the output is both differentially private and reliable. This idea is closely related to the work in \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00133)\], where the authors explore the use of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) to ensure the correctness of DP computations. While ZKPs are sufficient for achieving DP under verifiability requirements, they are not necessary, as shown in \[[11](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3f70dc09f979b102c010d4bb500ceb09f18e3b17)\], which demonstrates that verifiable DP can be achieved without ZKPs.

### <a id='category-differential-privacy-(dp)-foundations'></a>Differential Privacy (DP) Foundations: 11 papers found.
The category "Differential Privacy (DP) Foundations" encompasses foundational research on differential privacy, including its theoretical underpinnings, practical implementations, and related privacy models. Early work established differential privacy as a rigorous mathematical framework for privacy-preserving data analysis, where noise is added to query results to ensure that the output does not reveal information about any individual \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00133)\]. This approach was later extended to handle more complex scenarios, such as learning problems, where algorithms must maintain privacy while training on sensitive data \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/0803.0924)\]. The local differential privacy (LDP) model, which assumes users randomize their data before sending it to a server, has been studied extensively, with research showing that its privacy cost can be lower than previously thought when reports are anonymized \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.12469)\]. Additionally, the shuffled model has emerged as a promising intermediate approach between the local and central models, offering improved accuracy over the local model while avoiding the need for a trusted server \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1911.06879)\]. Recent refinements, such as truncated concentrated differential privacy (tCDP), have further enhanced the framework by providing more accurate composition guarantees and enabling better statistical analyses \[[12](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2f0ab8040034e128175d424616b504d22cac485c)\]. These developments highlight the ongoing evolution of differential privacy, addressing both theoretical challenges and practical limitations in real-world applications \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.01394)\].

### <a id='category-discrete-noise-distribution-mechanisms'></a>Discrete Noise Distribution Mechanisms: 2 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «A key tool for building differentially private systems is adding Gaussian noise to the output of a function evaluated on a sensitive dataset. Unfortunately, using a continuous distribution presents several practical challenges. First and foremost, finite computers cannot exactly represent samples from continuous distributions, and previous work has demonstrated that seemingly innocuous numerical errors can entirely destroy privacy. Moreover, when the underlying data is itself discrete (e.g., population counts), adding continuous noise makes the result less interpretable. 
With these shortcomings in mind, we introduce and analyze the discrete Gaussian in the context of differential privacy. Specifically, we theoretically and experimentally show that adding discrete Gaussian noise provides essentially the same privacy and accuracy guarantees as the addition of continuous Gaussian noise. We also present an simple and efficient algorithm for exact sampling from this distribution. This demonstrates its applicability for privately answering counting queries, or more generally, low-sensitivity integer-valued queries.»

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Differential Privacy (DP) is often presented as a strong privacy-enhancing technology with broad applicability and advocated as a de facto standard for releasing aggregate statistics on sensitive data. However, in many embodiments, DP introduces a new attack surface: a malicious entity entrusted with releasing statistics could manipulate the results and use the randomness of DP as a convenient smokescreen to mask its nefariousness.
