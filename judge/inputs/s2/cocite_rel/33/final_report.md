## Related Works

Rehearsal-based methods have emerged as a prominent approach to address catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) by storing and training on a subset of past data alongside new data in current tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These methods aim to mitigate the degradation of performance on previously learned tasks, a phenomenon well-documented in neural networks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. While concurrent rehearsal, where past and new data are trained together, is widely used, recent work has questioned whether this approach is always optimal \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. Inspired by human learning, where sequentially revisiting tasks helps mitigate forgetting, researchers have explored whether sequential rehearsal can offer greater benefits for CL compared to standard concurrent rehearsal \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\].

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of different rehearsal strategies. For instance, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12631)\] proposed a method that leverages experience replay buffers to store past events, allowing models to learn new tasks while retaining knowledge of previous ones. This approach has been shown to reduce catastrophic forgetting in both Atari and DMLab domains \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. Similarly, \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\] introduced a framework called Deep Generative Replay, which uses a generative model to sample and interleave data from previous tasks with new tasks, enabling efficient sequential learning \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.04476)\]. These methods highlight the potential of replay-based strategies in mitigating forgetting, though they often require significant memory or computational resources \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1904.07734)\].

The idea of sequential rehearsal has also been explored in the context of reinforcement learning, where agents are exposed to tasks in a sequence. \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10486)\] demonstrated that using experience replay buffers with a mixture of on- and off-policy learning can substantially reduce catastrophic forgetting, even matching the performance of methods that require task identities \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742)\]. Additionally, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.10269)\] proposed a method that selectively stores experiences in a long-term memory, showing that distribution matching can prevent catastrophic forgetting and is consistently the best approach on all domains tested \[[13](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7bfb4ef17eabec1acd266958bdb08622eebfbb05)\].

More recently, \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\] introduced a hybrid approach that combines concurrent and sequential rehearsal, training similar tasks concurrently and revisiting dissimilar tasks sequentially. This method was shown to outperform standard concurrent rehearsal in deep neural networks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.10635)\]. The theoretical analysis of rehearsal-based CL in overparameterized linear models further supports the effectiveness of sequential rehearsal when tasks are less similar \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12588)\]. These findings suggest that the choice of rehearsal strategy significantly impacts the performance of CL systems, and that sequential rehearsal may offer advantages in certain scenarios \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06562)\].

The category "Catastrophic Forgetting Mitigation Techniques" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at enabling neural networks to learn sequentially without losing previously acquired knowledge. These techniques are crucial for advancing continual learning, as traditional models suffer from catastrophic forgetting when exposed to new tasks. One approach involves protecting important weights for previous tasks, inspired by synaptic consolidation in neuroscience, which allows models to maintain expertise on old tasks while learning new ones \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. Another method introduces a task-based hard attention mechanism, which preserves past task information without interfering with current learning, significantly reducing forgetting rates by up to 80% \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. Additionally, modular architectures have been proposed, where modules represent atomic skills that can be reused or instantiated to solve new tasks, enabling efficient learning on long streams of tasks \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12631)\]. Learning without Forgetting is another technique that enables training on new tasks without access to previous task data, preserving original capabilities by leveraging only new task data \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09282)\]. Neural pruning methods, such as CLNP, have also been introduced, allowing models to maintain performance while sparsifying networks and enabling graceful forgetting \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1903.04476)\]. Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) is a model that alleviates forgetting and allows knowledge transfer to previous tasks, demonstrating strong performance on standard benchmarks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.08840)\]. OML, an objective that minimizes catastrophic interference, enables representations that are robust to forgetting and accelerate future learning \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12588)\]. Progressive networks expansion and deep model compression techniques also contribute to mitigating forgetting by integrating critical weights selection and model compaction \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06562)\]. ### <a id='category-experience-replay-and-memory-buffers'></a>Experience Replay and Memory Buffers: 7 papers found.
Experience Replay and Memory Buffers: The use of experience replay buffers has emerged as a critical strategy for mitigating catastrophic forgetting in continual learning (CL) \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)\]. These buffers store past experiences, allowing models to revisit them during training, thereby preserving knowledge from previous tasks \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.11682)\]. Various methods have been proposed to enhance the effectiveness of replay, including the use of on- and off-policy learning, behavioral cloning, and selective sampling of experiences based on their importance \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08690)\]. For instance, some approaches prioritize experiences that are most affected by parameter updates, leading to improved performance and reduced forgetting \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01423)\]. Additionally, methods such as reservoir sampling have shown promise in maintaining a balanced representation of past data, especially when memory size is limited \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.12631)\]. The integration of experience replay with other techniques, such as meta-learning, has also been explored to further enhance the ability of models to generalize across tasks while minimizing interference \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/90e04f3ae23ca7df5f59b11453341e3db943b6f4)\]. Overall, the design and implementation of memory buffers remain a central focus in the development of robust continual learning systems.
