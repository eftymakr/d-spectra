## Related Works

Calibration of deep learning models has been a critical area of research, particularly in ensuring that predicted probabilities reflect the true likelihood of correct predictions. Early studies highlighted that modern neural networks, unlike older models, are poorly calibrated, with confidence scores often not aligning with actual accuracy \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. This issue is exacerbated by factors such as network depth, width, and training procedures \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\]. To address this, temperature scaling—a simple post-processing method—has been shown to effectively improve calibration across various architectures and datasets \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)\].

Recent work has explored more sophisticated calibration techniques, such as Dirichlet calibration, which extends the concept of beta calibration to multiclass settings \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656)\]. This method is particularly useful for models where the output probabilities need to be adjusted to better reflect true confidence. Similarly, Bayesian Binning into Quantiles (BBQ) has been proposed as a non-parametric calibration method that can be integrated with existing classification algorithms \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/8d115c3b2ee80e0754360a154a9369bc1658b607)\]. These approaches, however, often rely on fixed or limited functional forms, which may not capture the complex calibration landscapes of deep networks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2003.06820)\].

To overcome these limitations, researchers have proposed learning-based calibration methods that allow for more flexible and expressive calibration functions. For instance, the use of deep ensembles has been shown to produce well-calibrated uncertainty estimates, comparable to Bayesian methods, while being more computationally efficient \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)\]. Additionally, focal loss has been introduced as a training objective that inherently promotes better calibration, often without compromising accuracy \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.09437)\].

Label smoothing, a technique that softens the target distribution during training, has also been shown to improve model calibration by reducing overconfidence \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02629)\]. This is particularly beneficial in tasks where predictive uncertainty is crucial, such as in medical or safety-critical applications. Furthermore, the use of mixup training, which generates augmented samples by combining pairs of images and labels, has been found to significantly improve calibration by promoting more robust feature representations \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.11001)\].

In the context of ranking models, the importance of calibration has been increasingly recognized. Traditional ranking methods often focus on relative ordering while neglecting the absolute values of predictions, which can be critical for downstream tasks \[[23](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/d388760ce74792bb764ccf77ea8620ca77406e56)\]. To address this, several works have proposed calibration methods tailored for ranking models, such as scale calibration, which ensures that the predicted scores are consistent with the relative ordering while also being interpretable \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/7f6bb146b9c1504ec77100e640d624bdc204f0ac)\]. These methods typically involve modifying the loss function during training to enforce scale consistency, thereby improving both ranking quality and score calibration.

	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Conformal prediction uses past experience to determine precise levels of confidence in new predictions. Given an error probability e, together with a method that makes a prediction ŷ of a label y, it produces a set of labels, typically containing ŷ, that also contains y with probability 1  e. Conformal prediction can be applied to any method for producing ŷ: a nearest-neighbor method, a support-vector machine, ridge regression, etc. 
	Conformal prediction is designed for an on-line setting in which labels are predicted successively, each one being revealed before the next is predicted. The most novel and valuable feature of conformal prediction is that if the successive examples are sampled independently from the same distribution, then the successive predictions will be right 1  e of the time, even though they are based on an accumulating data set rather than on independent data sets. 
	In addition to the model under which successive examples are sampled independently, other on-line compression models can also use conformal prediction. The widely used Gaussian linear model is one of these. 
	This tutorial presents a self-contained account of the theory of conformal prediction and works through several numerical examples. A more comprehensive treatment of the topic is provided in Algorithmic Learning in a Random World, by Vladimir Vovk, Alex Gammerman, and Glenn Shafer (Springer, 2005).»
