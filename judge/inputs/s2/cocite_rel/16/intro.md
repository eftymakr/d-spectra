## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection has emerged as a critical research direction in computer vision, enabling models to detect objects based on user-specified class vocabularies in natural language. This capability is particularly valuable in scenarios where predefined class lists are insufficient or impractical \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. However, the performance of these models can be significantly affected by the quality and relevance of the user-defined vocabulary \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. Prior work has explored various approaches to improve the effectiveness of open-vocabulary detection, including leveraging vision-language pre-training, weakly supervised learning, and pseudo-labeling techniques \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\].

Several studies have focused on using vision-language models to bridge the gap between visual and textual modalities. For instance, CLIP and its variants have demonstrated strong zero-shot transfer capabilities by aligning image and text representations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14940)\]. However, these models often struggle with fine-grained alignment between image regions and textual descriptions, limiting their effectiveness in object detection tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. To address this, RegionCLIP was introduced, extending CLIP to learn region-level visual representations, enabling more accurate alignment between image regions and textual concepts \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\]. Similarly, MDETR and ViLD have been proposed to condition object detection on text queries, leveraging pre-trained vision-language models to improve detection accuracy \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12698)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17270)\].

Weakly supervised and zero-shot detection methods have also been extensively explored to reduce the reliance on expensive bounding box annotations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\]. For example, Detic leverages image-level supervision to expand the vocabulary of detectors to tens of thousands of concepts, significantly improving performance on open-vocabulary benchmarks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12763)\]. Another approach, Cap2Det, utilizes unstructured textual descriptions to train detectors, demonstrating that captions can provide valuable supervision for object localization and classification \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06165)\]. These methods highlight the potential of using weak or unstructured supervision to enhance detection capabilities, although they often face challenges in accurately localizing objects \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\].

Recent advances in open-vocabulary detection have also focused on improving the efficiency and scalability of detection frameworks. For example, YOLO-World introduces a real-time open-vocabulary detection approach by integrating vision-language modeling with the YOLO architecture, achieving high accuracy and speed on challenging benchmarks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03195)\]. Similarly, Grounding DINO combines Transformer-based detection with grounded pre-training, enabling the detection of arbitrary objects using human inputs such as category names or referring expressions \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c5ff974a69fd0c760b4855b819e61e89f31cfffe)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of combining vision-language models with efficient detection architectures to achieve state-of-the-art performance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10164)\].
