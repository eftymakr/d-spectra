## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection has emerged as a critical research direction in computer vision, enabling models to detect objects based on user-specified class vocabularies in natural language. This capability is particularly valuable in scenarios where predefined class lists are insufficient or impractical \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. However, the performance of these models can be significantly affected by the quality and relevance of the user-defined vocabulary \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. Prior work has explored various approaches to improve the effectiveness of open-vocabulary detection, including leveraging vision-language pre-training, weakly supervised learning, and pseudo-labeling techniques \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]\[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\].

Several studies have focused on using vision-language models to bridge the gap between visual and textual modalities. For instance, CLIP and its variants have demonstrated strong zero-shot transfer capabilities by aligning image and text representations \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14940)\]. However, these models often struggle with fine-grained alignment between image regions and textual descriptions, limiting their effectiveness in object detection tasks \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. To address this, RegionCLIP was introduced, extending CLIP to learn region-level visual representations, enabling more accurate alignment between image regions and textual concepts \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06230)\]. Similarly, MDETR and ViLD have been proposed to condition object detection on text queries, leveraging pre-trained vision-language models to improve detection accuracy \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.12698)\]\[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17270)\].

Weakly supervised and zero-shot detection methods have also been extensively explored to reduce the reliance on expensive bounding box annotations \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\]. For example, Detic leverages image-level supervision to expand the vocabulary of detectors to tens of thousands of concepts, significantly improving performance on open-vocabulary benchmarks \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.12763)\]. Another approach, Cap2Det, utilizes unstructured textual descriptions to train detectors, demonstrating that captions can provide valuable supervision for object localization and classification \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06165)\]. These methods highlight the potential of using weak or unstructured supervision to enhance detection capabilities, although they often face challenges in accurately localizing objects \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312)\].

Recent advances in open-vocabulary detection have also focused on improving the efficiency and scalability of detection frameworks. For example, YOLO-World introduces a real-time open-vocabulary detection approach by integrating vision-language modeling with the YOLO architecture, achieving high accuracy and speed on challenging benchmarks \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03195)\]. Similarly, Grounding DINO combines Transformer-based detection with grounded pre-training, enabling the detection of arbitrary objects using human inputs such as category names or referring expressions \[[15](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/c5ff974a69fd0c760b4855b819e61e89f31cfffe)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of combining vision-language models with efficient detection architectures to achieve state-of-the-art performance \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10164)\].

### <a id='category-zero-shot-and-weakly-supervised-detection-methods'></a>Zero-shot and weakly supervised detection methods: 8 papers found.
The category of zero-shot and weakly supervised detection methods focuses on addressing the limitations of traditional supervised object detection by reducing reliance on extensive bounding box annotations. These methods aim to improve scalability and practicality by leveraging alternative forms of supervision, such as image-level labels, textual descriptions, or pre-trained vision-language models \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.10678)\]. For instance, open-vocabulary object detection extends this idea by allowing models to detect objects beyond the training set, using language embeddings and pseudo-labeling techniques to generalize to unseen categories \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.08954)\]. However, existing approaches often face challenges in aligning semantic information with precise localization, as CLIP-based models lack fine-grained object localization, while image-level supervision struggles with accurate region segmentation \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03482)\]. To address these issues, recent work has focused on object-centric alignment strategies, integrating vision-language models with pseudo-labeling processes to enhance detection accuracy and expand vocabulary size \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]. Additionally, weakly supervised detection methods have explored using captions or image-level annotations to train detectors without explicit bounding box supervision, though they often suffer from suboptimal performance compared to fully supervised models \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.10164)\]. These efforts highlight the ongoing pursuit of more efficient and scalable detection frameworks that balance performance with reduced annotation costs \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1511.02853)\].

### <a id='category-open-vocabulary-object-detection-frameworks'></a>Open-vocabulary object detection frameworks: 10 papers found.
Open-vocabulary object detection has seen significant advancements with methods like ViLD, which leverages Vision and Language knowledge distillation to align region embeddings with text and image embeddings from a pretrained model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.13921)\]. Similarly, Detic expands the vocabulary of detectors by training classifiers on image classification data, enabling detection of tens of thousands of concepts without complex assignment schemes \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605)\]. Detection Prompt (DetPro) introduces continuous prompt representations for open-vocabulary detection, improving performance on novel classes by incorporating background interpretation and context grading \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14940)\]. YOLO-World enhances YOLO with open-vocabulary capabilities through vision-language modeling, achieving high efficiency and accuracy on challenging datasets like LVIS \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.17270)\]. X-DETR proposes an architecture combining object detection, language encoding, and vision-language alignment, demonstrating effectiveness in instance-wise tasks \[[22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05626)\]. Additionally, methods like PB-OVD automatically generate pseudo bounding-box annotations from image-caption pairs, enabling training on diverse objects without manual annotations \[[25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.09452)\]. These approaches collectively address the challenge of detecting novel classes without extensive training data, focusing on efficient and scalable solutions for open-vocabulary detection.

### <a id='category-vision-language-pre-training-models'></a>Vision-language pre-training models: 5 papers found.
Vision-language pre-training models have emerged as a powerful approach to bridge the gap between visual and textual data, enabling zero-shot and transfer learning across a wide range of tasks. These models are typically trained on large-scale image-text pairs, where the goal is to learn cross-modal representations that capture semantic alignments between visual content and language \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]. For instance, CLIP leverages a simple pre-training task of matching captions to images, achieving state-of-the-art results on tasks such as image classification and object detection without requiring explicit class labels \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020)\]. Similarly, RegionCLIP extends this idea by focusing on fine-grained alignment at the region level, significantly improving performance on object detection tasks by learning region-level visual representations \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09106)\]. Other approaches, such as Oscar, utilize object tags detected in images as anchor points to align image and text features more effectively \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.06165)\]. Additionally, methods like Pixel-BERT aim to align image pixels directly with text semantics, offering a more thorough connection between visual and language data without relying on region-based features \[[29](http://arxiv.org/abs/2004.00849)\]. These models often benefit from large, noisy datasets, such as those derived from alt-text pairs, which can be leveraged to train robust representations even with minimal filtering \[[28](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05918)\]. Overall, vision-language pre-training models demonstrate strong transferability and zero-shot capabilities, making them a key area of research in cross-modal learning.
