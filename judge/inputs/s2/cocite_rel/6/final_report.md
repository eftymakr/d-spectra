## Related Works

Narrative summarization, which involves condensing long-form texts such as books, movies, and TV scripts, has been a challenging task for existing large language models (LLMs) due to the complexity of capturing intricate plotlines, character interactions, and thematic coherence \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12356)\]. Prior approaches have primarily relied on fine-tuning models on specific datasets, which limits their generalizability and adaptability to diverse narrative structures \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12356)\]. Additionally, many models suffer from issues such as repetition, inaccuracy, and lack of coherence in generated summaries \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\]. 

Several studies have explored abstractive summarization techniques using sequence-to-sequence models, attention mechanisms, and neural networks. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.04368)\] introduced a pointer-generator network that allows models to copy words from the source text while also generating novel content, thereby reducing repetition and improving factual accuracy. Similarly, \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.00685)\] proposed a local attention-based model for abstractive sentence summarization, which generates each word of the summary conditioned on the input sentence. While these models show promise, they often require extensive fine-tuning and may not generalize well to long-form narratives \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.12356)\].

Recent work has also focused on improving the quality of summaries through better modeling of key elements such as keywords, sentence structure, and hierarchical relationships. \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1602.06023)\] proposed attentional encoder-decoder recurrent neural networks that achieve state-of-the-art performance on summarization tasks by capturing key-words and sentence-to-word structures. \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.13164)\] introduced a hierarchical Transformer model for multi-document summarization, which encodes documents in a hierarchical manner and uses an attention mechanism to capture cross-document relationships. This approach has shown substantial improvements over strong baselines, particularly in handling complex, multi-document scenarios \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.13164)\].

Another line of research has focused on the evaluation of summarization models. \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12626)\] conducted a comprehensive evaluation of 14 automatic evaluation metrics and found that many of them are weakly correlated with human judgment. They also emphasized the importance of using diverse human annotations to better align evaluation protocols with real-world performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12626)\].
