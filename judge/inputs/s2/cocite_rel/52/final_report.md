## Related Works

Dense retrieval has emerged as a powerful paradigm for information retrieval, leveraging pre-trained language models (PLMs) to encode text into dense vector representations that can be compared using simple similarity operations like inner product \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Early work focused on fine-tuning PLMs such as BERT to adapt them for retrieval tasks, often using contrastive learning to improve performance \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)\]. However, recent studies have questioned the extent to which fine-tuning contributes to retrieval performance, arguing that much of the retrieval knowledge is acquired during pre-training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This idea was initially explored in the context of BERT-based encoders using the Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) framework \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\], but its generalizability across different architectures and datasets remains an open question.

Several works have investigated the role of pre-training versus fine-tuning in dense retrievers. For example, \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\] highlights the importance of pre-training in capturing semantic knowledge, while \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\] shows that fine-tuning can adjust neuron activations without reorganizing the underlying knowledge. This suggests that pre-training is the primary source of retrieval knowledge, and fine-tuning serves more as a refinement step. However, this pattern does not hold universally. For instance, models like Contriever, which use mean pooling, and decoder-based models like LLaMA, exhibit different behavior where fine-tuning plays a more significant role \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\].

The effectiveness of dense retrievers also depends on the choice of representation strategies. While the CLS token has been widely used as a fixed representation for the entire sequence, alternative approaches such as mean pooling or attention-based aggregation have shown promise \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. These methods can lead to more robust and context-aware representations, especially when combined with advanced pre-training objectives like contrastive learning \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. Additionally, the choice of backbone architecture—whether encoder-only (e.g., BERT) or decoder-only (e.g., LLaMA)—can significantly impact retrieval performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\].

To address the challenges of training dense retrievers, various techniques have been proposed. For example, \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\] introduces coCondenser, which adds an unsupervised corpus-level contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, reducing the need for extensive data engineering. Similarly, \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10641)\] proposes a novel contrastive span prediction task to pre-train the encoder alone, avoiding the bypass effect of the decoder. These methods highlight the importance of carefully designed pre-training objectives in achieving high-quality dense representations.

### <a id='category-unsupervised-pre-training-objectives'></a>Unsupervised Pre-training Objectives: 9 papers found.
The category "Unsupervised Pre-training Objectives" encompasses a range of approaches aimed at improving language models through pre-training without the need for labeled data. These methods focus on leveraging large-scale unlabeled text to capture general linguistic patterns, world knowledge, and semantic relationships, which can then be fine-tuned for specific tasks. For instance, BERT introduces a bidirectional training approach that allows models to understand context from both directions, leading to significant improvements in tasks like question answering and natural language inference \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08253)\]. Similarly, models like REALM incorporate a latent knowledge retriever to access external documents during pre-training, enhancing the model's ability to answer open-domain questions \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. The effectiveness of such pre-training objectives is further supported by studies showing that embedding-based retrieval models, when trained with carefully designed tasks like the Inverse Cloze Task, outperform traditional IR methods like BM-25 \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.03932)\]. Additionally, the quality of learned embeddings scales with both model size and training data, suggesting that larger models trained on more data can achieve better performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03659)\]. Moreover, models like XLNet and CLEAR explore alternative pre-training strategies, such as autoregressive formulations and contrastive learning, to capture richer contextual and semantic information \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08237)\]\[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15466)\]. These efforts collectively highlight the importance of pre-training objectives in shaping the capabilities of language models, particularly in tasks requiring deep understanding and retrieval of information.

### <a id='category-contrastive-learning-in-dense-retrieval'></a>Contrastive Learning in Dense Retrieval: 10 papers found.
Contrastive learning has emerged as a critical technique in dense retrieval, enabling models to learn effective text representations without reliance on extensive labeled data \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where training data is limited, as it allows for the development of unsupervised dense retrievers that can achieve strong performance \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. For instance, coCondenser leverages a contrastive loss to warm up the passage embedding space, eliminating the need for heavy data engineering and large batch training \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]. Similarly, ANCE introduces a training mechanism that constructs negatives from an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index, thereby improving the alignment between training and testing data distributions \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]. The effectiveness of contrastive learning is further supported by models like Spider, which uses recurring span retrieval to create pseudo examples for contrastive learning, achieving competitive results with minimal labeled data \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]. Additionally, contrastive learning has been shown to enable cross-lingual retrieval, allowing models to retrieve documents in one language based on queries in another \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\]. These advancements highlight the versatility and power of contrastive learning in dense retrieval, addressing key limitations such as data scarcity and the need for complex fine-tuning pipelines \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.05540)\]\[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00808)\]\[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1907.11692)\]\[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08909)\].

### <a id='category-training-techniques-for-dense-retrievers'></a>Training Techniques for Dense Retrievers: 5 papers found.
Training techniques for dense retrievers have seen significant advancements, focusing on improving the quality of text embeddings and the efficiency of training processes. Early approaches relied on autoencoder-based language models, which were found to potentially suffer from decoder shortcuts due to low reconstruction loss \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. To mitigate this, a self-learning method was introduced, using a weak decoder to enhance encoder performance \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5be217c678916543884c354654263f27c0a6bd9f)\]. Similarly, challenges in training dual-encoder architectures, such as training-inference discrepancy and limited data, led to the development of RocketQA, which employs cross-batch negatives, denoised hard negatives, and data augmentation to improve retrieval performance \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08191)\]. Additionally, resource efficiency has become a critical concern, with methods like TAS-Balanced introducing topic-aware query sampling and dual-teacher supervision to train lightweight models on consumer-grade GPUs \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06967)\]. Another approach, Inverse Cloze Task pre-training, was proposed to jointly learn retrievers and readers without relying on external IR systems, achieving significant improvements in open-domain QA \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00300)\]. Theoretical investigations into training strategies have also highlighted the importance of dynamic sampling methods, such as STAR and ADORE, which enhance stability and ranking performance by introducing randomness and optimizing directly for retrieval tasks \[[21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08051)\]. These techniques collectively address key challenges in dense retrieval, including representation quality, training efficiency, and generalization across diverse datasets.
