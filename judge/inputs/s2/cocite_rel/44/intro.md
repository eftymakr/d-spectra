## Related Works

Representation learning on graphs has seen significant advancements, particularly in the context of recommender systems and collaborative filtering. Early approaches such as matrix factorization \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\] and deep learning-based methods \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\] have been widely used to learn user and item embeddings. However, these methods often fail to capture the complex interactions between users and items, especially in large-scale graphs \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Recent work has focused on leveraging graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to model these interactions more effectively \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. For instance, LightGCN simplifies the design of GCNs by removing unnecessary components such as feature transformation and nonlinear activation, leading to improved performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This approach allows for the modeling of high-order connectivity in user-item graphs, enhancing the collaborative filtering effect \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Graph-based collaborative filtering has also been extended to incorporate additional information such as side information and knowledge graphs. For example, Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) explicitly models high-order relations in knowledge graphs to improve recommendation accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. Additionally, methods like BPR (Bayesian Personalized Ranking) have been developed to optimize models for personalized ranking tasks, directly addressing the need for ranking optimization in implicit feedback scenarios \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\]. These methods often rely on stochastic gradient descent with bootstrap sampling to optimize the learning objective \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\].

In the context of graph representation learning, approaches such as node2vec and DeepWalk have been proposed to learn continuous feature representations for nodes in networks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]. These methods use biased random walks to explore diverse neighborhoods and maximize the likelihood of preserving network neighborhoods of nodes \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. This has led to significant improvements in multi-label classification and link prediction tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. Furthermore, recent work on geometric matrix completion with recurrent multi-graph neural networks has shown promising results in capturing the local stationarity structures of user/item graphs \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\].

The challenge of information loss during representation binarization has also been addressed in prior work. For instance, variational autoencoders (VAEs) have been extended to collaborative filtering for implicit feedback, enabling more flexible modeling of user-item interactions \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\]. These methods use Bayesian inference for parameter estimation and introduce a different regularization parameter to achieve competitive performance \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\].
