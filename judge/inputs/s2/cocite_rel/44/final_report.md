## Related Works

Representation learning on graphs has seen significant advancements, particularly in the context of recommender systems and collaborative filtering. Early approaches such as matrix factorization \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\] and deep learning-based methods \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\] have been widely used to learn user and item embeddings. However, these methods often fail to capture the complex interactions between users and items, especially in large-scale graphs \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. Recent work has focused on leveraging graph convolutional networks (GCNs) to model these interactions more effectively \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\]. For instance, LightGCN simplifies the design of GCNs by removing unnecessary components such as feature transformation and nonlinear activation, leading to improved performance \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Similarly, Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF) integrates user-item interactions into the embedding process by propagating embeddings on the bipartite graph structure \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. This approach allows for the modeling of high-order connectivity in user-item graphs, enhancing the collaborative filtering effect \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\].

Graph-based collaborative filtering has also been extended to incorporate additional information such as side information and knowledge graphs. For example, Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT) explicitly models high-order relations in knowledge graphs to improve recommendation accuracy \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. Additionally, methods like BPR (Bayesian Personalized Ranking) have been developed to optimize models for personalized ranking tasks, directly addressing the need for ranking optimization in implicit feedback scenarios \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\]. These methods often rely on stochastic gradient descent with bootstrap sampling to optimize the learning objective \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\].

In the context of graph representation learning, approaches such as node2vec and DeepWalk have been proposed to learn continuous feature representations for nodes in networks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]\[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]. This has led to significant improvements in multi-label classification and link prediction tasks \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. Furthermore, recent work on geometric matrix completion with recurrent multi-graph neural networks has shown promising results in capturing the local stationarity structures of user/item graphs \[[17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06803)\].

### <a id='category-graph-neural-networks'></a>Graph Neural Networks: 6 papers found.
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful framework for learning node embeddings in graph-structured data, with significant applications in recommendation systems and node classification tasks \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Unlike traditional approaches that are transductive and limited to seen nodes \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\], GNNs offer an inductive framework that can generalize to unseen nodes by leveraging local neighborhood information \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. This capability is crucial for dynamic graphs, such as evolving information networks or protein-protein interaction graphs, where new nodes are continuously added \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\]. Recent advancements have focused on scalability and efficiency, particularly for large-scale recommendation tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. For instance, Pinterest's large-scale deep recommendation engine employs a data-efficient Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) that combines efficient random walks with graph convolutions to generate embeddings for billions of nodes \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\]. Additionally, LightGCN simplifies GCN designs by focusing on neighborhood aggregation, achieving substantial improvements in recommendation performance while maintaining a linear complexity \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. Furthermore, knowledge graph-based approaches, such as Knowledge Graph Attention Network (KGAT), have been developed to model high-order relations between items and users, enhancing recommendation accuracy by incorporating side information and attention mechanisms \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07854)\]. These developments highlight the growing importance of GNNs in addressing the challenges of scalability, generalization, and performance in real-world recommendation systems.

### <a id='category-collaborative-filtering-methods'></a>Collaborative Filtering Methods: 7 papers found.
The Related Works section for the paper's category on Collaborative Filtering Methods highlights several key approaches and advancements in the field. Early methods such as matrix factorization \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] and adaptive k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1205.2618)\] were foundational in modeling user-item interactions, but they lacked the ability to directly optimize for personalized ranking. More recent work has introduced neural network-based frameworks like NCF \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\], which replaces traditional inner products with deep architectures to better model user-item interactions. Additionally, probabilistic models such as VAEs \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05814)\] have been extended to collaborative filtering, enabling non-linear modeling and improved performance. Graph-based methods, including GCNs \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\], have also gained traction by leveraging the bipartite user-item interaction graph to capture higher-order collaborative signals. However, challenges such as over-smoothing and training difficulties persist. To address these, methods like NGCF \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\] and Multi-GCCF \[[18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.00267)\] have been proposed, integrating graph convolution and multiple graphs to enhance embedding learning. Furthermore, incorporating visual signals \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1510.01784)\] has shown promise in improving recommendation accuracy by leveraging item appearance data. These developments collectively emphasize the ongoing evolution of collaborative filtering techniques, driven by the need for more expressive and efficient models.

### <a id='category-graph-representation-learning'></a>Graph Representation Learning: 4 papers found.
Graph representation learning has seen significant advancements, with various approaches aimed at capturing the structural and relational information within networks. Traditional methods often relied on handcrafted features, such as degree statistics, but recent work has shifted toward automated feature learning techniques. For instance, node2vec introduces a flexible framework for learning continuous node representations by using biased random walks to explore diverse neighborhoods, enabling richer and more expressive representations \[[15](http://arxiv.org/abs/1607.00653)\]. Similarly, DeepWalk extends language modeling techniques to graphs by treating random walks as sentences, allowing the learning of latent representations that encode social relations \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/1403.6652)\]. These methods highlight the importance of leveraging both local and global network structures. Additionally, BiRank presents a novel approach for ranking vertices in bipartite graphs by optimizing a regularization function guided by query vectors, demonstrating the potential of integrating prior information with graph structure \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04396)\]. These contributions collectively underscore the evolving landscape of graph representation learning, emphasizing the need for methods that can adapt to diverse network structures and learning tasks.

### <a id='category-information-loss-mitigation'></a>Information Loss Mitigation: 1 papers found.
Context: 
	Document 1: [Snippet]: «Training Deep Neural Networks is complicated by the fact that the distribution of each layer's inputs changes during training, as the parameters of the previous layers change. This slows down the training by requiring lower learning rates and careful parameter initialization, and makes it notoriously hard to train models with saturating nonlinearities. We refer to this phenomenon as internal covariate shift, and address the problem by normalizing layer inputs. Our method draws its strength from making normalization a part of the model architecture and performing the normalization for each training mini-batch.
