## Related Works

Taxation policy design has been extensively studied, with a focus on both theoretical models and empirical observations. Traditional approaches to tax policy, such as the U.S. federal income tax, aim to reduce economic inequality but often lack adaptability to heterogeneous taxpayer behavior \[[1](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/0fba9c1bee4a5bda0d5dbf01f97ef5a8e212833e)\]. The Saez Optimal Taxation model \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10144)\] provides a dynamic framework for tax rate adjustment, yet it fails to account for irrational decision-making and behavioral heterogeneity among taxpayers. These limitations highlight the need for more sophisticated models that integrate behavioral insights with adaptive policy mechanisms.

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has emerged as a powerful tool for simulating complex economic behaviors, including tax evasion. Several studies have explored the dynamics of tax evasion using models inspired by statistical mechanics, such as the Ising model \[[3](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/12dba28ded03c013923430994d1aac347c647c8d)\]. These models incorporate social interactions and enforcement mechanisms to simulate how tax compliance may evolve over time. For instance, the Ising model has been used to demonstrate that tax evasion can be effectively controlled through punishment-based enforcement \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/0805.0998)\]. Similarly, the majority-vote model has been applied to analyze tax evasion dynamics on various network structures, showing robustness across different topologies \[[5](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/62ca7c681a393f15fe1ac3934631cae72e307b08)\].

Social interactions play a critical role in shaping taxpayer behavior. Research has shown that tax compliance can be influenced by social conformity, fairness, and sorting effects \[[6](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/3b38a079d28ee6945549ba16119bb95978fa1cc1)\]. Experimental studies have confirmed that individuals' decisions are often influenced by the behavior of others in their social group, suggesting that tax policies should consider these social dynamics \[[7](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/e3d048b7b4d82a7d0908dd72710dadc7c71b0953)\]. This aligns with findings from agent-based models that incorporate social networks to simulate tax compliance \[[8](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/4900a633d3f9bc2e5a9adda2afb2f04f837d5f39)\].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly large language models (LLMs), have opened new possibilities for policy design. While LLMs are primarily used for tasks requiring natural language understanding, their ability to process vast amounts of information has led to their application in decision-making systems \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/0801.2980)\]. However, fine-tuning LLMs for specific tasks is resource-intensive and may reduce their generalization capabilities \[[10](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a065b1629ff85e2a0db928bd8ce115b2f5e2a80f)\]. To address these challenges, new methodologies such as Experiential Learning (ExpeL) agents have been proposed, which enable models to learn from past experiences without requiring parametric updates \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/0806.0344)\].

In the context of tax policy, the integration of LLMs with ABM offers a promising approach to simulate heterogeneous taxpayer behavior and optimize tax rates dynamically. This aligns with the broader goal of designing adaptive tax policies that balance equity and productivity \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/0910.1964)\].
