## Related Works

Graph-based collaborative filtering has seen significant advancements in recent years, with graph convolutional networks (GCNs) becoming a cornerstone in recommendation systems \[[1](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.08108)\]. Early works such as Neural Collaborative Filtering (NCF) introduced deep learning techniques to model user-item interactions, replacing traditional inner products with neural architectures to capture non-linearities \[[2](http://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05031)\]. However, these methods often rely on matrix factorization and do not explicitly model the bipartite graph structure of user-item interactions \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1806.01973)\].

The integration of graph structures into collaborative filtering has been further explored with the introduction of Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering (NGCF), which propagates embeddings on the user-item graph to capture high-order connectivity \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.02126)\]. This approach explicitly injects collaborative signals into the embedding process, leading to improved performance over existing methods \[[5](http://arxiv.org/abs/1609.02907)\]. However, NGCF and similar models still face challenges in scalability and training efficiency, especially when dealing with large-scale graphs \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.09375)\].

LightGCN was proposed to simplify GCN-based models by removing unnecessary components such as feature transformations and nonlinear activations, resulting in a more efficient and effective model \[[7](http://arxiv.org/abs/1808.10523)\]. This simplification led to significant performance improvements, with LightGCN outperforming NGCF by about 16% on benchmark datasets \[[8](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01764)\]. Despite these improvements, the issue of over-smoothing in deep graph convolutional networks remains a challenge, as deeper layers can lose meaningful information \[[9](http://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02216)\].

To address over-smoothing, the paper "Revisiting Graph based Collaborative Filtering" proposed a linear residual graph convolutional network (LR-GCCF), which removes non-linearities and introduces a residual structure to alleviate the over-smoothing problem \[[10](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07243)\]. This approach is particularly effective for large-scale user-item graphs, where non-linear activation functions can hinder training \[[11](http://arxiv.org/abs/1902.07153)\].

The field has also seen the development of more sophisticated models such as Disentangled Graph Collaborative Filtering (DGCF), which focuses on disentangling user intents from interactions to improve representation quality \[[12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2001.10167)\]. Additionally, LightGCL introduced a graph contrastive learning paradigm that improves robustness against data sparsity and popularity bias by leveraging singular value decomposition for contrastive augmentation \[[13](http://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15114)\].

In the broader context of graph-based learning, Graph Attention Networks (GATs) have been proposed to address the limitations of traditional graph convolutions by using attention mechanisms to weigh different neighborhoods \[[14](http://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10903)\].
