## Related Works

Brain tumor segmentation is a critical task in medical imaging, with significant implications for diagnosis, treatment planning, and prognosis. Several approaches have been proposed to address this challenge, leveraging deep learning techniques to improve segmentation accuracy and efficiency. Early methods, such as the original U-Net \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04597)\], demonstrated the potential of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for biomedical image segmentation. The U-Net architecture, characterized by its symmetric encoder-decoder structure, has been widely adopted for 2D and 3D medical image segmentation tasks \[[3](http://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04597)\]. However, the performance of U-Net-based models is often limited by the need for extensive training data and the difficulty of capturing long-range dependencies in 3D data \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6c9c65db8099038733cc0644265764c0570d4fbe)\].

To address these limitations, several studies have explored the use of 3D convolutional networks, such as the 3D U-Net \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06650)\], which extends the U-Net architecture to handle volumetric data. The 3D U-Net has been successfully applied to brain tumor segmentation, achieving competitive performance on benchmark datasets like BraTS \[[20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06650)\]. However, training 3D CNNs often requires significant computational resources and is prone to overfitting due to the high dimensionality of the input data \[[22](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/6c9c65db8099038733cc0644265764c0570d4fbe)\].

To improve the efficiency of training, researchers have investigated the use of pre-trained models and transfer learning. For instance, the V-Net \[[6](http://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04797)\] is a fully convolutional neural network designed for volumetric medical image segmentation, which uses a dice loss function to handle class imbalance and data augmentation to prevent overfitting. Similarly, the EfficientNet-based U-Net \[[19](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.11052)\] incorporates efficient convolutional blocks to reduce computational complexity while maintaining high segmentation accuracy.

Recent advances have also focused on hybrid architectures that combine the strengths of CNNs and transformers. For example, the Swin UNETR \[[4](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.01266)\] leverages the power of vision transformers to capture long-range dependencies in 3D MRI data, leading to improved segmentation performance. Similarly, the TransBTS \[[16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04430)\] model integrates 3D CNNs with transformer-based architectures to enhance the modeling of local and global features, achieving state-of-the-art results on BraTS datasets.

Another promising approach is the use of ensembles of multiple models to improve robustness and performance.
