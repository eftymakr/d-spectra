## Related Works

Traditional approaches to addressing economic inequality often rely on static tax systems, such as the U.S. federal income tax, which lack the adaptability needed to respond to dynamic economic conditions \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\]. These systems are typically based on fixed tax brackets and do not account for taxpayer heterogeneity or irrational behavior \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\]. In contrast, models like the Saez Optimal Taxation framework attempt to dynamically adjust tax rates to optimize equity and productivity \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\]. However, these models often fail to incorporate the complexity of real-world taxpayer behavior and the need for adaptive policy-making \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\].

Recent advances in artificial intelligence have introduced new methodologies for policy design and evaluation. For instance, the use of large language models (LLMs) in conjunction with agent-based modeling (ABM) has been explored to simulate complex economic systems and design adaptive tax policies \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\]. This approach is particularly relevant to the TaxAgent framework, which integrates LLMs with ABM to iteratively optimize tax rates based on real-world taxpayer behaviors \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\]. The integration of LLMs into policy-making processes has also been discussed in the context of improving reasoning capabilities and aligning model outputs with human expectations \[[1' 2023-09-05](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144v1)\]. The concept of alignment fine-tuning (AFT) has been proposed to enhance the reliability of LLMs in reasoning tasks by calibrating their responses to ensure consistency with correct outcomes \[[1' 2023-09-05](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.02144v1)\].

Agent-based models have been widely used in policy evaluation, particularly in the context of consumer behavior and market dynamics \[[4' 2022-09-12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05109v1)\]. For example, the Consumat II model has been employed to simulate consumer behavior in the EU lighting market, demonstrating how information campaigns can influence adoption of energy-efficient lighting \[[4' 2022-09-12](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.05109v1)\]. Similarly, the use of agent-based models in evaluating the impact of tax policies has been explored in the context of wealth inequality \[[3' 2025-01-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08573v1)\]. These models allow for the simulation of heterogeneous agent behaviors and the evaluation of policy outcomes under varying conditions \[[3' 2025-01-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.08573v1)\].

The integration of machine learning into policy-making has also raised concerns regarding explainability and transparency. Explainable machine learning (XAI) has been proposed as a means to ensure that ML systems used in high-stakes policy decisions are interpretable and aligned with human values \[[5' 2020-10-27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14374v3)\]. This is particularly relevant to the TaxAgent framework, which requires transparency in its policy recommendations to ensure public trust and compliance \[[6' 2023-09-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09992v2)\].
