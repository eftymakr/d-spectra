## Related Works

Fine-grained emotion classification has emerged as a critical area within sentiment analysis, particularly in the context of user-generated content such as app reviews. While traditional sentiment analysis focuses on coarse-grained labels like positive, negative, and neutral \[[11' 2020-05-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882v2)\], fine-grained emotion classification aims to capture more nuanced emotional states, such as joy, sadness, anger, and fear \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\]. This distinction is crucial for applications requiring deeper emotional understanding, such as user experience analysis and customer feedback interpretation \[[11' 2020-05-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11882v2)\].

Several studies have explored the use of large language models (LLMs) for emotion annotation, highlighting their potential to reduce manual effort while maintaining reasonable agreement with human annotators \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\]. For instance, GPT-4 has demonstrated high performance in emotion annotation tasks, outperforming previous work where human labels were the only ground truth \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\]. However, discrepancies between human and LLM annotations persist, underscoring the need for human input in annotation studies \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\]. This aligns with findings from the field of affective computing, where human annotations remain essential due to the subjective nature of emotions \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\].

The integration of LLMs into emotion annotation pipelines has also been explored through hybrid approaches. For example, the E-ICL method improves fine-grained emotion recognition by leveraging prototype theory and dynamically adjusting emotional prototypes to enhance prediction accuracy \[[2' 2024-06-04](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02642v4)\]. This approach mitigates issues such as reliance on semantically similar but emotionally inaccurate examples and interference from irrelevant categories \[[2' 2024-06-04](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02642v4)\]. Additionally, the SensoryT5 model integrates sensory information into emotion classification, enhancing the richness of emotional representations by incorporating sensory cues into the attention mechanism \[[12' 2024-03-22](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15574v1)\].

Automated emotion annotation has also been evaluated in the context of app reviews, where large language models have shown promise in reducing manual effort while maintaining substantial agreement with human annotators \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\]. However, full automation remains challenging due to the complexity of emotional interpretation \[[1' 2024-12-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07906v1)\]. This is consistent with broader findings in natural language processing, where fine-grained emotion classification remains a difficult task, especially when relying on weakly supervised datasets \[[3' 2021-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2108.07638v2)\].

The development of annotated datasets has played a key role in advancing research in fine-grained emotion classification. For instance, the Car-1000 dataset provides a large-scale benchmark for visual categorization, highlighting the importance of diverse and up-to-date datasets in training and evaluating models \[[16' 2025-03-16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2503.12385v2)\]. Similarly, the EDOS and Empathetic-Dialogues datasets have been used to evaluate emotion recognition models, demonstrating the value of annotated data in improving model performance \[[2' 2024-06-04](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02642v4)\].
