## Related Works

De novo genome assembly has been a central challenge in bioinformatics, with significant progress made in recent years. Early approaches, such as those based on de Bruijn graphs, have been widely used due to their simplicity and effectiveness in handling short reads \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\]. However, these methods suffer from high memory usage and computational complexity, especially when dealing with large genomes \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\]. To address these limitations, several studies have proposed alternative models, such as the sparse k-mer graph, which significantly reduces memory consumption while maintaining robustness \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\]. This approach replaces the traditional de Bruijn graph with a more memory-efficient structure, allowing for efficient traversal and assembly \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\].

The computational challenges of de novo assembly have also led to the development of parallel and distributed algorithms. For instance, HipMER is designed for extreme-scale analysis and leverages efficient parallelization of the Meraculous code to handle large datasets \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. Similarly, the diBELLA 2D pipeline introduces new distributed-memory parallel algorithms for overlap detection and layout simplification, achieving near-linear scaling and significant performance improvements \[[3' 2020-10-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055v1)\]. These methods demonstrate the potential of parallel computing in overcoming the limitations of traditional assemblers.

Another critical area of research has focused on improving the accuracy and completeness of genome assemblies. The introduction of the hydrostructure framework has enabled the development of safe and complete genome assembly algorithms, ensuring that the results are consistent across different assembly strategies \[[4' 2020-11-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12635v3)\]. This work builds on earlier efforts to characterize safe partial solutions, such as unitigs, and extends them to omnitigs, which are more comprehensive \[[5' 2020-02-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10498v3)\]. The linear-time algorithm for identifying maximal omnitigs represents a significant theoretical advancement, providing a foundation for more accurate and efficient assembly methods \[[5' 2020-02-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10498v3)\].

In addition to algorithmic improvements, there has been a growing interest in the use of long-read sequencing technologies, which offer higher accuracy and better contiguity in genome assemblies \[[6' 2017-03-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10231v1)\]. Tools like Minimap and miniasm have been developed to efficiently map and assemble long reads without requiring an error correction stage, significantly reducing computational time \[[11' 2015-12-06](http://arxiv.org/abs/1512.01801v2)\]. These tools are particularly useful for assembling complex genomes, such as mammalian genomes, where traditional short-read assemblers struggle \[[6' 2017-03-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10231v1)\].

The challenges of de novo assembly are further compounded by the presence of segmental duplications and repetitive regions, which can lead to misassemblies and collapsed contigs \[[12' 2018-06-30](http://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00205v2)\]. ### <a id='category-memory-efficient-assembly-approaches'></a>Memory-Efficient Assembly Approaches: 5 papers found.
Memory-efficient genome assembly approaches have been actively explored to address the computational challenges of de novo assembly, particularly in reducing memory usage and improving robustness. Several methods have been proposed to overcome the limitations of traditional de Bruijn graph-based assemblers, which often suffer from high memory consumption and sensitivity to sequencing errors. For instance, SparseAssembler1 was introduced to reduce memory usage by up to 90% through the use of sparse k-mers, although it required multiple rounds of denoising \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\]. More recently, a new general model was developed that replaces the de Bruijn graph entirely with a sparse k-mer graph, enabling similar memory efficiency and improved robustness by traversing links between k-mers based on read information \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\]. Additionally, methods such as the MPNN model have been trained to execute graph simplification algorithms, allowing for efficient processing of large-scale genomic graphs \[[8' 2020-11-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.05013v1)\]. Furthermore, the fermi assembler was developed to produce unitigs that preserve most of the input read information, enabling accurate SNP and INDEL calling without a reference genome \[[9' 2012-03-28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1203.6364v2)\]. In the context of microbial sample analysis, Squeezambler was introduced as a divide-and-conquer approach that reduces sequencing costs and computational complexity by focusing on distinct genome types rather than individual cells \[[4' 2020-11-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12635v3)\]. For RNA-seq data, Bermuda was proposed to handle uneven transcript coverage by adaptively using multiple k-mer sizes during assembly, leading to better contiguity and lower redundancy \[[5' 2020-02-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10498v3)\]. These approaches collectively highlight the ongoing efforts to improve memory efficiency and computational performance in de novo genome assembly.

[Snippet]: «Genome assembly is a fundamental problem in Bioinformatics, requiring to reconstruct a source genome from an assembly graph built from a set of reads (short strings sequenced from the genome). A notion of genome assembly solution is that of an arc-covering walk of the graph. Since assembly graphs admit many solutions, the goal is to find what is definitely present in all solutions, or what is safe. Most practical assemblers are based on heuristics having at their core unitigs, namely paths whose internal nodes have unit in-degree and out-degree, and which are clearly safe. The long-standing open problem of finding all the safe parts of the solutions was recently solved [RECOMB 2016] yielding a 60% increase in contig length. This safe and complete genome assembly algorithm was followed by other works improving the time bounds, as well as extending the results for different notions of assembly solution. But it remained open whether one can be complete also for models of genome assembly of practical applicability.
  In this paper we present a universal framework for obtaining safe and complete algorithms which unify the previous results, while also allowing for easy generalisations to assembly problems including many practical aspects. This is based on a novel graph structure, called the hydrostructure of a walk, which highlights the reachability properties of the graph from the perspective of the walk. The hydrostructure allows for simple characterisations of the existing safe walks, and of their new practical versions. Almost all of our characterisations are directly adaptable to optimal verification algorithms, and simple enumeration algorithms. Most of these algorithms are also improved to optimality using an incremental computation procedure and a previous optimal algorithm of a specific model.»

### <a id='category-long-read-sequencing-tools-and-techniques'></a>Long-Read Sequencing Tools and Techniques: 6 papers found.
The category "Long-Read Sequencing Tools and Techniques" encompasses advancements in de novo genome assembly using long-read sequencing technologies, which have significantly improved the ability to reconstruct complete and accurate genome sequences. These technologies, such as Single Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) and Oxford Nanopore (ONT), produce reads exceeding 10 kbp, enabling high-quality assemblies at a lower cost \[[3' 2020-10-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055v1)\]. However, these reads come with a relatively high error rate (10-15%), necessitating complex and computationally intensive pipelines for error correction and assembly \[[3' 2020-10-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055v1)\]. Recent tools like minimap and miniasm have been developed to address these challenges by efficiently mapping and assembling SMRT and ONT reads without an error correction stage, achieving faster assembly times and better performance compared to existing pipelines \[[3' 2020-10-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10055v1)\]. Additionally, the computational complexity of de novo assembly remains a challenge, particularly due to the NP-hard nature of the problem when framed as finding the shortest sequence containing all reads \[[5' 2020-02-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2002.10498v3)\]. Despite these challenges, long-read sequencing has enabled near-complete telomere-to-telomere assemblies, allowing for chromosome-level reconstruction of mammalian genomes \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. The feasibility of achieving such assemblies depends on factors like repeat content and heterozygosity rate, which influence the required coverage and read length for unique reconstruction \[[6' 2017-03-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1703.10231v1)\]. Furthermore, the development of specialized algorithms and hardware solutions, such as NMP-PaK, aims to address the computational and memory limitations inherent in de novo assembly by leveraging near-memory processing and custom hardware-software co-design \[[1' 2011-08-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/1108.3556v2)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the efficiency, accuracy, and scalability of long-read sequencing technologies in genome assembly.

### <a id='category-segmental-duplication-detection-methods'></a>Segmental Duplication Detection Methods: 3 papers found.
Segmental duplications (SDs) are critical in genome assembly due to their role in genomic structural variation and disease association, yet they pose significant challenges in de novo assembly by causing ambiguities in both overlap-layout-consensus and de Bruijn graphs \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. These ambiguities result in misassemblies, collapsed regions, or complete omission of SDs from reference genomes, thereby limiting our understanding of genome evolution and architecture \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. Existing tools, such as Whole Genome Assembly Comparison (WGAC), are limited by their complexity and time-consuming nature, making them impractical for widespread use \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. To address these limitations, the SEgmental Duplication Evaluation Framework (SEDEF) was introduced, offering a more efficient and accurate method for detecting SDs through advanced filtering strategies based on Jaccard similarity and local chaining \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. SEDEF not only improves detection accuracy but also significantly reduces runtime, achieving results in minutes rather than weeks \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. Additionally, SEDEF captures up to 25% pairwise error between segments, surpassing previous methods that focused on only 10%, thus enabling deeper insights into evolutionary history \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\]. These advancements highlight the ongoing efforts to improve the characterization of SDs within genome assemblies, underscoring their importance in both research and clinical applications \[[2' 2017-05-31](http://arxiv.org/abs/1705.11147v1)\].
