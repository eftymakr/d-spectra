## Related Works

Anomaly detection has become a critical component in intelligent manufacturing, where maintaining quality control on production lines is essential. Various approaches have been proposed to address the challenges of detecting anomalies in industrial settings, especially in resource-constrained embedded systems. These methods differ in their design, performance, and applicability to real-world scenarios.

Early works on anomaly detection relied on traditional statistical methods such as thresholding and clustering, which are limited in their ability to capture complex patterns in high-dimensional data . More recently, deep learning-based approaches have emerged as a promising alternative, leveraging the power of neural networks to learn intricate representations of normal behavior and detect deviations . However, most of these models are computationally intensive and not suitable for deployment on embedded devices with limited resources .

To address the computational limitations of deep learning models, researchers have proposed lightweight architectures and model compression techniques. For instance,  introduced a model compression pipeline that enables the deployment of lightweight object detectors on edge devices. Similarly,  proposed a sequence-to-sequence neural network-based approach for anomaly detection in industrial control systems, which requires only a normal dataset for training and can detect anomalies in real-time. Another approach to reducing computational overhead involves the use of pre-trained models with fine-tuning. For example, \[[Mathis Kruse' 2024-04-10](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06832v1)\] proposed AnomalousPatchCore, which fine-tunes a feature extractor using both normal and anomalous in-domain samples, leading to improved performance in industrial anomaly detection. Similarly,  introduced the Prior Normality Prompt Transformer (PNPT), which incorporates normal semantics prompting to mitigate the "identical mapping" problem in multi-class anomaly detection. These methods demonstrate that fine-tuning pre-trained models can lead to significant improvements in performance while maintaining computational efficiency.

In addition to model compression and pre-training, several studies have focused on the use of graph-based and vision foundation models for anomaly detection. For example,  proposed a graph neural network-based approach that leverages the spectral properties of graphs to detect anomalies. This method was evaluated on multiple datasets and showed promising results in detecting anomalies in complex systems. Similarly,  explored the use of vision foundation models for semantic anomaly detection, comparing local vision embeddings from runtime images to a database of nominal scenarios to identify anomalies.

While these methods have made significant strides in improving the performance and efficiency of anomaly detection, they often face challenges in terms of interpretability, robustness, and generalization to new domains. In summary, the field of anomaly detection has seen a wide range of approaches, from traditional statistical methods to deep learning-based models. While many of these methods have demonstrated promising results, there remains a need for more efficient, interpretable, and robust solutions that can be deployed in real-world industrial settings.
