## Related Works

The study of database isolation levels and their testing has gained significant attention in recent years, particularly due to the increasing reliance on weak isolation guarantees in modern databases to achieve high throughput and low latency. Several works have explored the complexity and practicality of testing these isolation levels. Early research on transaction isolation focused primarily on strong isolation levels such as serializability, with the problem of testing strong isolation being shown to be NP-complete \[[Ranadeep Biswas' 2021-03-04](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.02830v1)\]. This result established a theoretical foundation for understanding the computational limits of testing transactional consistency. However, as databases began to adopt weak isolation levels like Read Committed (RC) and Causal Consistency (CC) to improve performance, the need for efficient testing methods for these levels became apparent \[[Mohit Kumar Ahuja' 2022-02-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12139v1)\].

Recent work has shown that weak isolation levels can be tested in polynomial time, which has enabled the development of scalable testing tools \[[Hengfeng Wei' 2025-04-03](http://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02344v1)\]. However, existing testers often have high polynomial complexity, limiting their applicability to large-scale workloads \[[Anders Alnor Mathiasen' 2025-01-24](http://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14421v2)\]. This limitation is particularly problematic for production databases that handle massive transaction histories, as the testing of such systems must be both efficient and effective \[[Sheldon Stone' 1999-04-15](http://arxiv.org/abs/hep-ph/9904350v1)\].

A notable contribution in this area is the development of MonkeyDB, a mock storage system designed to test storage-backed applications under weak isolation levels. MonkeyDB supports multiple isolation levels and uses a logical specification to generate possible return values for read operations, enabling the simulation of weak behaviors \[[Myeongsoo Kim' 2022-04-18](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08348v3)\]. While MonkeyDB provides a valuable framework for testing, it does not achieve the same level of efficiency as the proposed AWDIT tester.

The work on AWDIT builds upon earlier studies that explored the complexity of testing weak isolation levels. For instance, the paper by \[[V. E. Karachentseva' 2009-08-11](http://arxiv.org/abs/0908.1512v1)\] introduced a formal model for weak isolation levels and demonstrated that testing these levels can be done in polynomial time. This work laid the groundwork for the development of more efficient testing algorithms. Additionally, \[[Jai Manral' 2015-09-28](http://arxiv.org/abs/1509.08401v1)\] explored the use of probabilistic methods to test weak isolation levels, showing that such approaches can provide good coverage of weak behaviors.

The proposed AWDIT tester improves upon these earlier works by achieving a significantly lower complexity. Specifically, AWDIT tests RC and RA in $O(n^{3/2})$ time and CC in $O(n \cdot k)$ time, where $n$ is the size of the history and $k$ is the number of sessions \[[Marcel Balsiger' 2018-03-19](http://arxiv.org/abs/1803.07045v2)\]. This improvement is crucial for handling large-scale workloads, as demonstrated by the experimental results showing an average speedup of $245\ttimes$, $193\ttimes$, and $62\ttimes$ over existing testers for RC, RA, and CC, respectively \[[A. Isabella' 2012-02-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/1202.4527v1)\].
