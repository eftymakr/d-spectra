## Related Works

Fairness in re-ranking has been a focal point in recent research, with a growing emphasis on understanding the trade-offs between ranking accuracy and fairness. Early works such as \[[Chen Xu' 2024-04-27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826v1)\] introduced fairness-aware re-ranking algorithms that balance accuracy and provider-side fairness, emphasizing the need for personalized user tolerance towards diversity. This approach iteratively generates ranking lists by trading off accuracy and provider coverage, while incorporating user preferences \[[Chen Xu' 2024-04-27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826v1)\]. Similarly, \[[Weiwen Liu' 2018-09-09](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921v2)\] proposed a method that ensures provider fairness by formulating the problem as a resource allocation task, where exposure slots are treated as resources to be distributed fairly. This work demonstrated that max-min fairness (MMF) is a better metric for provider fairness than proportion fairness (PF), as it prioritizes the utility of the worst-off providers \[[Weiwen Liu' 2018-09-09](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921v2)\].

The concept of fairness in re-ranking has also been explored from a taxation perspective, where fairness constraints are modeled as item-level tax policies. \[[Xiaopeng Ye' 2024-05-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120v2)\] introduced Tax-rank, a fair re-ranking method that levies taxes based on the utility difference between items, and optimized it using the Sinkhorn algorithm. This approach theoretically demonstrated continuity and controllability over accuracy loss, addressing limitations of previous methods \[[Xiaopeng Ye' 2024-05-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120v2)\]. In addition, \[[Mohammadmehdi Naghiaei' 2022-04-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085v1)\] proposed a framework for fairness-aware re-ranking that incorporates demographic fairness, using representations of demographic groups to ensure fairness in ranked results. This method was evaluated on a stock photo dataset and showed higher precision than baseline methods \[[Mohammadmehdi Naghiaei' 2022-04-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085v1)\].

Recent works have also explored fairness from a multi-stakeholder perspective, recognizing that recommender systems operate in a two-sided marketplace. \[[Robert Lee Poe' 2023-06-02](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01417v1)\] introduced CPFair, an optimization-based re-ranking approach that integrates fairness constraints from both consumer and producer sides. This method improved both consumer and producer fairness without reducing overall recommendation quality \[[Robert Lee Poe' 2023-06-02](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01417v1)\]. Similarly, \[[Nasim Sonboli' 2020-05-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974v1)\] proposed P-MMF, an online re-ranking model that ensures provider fairness by treating exposure slots as resources to be allocated. This model was shown to outperform state-of-the-art baselines on multiple datasets \[[Nasim Sonboli' 2020-05-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974v1)\].

The fairness-accuracy trade-off in re-ranking has also been analyzed through the lens of elasticity, drawing parallels with economic theories. \[[Elena Stefancova' 2024-09-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14078v1)\] introduced the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) as an evaluation framework, enabling comparative analysis of algorithm performance across different elasticity levels. This framework was used to evaluate ElasticRank, a fair re-ranking algorithm that adjusts inter-item distances using elasticity calculations \[[Elena Stefancova' 2024-09-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14078v1)\]. The insights from \[[Elena Stefancova' 2024-09-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14078v1)\] build on earlier work that recognized the importance of elasticity in understanding the trade-offs between fairness and accuracy in re-ranking \[[Xiaopeng Ye' 2024-05-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120v2)\].

In addition to these contributions, \[[Ankit Kulshrestha' 2021-11-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08878v3)\] explored fairness in recommendation systems by proposing a multi-target multiplicity framework that considers the impact of target choice on fairness. This work highlighted the importance of considering resource constraints when evaluating fairness in recommendation systems \[[Ankit Kulshrestha' 2021-11-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08878v3)\].

has identified a trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness. However,

tax on suppliers, ultimately translate into a cost passed on to consumers.
Analogously, item-side fairness constraints result in a decline in user-side
accuracy. In economics, the extent to which commodity tax on the supplier (item
fairness) transfers to commodity tax on users (accuracy loss) is formalized
using the notion of elasticity. The re-ranking fairness-accuracy trade-off is
similarly governed by the elasticity of utility between item groups. This
insight underscores the limitations of current fair re-ranking evaluations,
which often rely solely on a single fairness metric, hindering comprehensive
assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms. Centered around the concept of
elasticity, this work presents two significant contributions. We introduce the
Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) as an evaluation framework. This framework
enables a comparative analysis of algorithm performance across different
elasticity levels, facilitating the selection of the most suitable approach.

The field of algorithmic fairness evaluation has seen significant advancements, with researchers addressing the challenges of bias in automated decision-making systems. Prior work has highlighted the need to incorporate fairness criteria into machine learning models, as traditional approaches often fail to account for inherent biases in training data or algorithmic assumptions \[[Chen Xu' 2024-04-27](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.17826v1)\]. Several studies have explored the trade-offs between accuracy and fairness, emphasizing that fairness requirements can be formulated as constrained optimization problems, where utility maximization is balanced against fairness constraints \[[Weiwen Liu' 2018-09-09](http://arxiv.org/abs/1809.02921v2)\]. For instance, some research has shown that group-specific threshold rules can be optimal for achieving fairness under certain criteria, such as positive predictive value (PPV) parity or false omission rate (FOR) parity, although this may lead to counter-intuitive outcomes, such as selecting individuals with lower utility for specific groups \[[Xiaopeng Ye' 2024-05-25](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16120v2)\]. Additionally, the use of random classifier ensembles has been proposed as a way to enhance fairness, as ensembles of fair classifiers are guaranteed to be fair, and ensembles of unfair classifiers can still achieve fair outcomes \[[Mohammadmehdi Naghiaei' 2022-04-17](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.08085v1)\]. Moreover, fairness in high-stakes domains, such as face recognition, has been evaluated through robustness metrics, with studies revealing subgroup disparities that are not apparent in aggregate performance measures \[[Robert Lee Poe' 2023-06-02](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01417v1)\]. These findings underscore the importance of developing comprehensive fairness evaluation frameworks that go beyond single metrics, such as the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) introduced in this work \[[Nasim Sonboli' 2020-05-21](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12974v1)\].
