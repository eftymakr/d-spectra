## Related Works

The field of large language model (LLM) alignment has seen significant advancements, particularly in the areas of steering model behavior and identifying refusal patterns. Prior work has primarily focused on detecting refusal behaviors through predefined templates or manual analysis of output tokens \[[Nguyen Ngoc-Hieu' 2023-06-23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14920v1)\]. These approaches, while effective in certain scenarios, are limited by their reliance on explicit signals and lack generalizability across different alignment conditions. For instance, methods such as those described in \[[Hiroaki Yamagiwa' 2024-06-16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10984v3)\] often require specific refusal tokens to be present in the output, which may not always be the case in adversarial or weakly aligned models. Similarly, \[[Federico Tessari' 2024-07-11](http://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08623v4)\] explores the use of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align models with user intent, but this approach is constrained by the need for extensive human annotations and may not scale effectively to large-scale deployment.

Recent efforts have also aimed to improve the efficiency and robustness of alignment techniques. For example, \[[Harald Steck' 2024-03-08](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440v1)\] introduces a method for automated alignment that leverages the model's internal representations to guide steering without explicit output-based signals. This approach aligns with the core idea of COSMIC, which utilizes cosine similarity metrics to identify steering directions independently of model outputs. However, unlike COSMIC, \[[Harald Steck' 2024-03-08](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440v1)\] still requires assumptions about the model's refusal behavior, such as the presence of specific refusal tokens. Cosine similarity has been extensively explored for alignment tasks, with various applications in detecting out-of-distribution data, improving semantic similarity measurements, and enhancing neural network normalization. For instance, cosine similarity between test features and typical in-distribution features has been shown to effectively detect out-of-distribution data, leading to the development of Class Typical Matching (CTM) \[[Nguyen Ngoc-Hieu' 2023-06-23](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14920v1)\]. Additionally, cosine similarity is used to measure semantic similarity between embeddings, with recent work proposing novel interpretations based on Independent Component Analysis (ICA), where cosine similarity is viewed as the sum of semantic similarities over interpretable axes \[[Hiroaki Yamagiwa' 2024-06-16](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10984v3)\]. The use of cosine similarity in neural networks has also been studied, where it is proposed as an alternative to dot product to reduce variance and improve generalization, leading to the concept of cosine normalization \[[Harald Steck' 2024-03-08](http://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05440v1)\]. Furthermore, cosine similarity has been extended to handle multi-modal and multi-vector alignment tasks, such as the Joint Generalized Cosine Similarity (JGCS), which enables alignment across an arbitrary number of vectors, offering advantages in contrastive learning \[[Chunjie Luo' 2017-02-20](http://arxiv.org/abs/1702.05870v5)\]. In the context of word embeddings, extended cosine similarity measures have been introduced to improve performance on word similarity tasks, particularly when words share similar contexts \[[Erich Schubert' 2021-07-08](http://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04071v1)\]. These studies collectively highlight the versatility and effectiveness of cosine similarity in alignment tasks, ranging from model normalization to multi-modal learning.
